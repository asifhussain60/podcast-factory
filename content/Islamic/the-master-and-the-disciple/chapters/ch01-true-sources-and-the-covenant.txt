# The True Sources and the Covenant

## Where this episode opens

This first episode opens the book by establishing the conditions under which knowledge of the true religion can be sought, given, and made effective. It does this not through a treatise but through a story: a Persian scholar who has been awakened by a master, who has then traveled across Arab and non-Arab lands in search of a worthy disciple, and who finally enters a strange city, listens to a circle of townspeople debating religion, and identifies one young man whose heart has been awakened by reflection. The episode follows that encounter from first contact through the oath that binds the two together. Along the way it lays out the three-part scaffolding the entire book will return to — gratitude, comportment, and the covenant — and stakes a claim that the listener should hold steady from the opening minute: religion is not a body of beliefs to be sampled; it is a graduated discipline received under allegiance to the Father of Imams, and any act of piety performed outside that allegiance, however correct in form, is structurally unrecoverable.

## The Three Thanks and the Search for a Worthy Disciple

The book opens in a curious way. Before anyone is introduced, before the story properly begins, a group of believers and a circle of those who call others to the true religion approach one of their scholars with a question. They tell him: you have liberated us. You have helped us learn about an affair of the highest importance, and now we feel obligated to express our gratitude in three directions. We owe you thanks for having called us to the true religion in the first place. We owe thanks for the knowledge you pointed us toward. And we owe thanks for the religious practices you commanded us to perform. So teach us, they say, how those three thanks should actually be expressed. And while you are at it, inform us about the rights and duties that the ordinances of religion place upon us, about the proper etiquette of a seeker in the way he poses his questions, about the corresponding obligations upon a scholar in the way he answers, and about the ways of the righteous and the comportment of the seekers.

That framing tells you what kind of book this is going to be. Before any doctrine is delivered, two concepts are placed at the very entrance: shukr, gratitude, and adab, comportment. Comportment is the way you carry yourself in relation to someone or something. It is not merely good manners. In the technical sense the book uses, adab is the disposition that prioritizes someone else over the self. It is the force that opposes desire. It is what structures every act of worship as a form of self-surrender. The author wants this fixed in the listener's mind from the start, because the rest of the book is going to ask the disciple to surrender quite a lot, and none of that surrender is intelligible without first understanding that we are designed for service. We are servants created for the task of servitude, and servitude — far from being a deprivation — is what makes life enjoyable as an experience. Servitude to Allah, the author will say, is the greatest of all delights.

The Master then gives his reply to the gratitude question, and his reply is itself a small cosmology. The religion, he says, is something Allah has bestowed upon his servants as an honor — a religion he has perfected, and through which he honors whoever responds to him. Then comes a sentence that sets the architectural pattern for the entire book. For every beginning, the Master says, Allah has placed within it an end. For every end he designed a goal. And for each goal he set a limit whose full extent cannot be attained. These are the way-stations of the people of true understanding, and the key to traversing them is the constant remembrance of Allah. The point is not abstract metaphysics. The point is that the religion has been structured as a graded path, and the path has a beginning, a middle, and an end. The initial stage is trial. The middle stage is guidance. The final stage is piety.

Trial comes first because the very customized soul each of us carries rebels. It gets in the way like a wild horse refusing to be tamed, pushing us toward what we want rather than what Allah wants. Once that wild horse has been tamed, the second stage opens — guidance — and the seeker begins to experience the benefits of being on the right road. The third and final stage is piety, which the book describes as a constant, deep-rooted awareness of Allah's presence in your life. The tool that carries a person across all three stages is the remembrance of Allah. That formula — trial, then guidance, then piety, sustained by remembrance — is the spiritual map the entire book operates on.

It is worth dwelling for a moment on what makes this map distinctive. Many spiritual traditions describe stages of progress, and many describe a final state of nearness to the divine. What the book is doing here is something more pointed. It is naming trial not as an early obstacle to be overcome and forgotten, but as a structural condition of the soul — the customized soul each person is born with — that has to be tamed in order for guidance to register at all. Guidance, on this picture, is not new information delivered to a neutral mind. It is what becomes possible only once the soul has been broken to harness. And piety, in turn, is not the careful keeping of rules but the running awareness of presence that follows once the soul stops fighting the bridle. The point of the sequence is that no stage can be skipped. A person who has not undergone trial cannot recognize guidance when it arrives. A person who has been guided but does not stabilize in remembrance does not reach piety. Each stage is the precondition for the next, which is why the book will, in later chapters, refuse the shortcut every time someone tries to take it.

The Master then answers the gratitude question directly, and his answer recasts the meaning of thanks. Gratitude to the scholar, he says, is not a verbal thank-you. It is obedience. Gratitude for the knowledge you have received is turning that knowledge into action and calling others to it. And gratitude for the right practice is striving patiently in it and being consistent with it. The image the book offers is the doctor and the patient. The spiritual guide is like a doctor who prescribes a medication for an illness. Praising the doctor will not cure you. What cures you is doing what he prescribes, consistently, for the prescribed duration. The three thanks form a recurring three-part template the book will return to again and again: the call answered by obedience to the teacher, the knowledge answered by action, the action answered by perseverance.

With that template established, the author shifts into narrative. A man from Persia, we are told, had a rich heart, a brilliant intellect, and a decent education, but he was subject to the trials of spiritual ignorance — his ignorance, the author says, outweighing his knowledge. He was restless in the burning heat of his thirst for knowledge. He chased mirages, assuming the shimmer of distant water to be real, until he reached the mirage only to find nothing there. And precisely at that point of finding nothing, he found Allah, and paid him his account in full. Allah then honored his abode and removed the veil from his intellect through a teacher who found him astray and guided him, who found him in need and satisfied him with knowledge. By the time this teacher was done, the Persian had become one of the great scholars of Sinai, an angelic dweller in what the book calls the well-populated temple.

When his guidance was complete, the new scholar felt duty-bound to show gratitude to his master and to exert himself for his Lord. There was a proverb his spiritual father used to repeat that had taken on, in him, the rank of soul to body: the most excellent of deeds is giving life to the dead — meaning imparting knowledge to the ignorant, because the ignorant is like a corpse. And so the scholar said to himself: I used to be dead, and my master gave me life. I was ignorant, and he gave me knowledge. I am not the first to be ignorant, nor will I be the last. Out of gratitude I must pass on this divine trust to those who come after me, just as those before me handed it down to me. The origin of this affair is from Allah, and it reached me only through its many intermediaries, the first passing it on to the second, the second to the third, until it descended from the heavenly host to the creatures of this lower world. Did all of this knowledge, the scholar asked himself, rip through the veil and pass through intermediary after intermediary only so that I could be its final goal and ultimate end? No. Those who transmitted it before me are more deserving of precedence. In fact I myself am part of what they earned by teaching me. And their precedence does not relieve me from having to act, because my final duty is similar to my initial duty.

So he traveled toward his Lord, leaving his people and his possessions behind. He passed among Arab and non-Arab tribes, scrutinizing their faces for signs of spiritual aptitude, posing religious questions, finding no response and meeting no real seeker, until he reached the furthest part of the peninsula. The teaching embedded here is one about discernment. The scholar did not share his knowledge with just anyone. He searched for the one who deserved it, who had a burning desire, the right attitude, the aptitude, and the capacity to bear the weight of what he was carrying. The book is explicit on the cost of getting this wrong: knowledge given to the wrong person is not merely wasted; it can have an adverse effect.

Eventually he entered a city where nobody knew him. There he came upon a group of townspeople discussing religion without any guidance, recklessly following the inclinations of their passions. He sat nearby, out of sight, and listened. He analyzed which of them was closer to the right path. Then he said to himself: these people are more deserving of life — meaning spiritual knowledge — for three reasons. First, the true divine argument has reached them at all. Second, they belong to the people of his community. Third, they are closer to the right path simply by the fact that they are inquiring about religion at all. The person who is already searching for something, the Master observes, is much closer to finding it.

When the townspeople finished, they turned to the stranger and asked who he was. He had waited patiently — he had not interrupted them to correct them, which is itself a small lesson in the comportment a scholar owes to his audience. He told them he was a servant of Allah and one of the residents of his sanctuary. He said his business — the acquisition of knowledge — was complete, and that he was now looking for work, the spreading of that acquired knowledge. They tested him with a question about commendable and reprehensible speech. Commendable speech, he said, is speech that is accurate — speech whose origin comes from Allah and through which people are called toward Allah. Reprehensible speech is speech that originates in the passions of the soul, used to call people to something other than guidance. He added a quiet disclaimer that the book will keep extending: I have no words of my own, for I am following the words of another. Recognizing the truth in his answers, the townspeople asked him to become a gateway for them, through which Allah might send his loving kindness.

## The Sermon — The Design of Creation

The scholar stood, turned to the attentive group, and began his glorification of Allah. What follows is one of the central sermons of the book — a single sustained address that lays out, in compressed form, the entire metaphysics on which the rest of the doctrine will be built. The translation is rendered whole because the book treats it as whole.

Indeed! He created with his power, then arranged everything perfectly. He determined a path through his generosity, then guided his creation toward it. He brought forth the paths of the pastureland, then made its outer form a dried-up brush and its inner aspect broad in scope and content. His knowledge does not exist outwardly so that the unworthy could possess and alter it. Instead his knowledge is benevolent and well-protected because of its excellence; well concealed, its secrets guarded for those worthy of them. Its dignity is the splendid robe of guidance, and its perfection is the garment of piety. It is marked out by the light of lights, entrusted safely in the hearts of the best and the chosen ones — the truly pious, the guardians, the protectors, the generous and the recorders. This knowledge is a trust which they safely pass on, and they do not transgress the divine ordinances. He honored his knowledge by hiding it and making it inward, protected it by keeping it secret, veiled it with his obligations, and crowned it with his covenants.

For it cannot be reached by the aspirations of those who rely on the strength of their intellects, nor attained by those scheming for it. Its outer aspect is a reminder and a sign for the rightly guided, while its inner aspect is a sweet drink gushing forth. His lofty heavens come down close. His earth is paved for those who inquire about him. His guiding stars are organized for those who seek him. And he has signs whose wonders follow each other in succession, pointing to him through their symbols. His accustomed way is preferment — preferring the righteous over the unrighteous. His method is approached through distinguished ranks. He cannot be accessed without his intermediaries; this is why we find the concept of the linking-means in every part of the religion. He has made his ways a guide and a path leading to good, with its outer aspect a protective shell and a sign pointing toward its inner dimensions.

The young man and the listening crowd are silent. The scholar then closes with an exhortation: be mindful of Allah like the one who fears him, and fear him like the one who is hoping to please him. Seek knowledge and you will learn. Act according to that knowledge and you will receive Allah's loving mercy. The book says his eyes overflowed with tears at this point, that he broke off, and that he bid the group farewell. The intensity of his speech had overcome everyone present.

It is worth pausing on what this sermon is actually claiming. It claims that creation has been built on a deliberate two-layer design. Allah took all the spiritual realities and hid them inside the shell of the exterior, so that everything we see in the visible world — the sky, the earth, the sun, the moon, the stars, the changing seasons, the winds, the oceans, the rivers — represents inner spiritual realities. He veiled the spiritual realities within the physical creations, thereby immortalizing them. People come and go. Generations come and go. Entire nations come and go. But the external and the internal realities, paired together as outer shell and inner meaning, live forever. The outer-and-inner distinction is therefore not merely a textual technique or a hermeneutical option. It is a deliberate cosmic design principle, a mechanism of preservation. The sermon's phrase is precise on this: by veiling the spiritual realities within physical creations, Allah was immortalizing them.

The second move of the sermon is just as important. Having claimed this two-layer design, the sermon insists that the inner layer is not freely accessible. It cannot be reached by the aspirations of those who rely on the strength of their intellects, nor attained by those scheming for it. The book is naming, very early on, a recognizable opponent — the figure who tries to seize the inner meaning by intellectual power alone, or by political maneuvering, without standing in the proper relation to the trustees who carry it. The reply is that the inner aspect is given through intermediaries. Allah's method, the sermon says, is approached through distinguished ranks. He cannot be accessed without his intermediaries. The chosen ones — the trustees of his knowledge — are the ones to unveil these realities. None of us can decipher the codes without them.

The vocabulary the sermon uses for those intermediaries is also worth pausing on. The Master speaks of preferment as Allah's accustomed way — the active sorting by which the righteous are placed above the unrighteous in the order of access. He speaks of distinguished ranks — the graded arrangement through which knowledge descends, level by level, rather than being dumped indiscriminately into the world. And he speaks of the linking-means, the bridge concept by which every level connects to the level above and below it. The picture is not a flat democracy of access but a ladder. The rungs are real. Skipping rungs is not heroic; it is impossible. The whole architecture is held together by the fact that the highest rung is occupied by trustees Allah himself has chosen and equipped, and that those trustees are the only authorized doorway to the inner content. The sermon's list of those trustees — the truly pious, the guardians, the protectors, the generous, and the recorders — is a compressed catalogue of the offices the rest of the book will unfold in detail. For the purposes of this opening, what matters is the principle: the inner knowledge has a custody chain, and the custody chain is not optional.

The third move closes the loop on creation and need. In Allah's perfect order, he created our spiritual sustenance first — identifying his chosen people and giving them knowledge of everything — and only then created us with a need for what they carry. He gave us an intellect designed to seek things out, and he created us in ignorance so that we would recognize our need. When our ignorance forces our intellects toward acquiring knowledge, the fountainheads of that knowledge are already in place. Blessed is the One, the sermon concludes, who placed the night and the day in succession for those who reflect and those who wish to be grateful. May Allah's blessings be on the one he has specially chosen from his servants and made a warner for all the worlds.

When the scholar broke off and walked away, a young man from the group followed him — the youngest in the gathering, yet of the best character, his intellect complete and his heart awakened by reflection. The book is careful here to make a point about the disciple's situation that will matter for everything that comes next. This young man's father is a great Sheikh among the most distinguished of the Arabs by lineage, and one of the most devoted supporters of a rival community that had rejected the Imam. The disciple, in other words, is not arriving from a neutral position. He is the son of a sectarian leader who has explicitly rejected the source of true knowledge, and any premature disclosure of his relationship to this new master could destroy everything. With the scholar's permission, he accompanied the master to his inn. They ate. And then they began to talk.

## The Etiquette of Learning and the Covenant of Allegiance

The young man opened, and the way he opened is itself a small course in comportment. O wise man, he said. You spoke and we listened. You preached with perfect eloquence. You adorned religion with the most beautiful ornaments, motivated us with the most beautiful incentives, called us through the most eminent responsibilities, and forced our intellects to inquire about all that has struck our hearts. You are like a tree: its roots firm, its splendor towering over the worlds, its fruits hanging low for those who desire them. So I ask you to become one of those blessed branches for me. Only after this preface did he come to his question: you said that our minds are forced by their ignorance to seek knowledge. My mind is the most needy of all. Is there any way to life for me? Notice what the preface is and is not. It is not flattery. Every quality the disciple names was actually present in what the scholar had just delivered. Comportment, the book is showing the listener, is naming truthfully what is in front of you, and only then asking for what you need.

The scholar's answer reframes the seeker himself. The One who created by his power, the scholar says, did not create without purpose, nor did he create his creatures fully knowledgeable, nor did he leave them wandering in ignorance. He established a way out for them — an instrument — and around that instrument he shaped their constitution. He made their hearing, their sight, and their hearts the openings through which the light of his knowledge enters. And by those same instruments he completed his argument upon them. The triad of hearing, sight, and heart is not arbitrary. It is the recurring vocabulary in which the Quran describes the human equipment for receiving guidance. In one chapter the Quran says: Say, it is He who has created you and endowed you with hearing, sight, and hearts. Very few of you are grateful for these gifts — chapter 67, verse 23. In another: those are the ones upon whose hearts, hearing, and sight Allah has set a seal, and they are the heedless — chapter 16, verse 108. In another: Allah has set a seal on their hearts and on their hearings, and on their eyes there is a covering, and theirs will be a great torment — chapter 2, verse 7. And in another: indeed the hearing, the sight, and the heart — of each of those you will be questioned — chapter 17, verse 36. The triad is, in other words, the apparatus by which a person becomes responsible. Hearing, sight, and heart are the means through which the light of guidance enters, and the means through which the argument against the heedless is sealed.

Having established the equipment, the scholar names the second half of the design. He placed the treasures of his wisdom and the specific characteristics of his will in the pure, chosen, and elite of his creation. He gave them the responsibility of worshiping him and calling others, while imposing upon people the duty of responding to that call by using their instruments — so that he may distinguish the wicked from the good. The architecture is symmetrical. Provision on one side, hearing-and-sight-and-heart on the other, intellect bridging them. There is, the scholar concludes, no argument against Allah after the sending of his messengers, nor any excuse for straying after the right path has been clearly pointed out.

The young man begged to become his servant. The scholar's reply is a small parable. Allah did not create his creatures as fully grown adults or as scholars. He created them as infants — small and ignorant — so that they grow big after having been small, and come to know after having been ignorant. Their smallness is a precondition for their greatness. Their ignorance is a sign pointing toward knowledge. The same is true of spiritual knowledge. One can only attain its inner essence on the basis of preceding, lower forms of knowledge that the heart can quickly assimilate. That is Allah's accustomed way regarding his servants. The point, when expanded, is direct. Just as you cannot feed a rich meat dish to a newborn — all the newborn can do is suck milk — most of us are spiritual infants who must first learn the story of the prophets, then the obligations of the visible religion, then the interpretive science, before reaching the high realities. A note in passing here that runs through the rest of the book: when the Master uses the word sunna, he means it in this older, broader sense — Allah's accustomed way, the recurring divine pattern embedded in creation. It allows him to speak of the sunna of pairs in cosmology and the sunna of sending messengers as one and the same divine habit.

The young man accepted his condition. I am an infant in relation to what I wish to achieve, he said, so raise and educate me. The scholar warned him that compassion alone would not bring him closer without his own working for it. Just as every word has a proper employment that can only be accepted if it is sincere and true, knowledge is of no benefit until it has been put into action, and action is of no benefit until it has been accepted by Allah. The only thing Allah accepts is sincerity. The scholar then gave a small handbook for how to acquire knowledge. Do not seek knowledge impatiently. Do not dismiss it out of negligence. Do not accept it out of blind imitation, without a convincing argument, because if you do it will slip out of your heart at the first onset of doubt. But once an argument has been firmly established, do not deny it, or you will become the pleader for all that is false. And do not stop inquiring about the knowledge you have attained, or your judgment will become sterile. Trust me without prejudice, the scholar told him. Make every effort to understand without confusion. Seek the path as you seek the truth. And constantly ask Allah's help in your endeavor.

When the disciple finally asked about the revealed paths of righteous acts, the scholar imposed five conditions on him before answering. Do not neglect anything entrusted to you, nor take anything given to you lightly. Do not conceal anything when questioned. Do not come looking for me whenever you have a question. Do not ask about anything until I myself bring it up. And — most heavily — do not mention me to your father. The condition about the father is the political stake of the entire story. The young man's father is a known leader, a strong supporter of a community that has rejected the Imam, and any premature disclosure of the disciple's relationship to this new master would destroy the mission before it has begun. The scholar instructed him not to move away from his father — moving away would itself invite suspicion — but to stay with him, respect him, please him where possible, while protecting his innermost beliefs and keeping his spiritual concerns secret. Indeed Allah, the scholar said, will protect you from his, and other people's, hostility.

The two met only occasionally over a long period of time. The young man kept performing everything assigned to him from the visible religion, doing so without yet receiving the inner knowledge behind those actions, not even knowing where the scholar lived. This was his period of trial — the first of the three stages the book opened with. When his merit and his perseverance had become clear, a meeting was finally arranged. The young man prostrated himself in gratitude, and the scholar gently corrected him: gratitude offered before the deed gives rise to suspicion of insincerity. Then, at last, he prepared to teach.

There is a key to religion, the scholar began, which renders it either sacred or profane, just like the difference between marriage and fornication. Both are the same physical act. What differentiates the lawful from the forbidden is the formality of the marriage contract — blessing it with Allah's name. It is the same animal we eat, but the prescribed slaughter, blessing it with Allah's name, is what makes the meat lawful. That key, the scholar said, is the pact of Allah, which confirms his rights, encompasses his obligations, and safeguards his friends. It is the rope of Allah upon his earth and his guarantee of security among his creation.

The scholar then took the noble oath of allegiance from the young man, for without it all the inner knowledge of interpretation would have been forbidden to him. This is the mechanism the book calls the mastership-bond. The true knowledge belongs to the Imam. Accepting his mastership-bond is like paying the owner his due. Using that knowledge without the mastership-bond is theft. And stolen food remains forbidden regardless of how lawful the animal was. The book illustrates this with a parable told about an Imam of the prophet's household. A man came to him having stolen five apples and given them as charity, calculating that fifty rewards minus five sins left him forty-five net rewards in the ledger. The Imam corrected him. When you stole those apples, you earned five sins. When you gave them to the old woman, you earned another five sins — because you had propagated what was forbidden to another person. Total: ten sins, zero rewards. This is the structural reason acts of piety performed without the mastership-bond are ineffective. The act without its covenant is stolen, and what is stolen cannot be sanctified.

This is a hard claim and the book wants the listener to feel its hardness. The argument is not that piety without allegiance is merely less valuable, or that it earns a smaller reward. The argument is that piety without allegiance is structurally a different kind of act. The same physical motion — fasting, praying, giving — is, depending on whether the covenant is in place, either an instance of returning what belongs to the owner or an instance of taking from the owner without permission. The apple parable is meant to dismantle the accounting intuition that almost everyone brings to religious life: the intuition that good acts and bad acts sit on opposite sides of a scale and that the net difference is what counts. The Imam in the parable is not adjusting the ledger; he is rejecting the ledger model entirely. There is no positive column on stolen property. The only way to make the act count is to put the act inside the right relationship to its source. That relationship is what the mastership-bond does.

This is also why the book's title is itself a teaching. The visible religion is a graduated discipline. Ritual purity and the daily prayer are required five times a day. Fasting is required one month a year. Almsgiving is required once a year. Pilgrimage is required once in a lifetime. And the mastership-bond is required every breath. The visible religion prepares us for knowledge. It builds discipline within us. That is, in fact, where the word disciple comes from — disciple from discipline, and discipline from the visible religion. The disciple of this book becomes the disciple precisely by submitting to that graduated discipline under the covenant.

So the scholar recited the oath. The young man repeated it and bound himself to it, his eyes streaming with tears, until he knew with certainty that from that moment on he was a member of the party of Allah and the party of Allah's friends.

## Closing

That oath is where the book's prologue closes and its substantive teaching begins. The structure the listener should carry forward from this episode has three pieces. First, the religion is a graded path — trial, then guidance, then piety — carried by remembrance. Second, creation has been built as a two-layer system in which the inner meaning is preserved by being veiled inside the outer form, and the inner meaning is only accessible through intermediaries Allah has appointed. Third, the entire scaffolding of acts is sanctified or annulled by a single covenant — allegiance to the Father of Imams — without which even the most beautifully performed act becomes structurally stolen. Everything the master is about to teach the disciple in the chapters that follow will be a working-out of those three pieces.

The episode also gives the listener a working portrait of what a disciple actually looks like at the moment of beginning. He is not a clean slate. He is the son of an opponent. He has been raised by a father whose loyalties run in the other direction, and he will have to hold his new commitments under a discipline of secrecy that protects both his teacher and his mission. He is not given the inner content right away; he is asked, instead, to perform the visible practice for an extended period of trial during which no explanation is offered. Only after the period of trial does the meeting take place at which the teaching can finally begin. The structure is exact. Performance precedes understanding. Discipline precedes interpretation. Allegiance precedes content. The book has arranged its prologue so that the listener, like the disciple, cannot reach the chapters of doctrine that follow without first having walked through the same gate.
