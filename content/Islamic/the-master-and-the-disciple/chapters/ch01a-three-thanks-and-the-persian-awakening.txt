# Three Thanks and the Persian Awakening

*Three Thanks and the Persian Awakening, the opening of a tenth-century dialogue by a scholar of the Ismaili tradition. The book is the timeless story of a seeker's quest for true knowledge and the process through which he gradually achieves it. It opens by asking a single, load-bearing question: when a person receives a call to true religion, a body of knowledge, and a set of practices, how does that person actually thank the one who gave them? The Master answers with a doctrine of gratitude that turns thanks into deeds, then names a three-stage spiritual path of trial, guidance, and piety. A Persian seeker then enters the story, a man whose thirst for knowledge led him through mirage after mirage, until he found God at the very point where the mirages dissolved into nothing, and how that finding placed him under a debt that could be repaid only by setting out, in his turn, to find a disciple of his own.*

## Where the dialogue opens

It has reached us that a number of the truly faithful, and a group among those who call people to the true religion, said to one of their scholars: "You have liberated us by helping us learn about an affair of great importance, and for that we feel obligated to express our gratitude for three reasons. Our gratitude for your having called us to the true religion. Our gratitude for the knowledge you pointed us toward. And our gratitude for the religious practices you commanded us to perform.

"So inform us how we should express our gratitude for these three blessings. Inform us about the rights and duties that are obligatory upon us as directed by the ordinances of religion. Tell us about the obligations of a seeker in his mode of questioning — the proper etiquette for seeking knowledge. Tell us about the obligations upon the scholar in his response — how one should impart his knowledge to the one who seeks it. And also inform us about the ways of the righteous and the proper behavior of the seekers."

Hear the way that opening request is shaped. The seekers do not ask for content first. They ask for the etiquette of the asking. They name the three gifts already received — the call, the knowledge, the practice — and present each one as an outstanding debt of thanks. They acknowledge that the asking itself has rules. They want to know how a seeker is supposed to question and how a scholar is supposed to answer, before they hear the answer to anything.

The premise that gratitude is itself a duty under divine law is older than the dialogue. The Quran sets the principle plainly in the chapter on Abraham, verse seven: *"If you are grateful, I will surely increase you in favor; but if you deny, indeed, My punishment is severe"* (Quran 14:7, Sahih International rendering). And again in the chapter of the sage and his counsel, verse twelve: *"Whoever is grateful is grateful for the good of his own soul"* (Quran 31:12, Yusuf Ali rendering). Gratitude is not pictured as a courtesy owed to a benefactor; it is pictured as a transaction by which the grateful one is himself enlarged.

## Comportment, gratitude, and the three thanks

At the very outset the author highlights two extremely important concepts: gratitude and comportment. Comportment is the way you carry yourself in relation to someone or something. It is not merely good manners. It is the force that opposes desire — the disposition that structures every act of worship as a form of self-surrender. The force opposing gratitude and comportment is desire itself. This is why this tradition is so focused on preferring others over the self: because preferring the other draws a person out of the traps of egocentricity. We are designed as servants for the task of service. Servitude is what makes life enjoyable as an experience, and servitude to God is the greatest of all delights.

Comportment in its essence is prioritizing someone else over the self. When one considers even the comportment of eating, the whole process is designed to respect and prioritize that sustenance over the self, to humble the self in relation to the blessing of food. The Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) said: *"Sit before your food like a servant sits before his master"* (reported in al-Hindi, *Kanz al-Ummal*, hadith 41723). Honor the food by covering your head, by using your right hand — because without that food we would be reduced to animals.

Hold that picture. The seekers in the story are not at a table. They are at the door of a scholar. But the comportment is the same comportment. The food has been given. The thanks owed for it must look like a deed.

The Master replied: "Now the affair to which I invite you is the religion that God has bestowed upon his servants as an honor — a religion he has perfected for them and through which he honors whoever responds to Him. So for every beginning he has placed within it an end, and for every end he designed a goal, and each goal has a limit whose full extent cannot be attained. These are the way-stations of the people of true understanding. The key to success is the constant remembrance of God."

Notice the move. The Master answers the question about gratitude not with three thanks but with a metaphysics of stages. Every beginning has been given an end. Every end has been given a goal. Every goal has a limit whose full extent cannot be attained. The seeker who wants to thank for three gifts is being told, first, that the gifts themselves are arranged inside a divine order of stages, each stage opening onto the next, the whole sequence aimed at a limit that the seeker can travel toward but never exhaust. Gratitude has to be calibrated against the shape of the gift, and the shape of the gift turns out to be a road.

The Master continued: "Their initial level is trial; their middle state is right guidance; and their final stage is piety." This is the three-stage spiritual path of the book: trial, then guidance, then piety. The initial stage is trial, because the unrefined self rebels. The self gets in the way like a wild horse refusing to be tamed, pushing the seeker toward what the self wants rather than what God wants. The Father of Imams names that resistance precisely in his collected sayings: *"The most disabling of ignorance is a man's ignorance of his own soul"* (Nahj al-Balagha, Saying 113, Reza translation). Once the seeker succeeds in taming this wild horse, the next stage is guidance: he begins to find direction and to experience its benefits. The final stage is piety: a constant, deep-rooted awareness of the presence of God in everything the seeker does. The tool that helps the seeker persevere through these three stages is the remembrance of God, repeated until it becomes a second pulse.

Only after he has placed the gifts inside this three-stage path does the Master answer the original question. "Whoever seeks to learn the basics of God's affairs with the truest of intentions, and then discovers the divinely determined ranks of religion, has sought guidance from the correct sources and has set upon it in the best possible manner. As for showing gratitude to the scholar, that is through obeying him — not a verbal thank-you, but obedience. As for gratitude for the knowledge, it is turning it into action and calling others to it. And the gratitude for the right practice is striving patiently in it, and being consistent with it."

The spiritual guide is like a doctor who prescribes a medication for an illness. Praising the doctor will not cure you. What cures you is doing what he prescribes, consistently, for the prescribed duration — taking the dose at the right times, not stopping when you start to feel better, not skipping the bitter ones. The Master answers the seekers' three thanks with three deeds because in his settled doctrine the only honest gratitude is a deed, and the only honest deed is the deed the giver of the gift named. These three thanks form the book's recurring three-part template: the call answered by obedience to the teacher, the knowledge answered by action, the action answered by perseverance. Every time the disciple later falls short, it will be against one of these three. The Father of Imams gives the same equation a single line in the book *The Brilliant Aphorisms*: *"Knowledge without action is a tree without fruit"* (al-Amidi, *Ghurar al-Hikam*, aphorism 6249).

## The Persian scholar and his thirst

The author then narrates the story of a man from Persia. He had a rich heart, a brilliant intellect, and a decent education. Yet he was subject to the trials of spiritual ignorance, and his ignorance outweighed his knowledge. He was restless in the burning heat of his thirst for knowledge, assuming the glimmering of mirages to be the reflection of water, until he reached it only to find it nothing — yet there he found God, and paid Him his account in full.

Stay with that image for a moment. A man crosses a desert. He sees water on the horizon. He runs to it. There is no water. He runs to the next shimmer. Again nothing. And again, and again. At some point the seeker arrives at the very point where the mirage has resolved into the nothing it always was — and it is *at that exact point of nothing*, at the bottom of the disappointment, that he meets God and pays his account in full.

The motif is older than this dialogue. The Quran uses precisely the same picture in the chapter of the Light, verse thirty-nine: *"And as for those who disbelieve, their deeds are like a mirage in a desert. The thirsty one thinks it is water, until he comes to it and finds it nothing, but finds God there, and He pays him his account in full"* (Quran 24:39, Pickthall rendering). The author has read his story onto a Quranic template — the modern scholar Paul E. Walker reads the move as deliberate: the Persian's biography is being framed by revelation, so that what looks like a personal anecdote is also a paraphrase of scripture (Walker, *Early Philosophical Shiism: The Ismaili Neoplatonism of Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani*, Cambridge University Press, 1993, p. 24).

Then God honored his abode and removed the veil from his intellect through a teacher who found him astray and guided him; who found him in need and satisfied him with knowledge. Thus he became one of the great scholars of Sinai, an angelic dweller in the well-populated heavenly temple. The Persian seeker, restless in the burning heat, becomes through a teacher one of the people of Sinai. Notice the geography of the metamorphosis. He starts in Persia, walks through mirages, and arrives at the place where prophecy itself was once received. The dialogue does not announce that. It is telling the story of how a person travels from ignorance to knowledge by being found. Henry Corbin, the historian of this tradition, calls this movement "a geography of the soul, not of the map" — Persia is the place of mirage, Sinai is the place where the veil lifts (Corbin, *Cyclical Time and Ismaili Gnosis*, Kegan Paul, 1983, p. 60).

When his guidance was completed, the Persian scholar felt duty-bound to show gratitude to his Master and to exert himself for his Lord. He used to hear his spiritual father repeat a proverb that held the rank of soul to his body: *"The most excellent of deeds is giving life to the dead"* — meaning, in the proverb's settled reading, imparting knowledge to the ignorant, for the ignorant is like a corpse. The proverb itself echoes a prophetic hadith preserved in the canonical Sunni collection of Ibn Majah: *"The seeking of knowledge is incumbent on every Muslim; whoever teaches knowledge to one who does not deserve it is like one who hangs jewels on swine"* (*Sunan Ibn Majah*, Book of the Sunnah, hadith 224). The proverb takes the prophetic seed and turns it outward: the dead are not the ignorant who refuse the call, but the ignorant who can still be raised.

## The chain that places him under debt

So he said to himself: "I used to be dead, and my master gave me life. I was ignorant, and he gave me knowledge. I am not the first to be ignorant, nor the last — so out of gratitude I must pass on this divine trust to those who come after me, just as those before me handed it down to me. For the origin of this affair is from God, and it reached me only through its many intermediaries, the first passing it on to the second, the second to the third, until it descended from the heavenly host to the creatures of this lower world.

"After that, did all this knowledge rip through the veil and pass through the intermediaries until it reached me, only so that I could be its final goal and ultimate end? No! Those who transmitted it before me are more deserving of precedence — in fact I myself am part of what they earned by teaching me. And their precedence does not relieve me from having to act: my final duty is similar to my initial duty."

Read the structure of this interior speech. It is a logical argument the scholar conducts on himself, not on anyone else. He starts from the proverb the most excellent of deeds is giving life to the dead. He recognizes that he himself was the dead one whom his master raised. He then asks the decisive counterfactual: did the chain of transmission rip through the veil, pass through every intermediary, descend from the heavenly host all the way to him, only so that he could be its endpoint? The answer is no — the same answer that requires action.

The same chain that elevated the previous transmitters into the very thing they earned by teaching the next one is now obligating him. The structural symmetry is exact: *my final duty is similar to my initial duty*. The first who received the trust was duty-bound to pass it on. So is the second. So is this Persian scholar. So, eventually, will be the disciple the scholar has not yet found. The chain runs not by institutional design but by a debt that every receiver of the trust owes to the one who passed it on. The scholar's only honest gratitude to his own master is to become, in his turn, the master of someone else. The Father of Imams puts the principle in a single sentence in the same collection: *"Whoever teaches me a single letter has made me his servant"* (al-Amidi, *Ghurar al-Hikam*, aphorism 8953) — and the chain in the dialogue is exactly that servitude, paid forward through generations.

So he traveled toward his Lord. He left his people and possessions behind. He passed among the Arab and non-Arab tribes, scrutinizing their faces for signs of spiritual aptitude and posing religious questions — but he found no response and met no seeker, until he reached the furthest part of the river country, at the far edge of the inhabited world, with a scholar who has crossed continents and not yet found the one human being for whom the trust will be released.

The scholar has answered the proverb. He has accepted the debt. He has walked. He has not found his disciple yet.
