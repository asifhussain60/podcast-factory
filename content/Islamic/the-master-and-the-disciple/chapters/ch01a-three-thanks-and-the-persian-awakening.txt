# Three Thanks and the Persian Awakening

*Episode one of* The Master and the Disciple, *the tenth-century dialogue by Sayyidina Ja'far ibn Mansur al-Yaman. The book is the timeless story of a seeker's quest for true knowledge and the process through which he gradually achieves it. This first episode opens that arc by asking a single, load-bearing question: when a person receives a call to true religion, a body of knowledge, and a set of practices, how does that person actually thank the one who gave them? The Master answers with a doctrine of gratitude that turns thanks into deeds, then names the three-stage spiritual path of trial, guidance, and piety. The episode then narrates the story of a Persian seeker whose thirst for knowledge led him through mirage after mirage, until he found Allah at the very point where the mirages dissolved into nothing, and how that finding placed him under a debt that could be repaid only by setting out, in his turn, to find a disciple of his own. The chapter that opens this book is also the chapter that opens this entire series of episodes, and what it lands here will be load-bearing for everything that follows.*

## Where this episode opens

It has reached us that a number of the truly faithful, and a group among the du'at al-din — those who call people to the true religion — said to one of their scholars: "You have liberated us by helping us learn about an affair of great importance, and for that we feel obligated to express our gratitude for three reasons. Our gratitude for your having called us to the true religion. Our gratitude for the knowledge you pointed us towards. And our gratitude for the religious practices you commanded us to perform.

"So inform us how we should express our gratitude for these three blessings. Inform us about the rights and duties that are obligatory upon us as directed by the ordinances of religion. Tell us about the obligations of a seeker in his mode of questioning — the proper etiquette for seeking knowledge. Tell us about the obligations upon the scholar in his response — how one should impart his knowledge to the one who seeks it. And also inform us about the ways of the righteous and the proper behavior of the seekers."

Hear the way that opening request is shaped. The seekers do not ask for content first. They ask for the etiquette of the asking. They name the three gifts already received — the da'wa, the 'ilm, the 'amal, the call, the knowledge, the practice — and present each one as an outstanding debt of thanks. They acknowledge that the asking itself has rules. They want to know how a seeker is supposed to question and how a scholar is supposed to answer, before they hear the answer to anything.

## Comportment, gratitude, and the three thanks

At the very outset the author highlights two extremely important concepts: shukr, gratitude, and adab, comportment. Comportment is the way you carry yourself in relation to someone or something. It is not merely good manners. It is the force that opposes desire — the disposition that structures every act of worship as a form of self-surrender. The force opposing gratitude and comportment is desire itself. This is why this tradition is so focused on ithar, on preferring others over the self: because preferring the other draws a person out of the traps of egocentricity. We are designed as 'abd for the task of 'ubudiyya — servants created for the task of service. Servitude is what makes life enjoyable as an experience, and servitude to Allah is the greatest of all delights.

Adab in its essence is prioritizing someone else over the self. When one considers even the adab of eating, the whole process is designed to respect and prioritize that sustenance over the self, to humble the self in relation to the blessing of food. The Prophet (Salallahu alayhi wa aalihee wa sallam) said: *Sit before your food like a slave sits before its master.* Honor the food by covering your head, by using your right hand — because without that food we would be reduced to animals.

Hold that picture. The seekers in the story are not at a table. They are at the door of a scholar. But the adab is the same adab. The food has been given. The thanks owed for it must look like a deed.

The Master replied: "Now the affair to which I invite you is the religion that Allah has bestowed upon his servants as an honor — a religion he has perfected for them and through which he honors whoever responds to Him. So for every beginning he has placed within it an end, and for every end he designed a goal, and each goal has a limit whose full extent cannot be attained. These are the way-stations of the people of true understanding. The key to success is the constant remembrance of Allah, dhikr Allah."

Notice the move. The Master answers the question about gratitude not with three thanks but with a metaphysics of stages. Every beginning has been given an end. Every end has been given a goal. Every goal has a limit whose full extent cannot be attained. The seeker who wants to thank for three gifts is being told, first, that the gifts themselves are arranged inside a divine order of stages, each stage opening onto the next, the whole sequence aimed at a limit that the seeker can travel toward but never exhaust. Gratitude has to be calibrated against the shape of the gift, and the shape of the gift turns out to be a road.

The Master continued: "Their initial level is trial, balwa; their middle state is right guidance, huda; and their final stage is piety, taqwa." This is the three-stage spiritual path of the book: balwa, then huda, then taqwa. The initial stage is trial, because the unrefined nafs rebels. The self gets in the way like a wild horse refusing to be tamed, pushing the seeker toward what the self wants rather than what Allah wants. Once the seeker succeeds in taming this wild horse, the next stage is huda: he begins to find guidance and to experience its benefits. The final stage is taqwa: a constant, deep-rooted awareness of the presence of Allah in everything the seeker does. The tool that helps the seeker persevere through these three stages is dhikr Allah, the remembrance of Allah, repeated until it becomes a second pulse.

Only after he has placed the gifts inside this three-stage path does the Master answer the original question. "Whoever seeks to learn the basics of Allah's affairs with the truest of intentions, and then discovers the divinely determined ranks — hudud al-din al-maqdur — has sought guidance from the correct sources and has set upon it in the best possible manner. As for showing gratitude to the scholar, that is through obeying him — not a verbal thank-you, but obedience. As for gratitude for the knowledge, it is turning it into action and calling others to it. And the gratitude for the right practice is striving patiently in it, with sabr, and being consistent with it."

The spiritual guide is like a doctor who prescribes a medication for an illness. Praising the doctor will not cure you. What cures you is doing what he prescribes, consistently, for the prescribed duration — taking the dose at the right times, not stopping when you start to feel better, not skipping the bitter ones. The Master answers the seekers' three thanks with three deeds because in his settled doctrine the only honest gratitude is a deed, and the only honest deed is the deed the giver of the gift named. These three thanks form the book's recurring three-part template: the call answered by obedience to the teacher, the knowledge answered by action, the action answered by perseverance. Every time the disciple later falls short, it will be against one of these three.

## The Persian scholar and his thirst

The author then narrates the story of a man from Persia. He had a rich heart, a brilliant intellect, and a decent education. Yet he was subject to the trials of spiritual ignorance, and his ignorance outweighed his knowledge. He was restless in the burning heat of his thirst for knowledge, assuming the glimmering of mirages to be the reflection of water, until he reached it only to find it nothing — yet there he found Allah, and paid Him his account in full.

Stay with that image for a moment. A man crosses a desert. He sees water on the horizon. He runs to it. There is no water. He runs to the next shimmer. Again nothing. And again, and again. At some point the seeker arrives at the very point where the mirage has resolved into the nothing it always was — and it is *at that exact point of nothing*, at the bottom of the disappointment, that he meets Allah and pays his account in full.

Then Allah honored his abode and removed the veil from his intellect through a teacher who found him astray and guided him; who found him in need and satisfied him with knowledge. Thus he became one of the great scholars of Sinai, an angelic dweller in the well-populated heavenly temple, the Bayt al-Ma'mur. The Persian seeker, restless in the burning heat, becomes through a teacher one of the people of Sinai. Notice the geography of the metamorphosis. He starts in Persia, walks through mirages, and arrives at the place where prophecy itself was once received. The chapter does not announce that. It is telling the story of how a person travels from ignorance to knowledge by being found.

When his guidance was completed, the Persian scholar felt duty-bound to show gratitude to his Master and to exert himself for his Lord. He used to hear his spiritual father repeat a proverb that held the rank of soul to his body: *The most excellent of deeds is giving life to the dead* — meaning, in the proverb's settled reading, imparting knowledge to the ignorant, for the ignorant is like a corpse.

## The chain that places him under debt

So he said to himself: "I used to be dead, and my master gave me life. I was ignorant, and he gave me knowledge. I am not the first to be ignorant, nor the last — so out of gratitude I must pass on this divine trust to those who come after me, just as those before me handed it down to me. For the origin of this affair is from Allah, and it reached me only through its many intermediaries, the asbab, the first passing it on to the second, the second to the third, until it descended from the heavenly host to the creatures of this lower world.

"After that, did all this knowledge rip through the veil and pass through the intermediaries until it reached me, only so that I could be its final goal and ultimate end? No! Those who transmitted it before me are more deserving of precedence — in fact I myself am part of what they earned by teaching me. And their precedence does not relieve me from having to act: my final duty is similar to my initial duty."

Read the structure of this interior speech. It is a logical argument the scholar conducts on himself, not on anyone else. He starts from the proverb the most excellent of deeds is giving life to the dead. He recognizes that he himself was the dead one whom his master raised. He then asks the decisive counterfactual: did the chain of transmission rip through the veil, pass through every intermediary, descend from the heavenly host all the way to him, only so that he could be its endpoint? The answer is no — the same answer that requires action.

The same chain that elevated the previous transmitters into the very thing they earned by teaching the next one is now obligating him. The structural symmetry is exact: *my final duty is similar to my initial duty*. The first who received the trust was duty-bound to pass it on. So is the second. So is this Persian scholar. So, eventually, will be the disciple the scholar has not yet found. The chain runs not by institutional design but by a debt that every receiver of the trust owes to the one who passed it on. The scholar's only honest gratitude to his own master is to become, in his turn, the master of someone else.

So he traveled towards his Lord. He left his people and possessions behind. He passed among the Arab and non-Arab tribes, scrutinizing their faces for signs of spiritual aptitude and posing religious questions — but he found no response and met no seeker, until he reached the furthest part of the jazira, the river country. The chapter ends his first leg here, at the far edge of the inhabited world, with a scholar who has crossed continents and not yet found the one human being for whom the trust will be released.

## Closing

The episode lands with the Persian scholar at the far edge of al-jazira, his journey unfinished. He has answered the proverb. He has accepted the debt. He has walked. He has not found his disciple yet. The next episode will pick up exactly there — with the scholar entering a city where nobody knows him, sitting out of sight to listen to a group of townspeople debating religion without a guide, and choosing, from the way one of them holds his silence and shapes his questions, the youngest in that group to follow him back to his inn.
