# Who Is Allah? — Beyond Names and Adjectives

*Episode seventeen of* The Master and the Disciple. *Salih opens the climax of the debate by refusing to let the conversation about Allah's justice happen at the level of names and adjectives. He insists that the words we use about Allah — "one," "just," "Creator" — must answer to realities behind them, and he presses Abu Malik on the names and the very letters of "Allah" until the easy creed dissolves. Abu Malik's fallback argument — that the world points by analogy to a Creator — is itself dismantled as a hidden likeness. By the end Abu Malik confesses: my words have left me; intellects are bewildered; imaginations cannot reach Him.*

## Where this episode opens

The episode opens with Salih stating what he has been calling Abu Malik toward. "I am calling you to Allah's justice and to the assertion of His unity." Abu Malik thinks the formula familiar. He has heard it. He can recite it. He treats it as one of the standard pieties of any believing community. Salih corrects him before he can settle into it. The school Abu Malik subscribes to, Salih says, describes Allah's justice without any clear understanding of who is being described, and strays regarding His names without any real understanding of His unity. The opening move is to refuse the shared vocabulary as common ground. Justice and unity, in Salih's mouth, are not the headers under which the two camps already agree; they are the two terms whose meaning the camps do not in fact share.

Abu Malik offers his creed. Allah is one. He is just. He commands by giving a choice. He forbids by giving warning. The four-line catechism is honest about what Abu Malik thinks the words do. They name attributes. They package the doctrine. They make it transmissible. Salih's reply makes the gap visible.

## Names cannot cook food

"Those are only names and descriptions," Salih says, "of no benefit without their essential realities." Then he gives the analogy that will govern the rest of the episode. "It is like saying fire is hot. True — but you cannot cook food with the words, nor brighten darkness without the reality of fire. If nouns and adjectives could take the place of realities, your tongue would burn at the mere mention of fire, and your hunger would be satiated at the mention of food."

The image is exact. The word "fire" is correct as a word. It picks out something real. But the word is not the thing. The word does not cook. The word does not light. A community that has only the word, and has lost the access to the reality, is not a community that has fire. It is a community that says "fire" and grows cold. A creed is the same. The creed about Allah's unity is correct as a sentence. But a creed without the access to the reality it points to does not unite anything. It does not feed anyone. It does not light any darkness. To recite "Allah is one, Allah is just" while having no access to what those words denote is to repeat the syllable "fire" and shiver.

Salih is not arguing that the words are wrong. He is arguing that the words alone are not the thing. The school he is criticizing has treated the words as if they were. The proof, he is about to say, is that the school's most careful theological work happens at the level of the words, not at the level of the realities they point to.

## The letters that do not add up to Allah

Then Salih presses on the letters themselves. The move is startling in how concrete it is.

"You say Allah is one — written with four letters, two joined and two separate. Which is 'one'? And the word Allah is also written with four letters — which of them is 'Allah'? Are they Allah together, or are they signs pointing to Allah?" Abu Malik concedes the only honest answer. They must be signs pointing to Allah. They cannot be Allah. The point of the question is that the letters obviously do not have the divine reality in them. They are marks on a page. They name. They do not contain.

Salih presses further. "But then which letters point to Him? When you say 'Allah,' you separate the initial alif and join the remaining three letters. Which of these points to Him — the one by itself, or the others joined?" Abu Malik does not know what to say. There is no answer he can give that the next question will not dissolve. If he says the alif points, the rest is unaccounted for. If he says the joined three point, the alif is left over. If he says all four together point, he is back at the first answer and has gained nothing. The grammatical structure of the name does not correspond, in any way Abu Malik can specify, to the divine reality it is supposed to point to.

The point of the question is not philological. The point is what Salih names next. The school has poured its scholarship into the structure of the word — into composing beautiful litanies of Allah's names, into perfecting the visible form of ablution and the angles of bowing and the placement of prostration — and has forgotten the essential meaning. "In the end it all boils down to one simple fact. Do you understand who Allah is, the reality behind His unity, or do you only have a textbook definition of that tawhid?"

The criticism is not that ritual form is bad. The criticism is that ritual form has displaced the question it was meant to answer. The community has mastered the choreography of saying "fire" and has lost the fire. It can recite the name and stage the gesture. It cannot show that anyone inside the recitation has come into contact with what the recitation is for.

## The forged likeness and the bewildered intellect

Salih then asks where Abu Malik's understanding of Allah actually comes from. From a name? From an attribute? Abu Malik says: from a name. The name "Allah." Salih steps the question down.

"Is Allah a name, or someone named?" "Someone named." "Then what is His name?" "Allah." "Allah is a name for Allah? Then what is the difference between the name and the one named — and which one do you worship?"

The trap closes neatly. Abu Malik cannot say he worships the name; that is idolatry of the word. He cannot say he worships "the one named" without specifying how he knows that one, since the name has just been shown to be a sign and not the thing. He is bewildered.

He falls back on the move every careful theologian eventually falls back on. He has, he says, observed that everything manufactured has a manufacturer; from the immense order of this universe he has concluded that it must have a Creator who does not resemble His creations. The argument is the standard argument by analogy. It is the move the school is best at. It is the move a careful man uses to feel that he has earned his belief honestly.

Salih corrects even this. He does not say the conclusion is wrong. He says the route is wrong, and the route has cost.

"You have taken us away from your original meaning. Your difficulty in comprehending Allah's unity forced you to seek Him through logical assumption — and so you forged a likeness for Him." The argument from manufactured things assumes a relationship between the universe and Allah that is modeled on the relationship between a craftsman and the thing he makes. The craftsman is observable. The thing is observable. The relation is observable. From observing the relation we can infer the maker. Salih's reply is that this whole transfer secretly puts Allah inside the comparison. "Everything is made from a pre-existing substance," he says. "Allah creates from no substance. As for worldly manufacturers, you became aware of them by observing how they constructed things — but Allah did not make you witness His creation, nor does He have a likeness whose creation you could observe."

Then he names the giveaway in the formula Abu Malik's school uses. The school says "there is nothing like His image" — and Salih points out that the very phrasing implies He has an image. "If you truly meant absolute unity, you would have said there is none like Him. He is far exalted beyond your implicit analogy." The chosen wording smuggles the comparison back into the denial. The denial of likeness has still kept a likeness under the table.

He closes with the lesser comparison that exposes the limit of the senses. "Eyes cannot tolerate the light of the sun — which is only one of this lower world's lights. So how can they perceive the Creator?" The faculty meant to see the world cannot bear even the world's own greater objects without breaking down. To imagine it then competent for the Creator of the world is to overrate the eye and underrate the One.

There is a second move under Salih's correction that is worth marking before it is missed. The argument from manufactured things is a good argument when it is held in the right place. It is the argument the Quran itself uses when it points to the sky, to the alternation of day and night, to the milk between dung and blood. The verses do not reject the move; they make it constantly. What Salih is rejecting is the use of the move as a definition of Allah rather than as an invitation toward Him. As an invitation it is honest. The world is full of signs; the signs point; the seeker follows. As a definition it is dishonest. It claims to have told you what Allah is — by analogy to a craftsman — and the analogy carries with it the very limit it was meant to deny. The craftsman uses material; Allah does not. The craftsman is observed; Allah is not. The craftsman is one being among many; Allah is not. The analogy could not survive any of these corrections. So the analogy was never giving Abu Malik what he thought it was giving him. It was giving him the comfort of having proved something, and was hiding from him the fact that the proof had been bought at the price of the very unity it was supposed to defend.

Abu Malik confesses where this has brought him. "My words have left me. Intellects are bewildered. Imaginations are incapable of thinking about Him." The confession is not a defeat in the debate. It is the first honest sentence in the conversation about Allah that Abu Malik has been able to make. The names have been emptied of the function he was using them for. The letters have been shown not to carry the divine. The analogy has been shown to forge a likeness. What is left to Abu Malik is the awareness that he has been describing without knowing — and that awareness, in the source's economy, is exactly the posture in which the next stage of the argument can begin.

## Closing

The opening of the climax has done exactly what Abu Malik's standard was supposed to require. The terms "justice" and "unity" have been refused as shared furniture; the creed of names and adjectives has been refused as access to the realities; the analysis of letters has shown how the school spent its scholarship on the form of the word and lost the meaning; and the analogical proof of a Creator has been shown to forge a hidden likeness. The eye cannot see the sun; the intellect cannot weigh the One. Abu Malik does not protest. He says where his own resources have ended. That confession is the doorway. The next episode walks through it, into the syllogism by which Allah's justice requires a living witness in every age.
