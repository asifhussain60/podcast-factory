# Abu Malik Arrives and the Fair Inquiry

*Episode fourteen of* The Master and the Disciple. *Salih and al-Bakhtari have become visible. Their generosity and their knowledge have made them respected in their region, and word of their religious position has reached the community they left. The former co-religionists of al-Bakhtari are afraid — afraid of losing his favors, afraid of being on the wrong side of whatever has happened. They send for the greatest scholar of their sect, the Yemeni Abu Malik — known among them as Kab al-Ahbar, "the glory of the yearned" — to handle Salih and his father. Abu Malik does not act as expected. He refuses to condemn without hearing; he refuses to assume guidance without testing; he names the inversion of signs by which truth is hard to recognize; and he insists that he and his companions go together, today, not in three days, not in two.*

## Where this episode opens

The previous episode closed with the sentence the source allowed itself to write at the end of the homecoming: through father and son, Allah gave life to a great number of people in that country. That sentence has a price. Visibility is not free. A son who has converted his father, and a father whose conversion has carried a great number of others, becomes a thing the world will respond to. The community al-Bakhtari left did not stop existing. Its members watched him give. They watched him give to them. And now they are watching him give his loyalty somewhere else.

The episode begins with this watching. Salih and his father have become respected — for generosity, for knowledge, for the rank their conversion has given them in the eyes of the people around them. Their religious position has spread. It has reached the people who knew al-Bakhtari before. And those people — the followers of the former beliefs whom al-Bakhtari had been generous to — are afraid.

## Who Abu Malik is and why he was summoned

The fear of these former co-religionists has a shape that matters. They are afraid of losing al-Bakhtari's favors now that he has left their sect. The fear is honest about itself. It is not at the level of doctrine in its first move; it is at the level of dependence on the patron whose generosity has supported them. But around that core sits a second fear — the fear of being wrong. If their old friend has found the truth, then they have been left behind. If their old friend has been led into error, then they have been negligent in not preventing it. Either way they are exposed. Either way they need help.

So they look for a champion. They send for Abu Malik. The source records his standing with care.

His name is Abd al-Jabbar ibn Abu Malik. Among them he is known as Kab al-Ahbar, "the glory of the yearned" — a title given for his scrupulous piety, his great understanding, and his passionate devotion. He is a seventh-century Yemeni scholar, famous for his knowledge and for his ability to defeat people in debates. The honorific in the community signifies the highest scholarly reputation that community can confer. Whoever debates Abu Malik is not debating a naive questioner. He is debating the very best the opposing community can field.

The men who summon him do not hide their motives. They tell Abu Malik that Salih, son of al-Bakhtari, has left their sect under the influence of a persuasive stranger, and that al-Bakhtari has now joined his son. They confess what they have done so far. They have avoided the two men. And they have avoided them for three reasons. Their gratitude for al-Bakhtari's past favors. The fear that, if these people are following something authentic, they themselves are at fault for not following it. And the fear that, if these people have strayed, they themselves are at fault for not preventing it. The three reasons together describe a community that knows it has not done its duty in either direction.

The trap they have laid for themselves is now in Abu Malik's hands. He could solve it for them in either of two cheap ways. He could condemn Salih and al-Bakhtari sight unseen, which would let his clients keep their grievance and their hope of restored favors. Or he could pronounce that Salih and al-Bakhtari must be right, since their generosity is real and their knowledge has grown, which would let his clients run after them. He does neither. His response is the body of this episode.

## Abu Malik's fair inquiry

Abu Malik refuses to condemn al-Bakhtari and his son without first hearing them. That, he says, would be injustice. And he refuses to blindly assume that they are rightly guided. That, he says, would be injustice too. He has named the two cheap moves and ruled them both out. He will neither prejudge against nor prejudge in favor. He will go and find out.

Then he warns his own companions against a danger they may not have noticed they are in. He warns against following the passions of the false idols of previous religious communities — those who were pleased by their own sound intellect and rejoiced in the knowledge they possessed, assuming they had encompassed everything from Allah's command. They said, he reminds them, that Allah would never send a messenger after their messenger. They did not realize there was a way of proceeding beyond the knowledge they had attained. The warning lands on his own companions as much as on anyone. It is the classical error of a community satisfied with what it already has. The error has a name in the Quran's own accounts of past peoples and Abu Malik is naming it to his own friends in the same breath.

Then he gives them a fairer frame for thinking about Salih and al-Bakhtari. The change of belief they have witnessed in their friends, he says, can only be for one of three reasons. Either Salih and al-Bakhtari are still on the old religion with an added element — tradition, or poetry, or some local custom — in which case there is nothing fundamental at stake. Or the stranger has brought them something pleasing in addition to their religion — in which case, Abu Malik says, knowledge is the lost camel of the believer, and an increase in knowledge is better than its lack. Or — and this is the third — their guidance has come from a true source of prophethood, in which case they are following clear guidance.

In all three readings the response is the same. Go and find out. There is no version of the situation in which the right move is to stay home and condemn the converts from a distance. The first reading dissolves the problem. The second reading turns the converts into a resource. The third reading turns them into a guide. None of the three rewards Abu Malik's clients for the avoidance they have practiced for so long. None of the three lets the audit be deferred.

## The inversion of signs and the sound heart

Then Abu Malik names what makes the audit hard. The audit is hard, he says, because truth wears a costume the eye does not expect.

"Pride in one's own opinion is blindness, and neglecting to seek Allah is a sin. But there is no fault for those who continue searching." That is the door. Behind it is the description of why the search is hard.

"The distinctive signs of falsehood manifest themselves in hypocritical appearances and in conformity to accepted opinions. But the clear signs of truth only manifest themselves through tests and afflictions, and through the opposition of passions. Only those with a sound heart — qalb salim — can renounce their passions and persevere through the onset of tests."

The image Abu Malik gives his companions is the inversion of the natural reading of a market. The thing that looks well-made is often the thing that has been polished by the hands of many to look well-made. The thing that comes through tests and against the run of one's own passions is often the thing whose signs are the marks of the trial itself. Falsehood appears attractive and easy. Truth appears harsh and demanding. This is, he tells them, why the majority always follow the easier-appearing path. The market is full of glass that has been polished to look like jewel. The jewel is the one that has come through fire.

He gives them, too, a working definition of the sound heart. A sound heart, he says, is one that does not insist on denying what occurs to it, until the truth reveals its superiority and falsehood unveils its defects. The sound heart is open to what enters it; it suspends judgment; it lets the rival proposals show themselves; only then does it choose. The proud heart denies first and assesses later, by which time the choice has already been made.

And then Abu Malik adds the move that will govern the rest of his approach to Salih. He tells his companions that true understanding of the search means knowing that you are in need of what you seek, and approaching it with the humility that comes from that need — a humility that must come before the truth becomes clear, for only through it can one witness the manifestation of truth. The humility cannot be a reward at the end of the search; it must be the posture in which the search is made. The truth does not unveil itself to the audit that is sure of itself in advance. It unveils itself to the auditor who already knows he is in need.

His companions, the source records, were softened. Their hearts moved. They asked permission to go to Salih and al-Bakhtari. Abu Malik refused to send them alone. He would go with them. He would not, he said, spare himself what he asked of them. And they must act today — not in three days, not in two. "I will do something good tomorrow is a satanic thought; the only time to do something is today and now."

So they repented of their sins. They repaid their debts. They purified themselves. And they set out the same day, saying, there is no power except through Allah.

## Closing

The man the opposing community has fielded is not the dim caricature a reader might expect. Abu Malik refuses both cheap moves on which his clients had been counting — neither condemnation without hearing, nor assumption of guidance without testing. He warns his own companions against the very error of past communities who were satisfied with the knowledge they had. He sets out three possible readings of Salih's conversion under each of which the right move is to go and meet. He names the inversion of signs by which falsehood wears the costume of conformity and truth wears the costume of trial. He defines the sound heart as the one that does not deny what enters it. He names humility before the truth as the precondition of seeing the truth at all. And he refuses delay. The greatest scholar of the opposing community has just set the terms of his own debate by the standard of justice. The next episode brings him to al-Bakhtari's door.
