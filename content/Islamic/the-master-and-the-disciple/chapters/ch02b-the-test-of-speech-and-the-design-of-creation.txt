# The Test of Speech and the Design of Creation

*Episode two of* The Master and the Disciple. *The Persian scholar, having crossed Arab and non-Arab lands without finding a disciple, arrives at the far edge of the river country and enters a city where nobody knows him. He sits out of sight to listen to a group of townspeople discuss religion without a guide. From the way they hold themselves and the questions they ask, he picks them — for three precise reasons — as deserving of life. They test his speech and he answers with a doctrine of transmissive authority. Then he rises and delivers the great glorification of Allah, the design-of-creation sermon — recited here whole, the way the source recites it — that names the outer and the inner as a cosmic preservation principle rather than a textual layer. The chapter closes with the disciple's question already half-asked: if the design of creation requires intermediaries, what is the instrument by which a seeker uses them? The Master answers with the Quranic triad of hearing, sight, and the heart.*

## Where this episode picks up

The Persian scholar had crossed continents looking for a disciple and found no response. At the furthest part of the river country he entered a city where nobody knew him. He noticed a group of townspeople discussing religion without any guidance, recklessly following the inclinations of their passions. He sat nearby, out of sight, listening carefully and analyzing which of them was closer to the right path.

That pause matters. The scholar did not interrupt to correct them. He did not knock on a door. He sat out of sight until the questions being asked told him whether the asking was real. Then he said to himself: "These people are more deserving of life — this spiritual knowledge — for three reasons. First, the true divine argument has reached them. Second, they belong to the people of my community. And third, they are closer to the right path by their keen interest in religion and the fact that they are inquiring about it. *Indeed the person who is already searching for something is much closer to finding it.*"

The three-condition test — that the divine argument has reached them, that they belong to the community, and that they are already inquiring — is the scholar's working catalog for deservedness. The fact of inquiry is signal; the absence of inquiry is signal of a different kind. The chapter's doctrine of discernment runs through that quiet detail of where the scholar sits. Knowledge that lands in the wrong heart is not merely wasted; it could have an adverse effect. The scholar searched for the one who deserved it, who had the right desire — a burning desire — and the right attitude, aptitude, and capacity to bear that knowledge.

The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, made the same point in a saying preserved in the canonical Sunni Prophetic-traditions collection (the chapter on knowledge, sermon section): "Whoever Allah wishes good for, He grants him understanding of the religion." The pairing of divine wish and human understanding is the same pairing the scholar listens for. Inquiry is the visible trace of an invisible grant. Where there is no trace, no teacher arrives. Where the trace is real, a stranger sits down across the room and waits.

## The test of speech and transmissive authority

When the townspeople completed their discussion, they turned to him and asked who he was. He answered that he was a servant of Allah and one of the residents of His sanctuary; that his business — the acquisition of knowledge — was complete, and that he was now looking for work, the spreading of that acquired knowledge.

They tested him about commendable and reprehensible speech. He answered that commendable speech is that which is true: speech whose origin comes from Allah and through which people are called towards Allah — the source uses a word meaning accurate, correctness aimed at its mark. Reprehensible speech is that based on the passions of the soul, used to call people to something other than guidance. Then he added the sentence the disciple will return to again and again: "I have no words of my own, for I am following the words of another."

That sentence is the chapter's first definition of authority. The scholar's authority is transmissive. He does not own the words he speaks. The criterion of true speech is not the speaker's eloquence; it is the origin of the speech in Allah and its intent of calling toward Allah. The townspeople recognize that the criterion is being applied to the scholar's own answer at the same time the scholar is teaching them the criterion. He has tested himself in front of them by the very test he just named. Recognizing the truth in his answers, they asked him to become "a gateway for us, through which Allah may send His loving kindness for us."

The Commander of the Faithful captures the same standard in a terse aphorism from the standard compilation of his sermons and letters (aphorism number seventy-three in the conventional ordering, Sayed Ali Reza's English rendering): "The tongue of the wise is behind his heart, and the heart of the fool is behind his tongue." Speech that originates from Allah passes through the heart first; speech that originates in the passions of the soul runs ahead of any deliberation at all. The townspeople's request — that the scholar serve as a gateway — is recognition that they have heard a tongue placed behind a heart, not a heart dragged behind a tongue.

That is the rhetorical position from which the scholar then rises. He has been recognized as a gate. The townspeople have stopped testing and started receiving. What he delivers next is not a continuation of conversation. It is a sermon.

## The design-of-creation sermon

The scholar stood, faced the attentive group, and began his glorification of Allah:

*Indeed! He created with His power, then arranged everything perfectly. He determined a path through His generosity, then guided his creation towards it. He brought forth the paths of the pastureland, then made its outer form a dried-up brush and its inner aspect broad in scope and content. His knowledge does not exist outwardly so that the unworthy could possess and alter it. Instead his knowledge is benevolent and well-protected because of its excellence; well concealed, its secrets guarded for those worthy of them. Its dignity is the splendid robe of guidance, and its perfection is the garment of piety. It is marked out by the light of lights, entrusted safely in the hearts of the best and the chosen ones — the truly pious, the guardians, the protectors, the generous and the recorders. This knowledge is a trust which they safely pass on, and they do not transgress the divine ordinances. He honored his knowledge by hiding it and making it inward, protected it by keeping it secret, veiled it with His obligations, and crowned it with His covenants.*

*For it cannot be reached by the aspirations of those who rely on the strength of their intellects, nor attained by those scheming for it. Its outer aspect is a reminder and a sign for the rightly guided, while its inner aspect is a sweet drink gushing forth. His lofty heavens come down close, his earth is paved for those who inquire about Him, his guiding stars are organized for those who seek Him, and he has signs whose wonders follow each other in succession, pointing to Him through their symbols. His accustomed way is preferment, preferring the righteous over the unrighteous; his method is approached through distinguished ranks. He cannot be accessed without his intermediaries — this is why we have the concept of the linking-means in everything. He has made his ways a guide and a path leading to good, with its outer aspect a protective shell and a sign pointing toward its inner dimensions.*

This is the blueprint of Allah's design. He took all the spiritual realities and hid them in the shell of the exterior. Everything we see in this world — the sky, the earth, the sun, the moon, the stars, the changing seasons, the winds, the oceans, the rivers — represents inner spiritual realities. He veiled the spiritual realities within physical creations, *therefore immortalizing them*. People will come and go, generations and entire nations will come and go, but these external and internal realities will live forever. The distinction between the outer and the inner is therefore not merely a textual or hermeneutical layer; it is a deliberate cosmic design principle, a mechanism of preservation. And he made the chosen ones, the trustees of his knowledge, the ones to unveil these realities; none of us can decipher these codes without them.

The chapter of the Cattle, verse fifty-nine in the translation by Marmaduke Pickthall, makes the same point about cosmic concealment in one breath: *And with Him are the keys of the unseen. None knows them but He. And He knows what is in the land and the sea. Not a leaf falls but He knows it; nor is there a grain in the darkness of the earth, nor anything fresh or dry, but is in a clear record.* The outer leaf falls into view; the inner record is held by Him alone, and released through the trustees He appoints. Cosmic order is documentary, not accidental, and the document is sealed.

As part of His design, Allah created this universe with all its components representing the dimensions of spirituality. He chose certain people based on their piety and capability and made them custodians of the knowledge of these spiritual realities — *and Allah taught Adam all the names*, the phrase from the chapter of the Cow (chapter 2, verse 31). Then, in His perfect order, He created our spiritual sustenance first, identifying His chosen people and giving them knowledge of everything, and only then created us with a need. He gave us an intellect designed to seek things out, and created us ignorant so that we would realize our need. So when our ignorance forces our intellects toward the acquisition of knowledge, these fountainheads of knowledge are already in place. *Blessed is the One Who placed the night and the day in succession, for those who reflect and those who wish to be grateful. May Allah's blessings be on the one He has specially chosen from His servants, and made a warner for all the worlds.*

Then the scholar said: *Be mindful of Allah like the one who fears Him; and fear Him like the one who is hoping to please Him. Seek knowledge and you will learn; act according to that knowledge and you will receive Allah's loving mercy.*

His eyes overflowed with tears, and he broke off and bid the group farewell. The intensity of his speech overcame everyone.

A young man from the group followed him — the youngest, yet of the best character, his intellect complete and his heart awakened by reflection. His father was a great elder among the most distinguished of the Arabs by lineage, and one of the most devoted supporters of the people of his religion — a rival community that had rejected the Imam. With the scholar's permission the young man accompanied him to his inn, where the scholar ordered food, and after they ate they began to talk.

## The instrument of seeking: hearing, sight, and heart

The young man said: "O wise man! You spoke and we listened. You preached with perfect eloquence; you adorned religion with the most beautiful ornaments; you motivated us with the most beautiful incentives; you called us through the most eminent responsibilities, forcing our intellects to inquire about all that has struck our hearts. You are like a tree: its roots firm, its splendor towering over the worlds, its fruits hanging low for those who desire them. So I ask you to become one of those blessed branches for me."

That preface is a perfect demonstration of courteous decorum — without flattery, for everything the young man recognized was actually present in the scholar's speech. Only after this preface did he come to his question: "You said that our minds are forced by their ignorance to seek knowledge. Now my mind is the most needy of all — so is there any way to life for me?"

The scholar replied: "Surely the One who created by his power did not create without purpose, nor did he create them fully knowledgeable, nor leave them wandering in ignorance. So he established a way out for them by means of an instrument, an intellect, around which he shaped their constitution. He made their hearing, their sight, and their hearts openings to accept the light of his knowledge, and by these same instruments he completed his argument for them."

The components of that instrument are hearing, sight, and the heart. This is why numerous verses of the Quran mention these three together.

From the chapter of the Sovereignty (chapter 67, verse 23): *Say: it is He Who has created you, and endowed you with hearing, sight, and hearts. Very few of you are grateful for these gifts.*

From the chapter of the Bee (chapter 16, verse 108): *They are those upon whose hearts, hearing and sight Allah has set a seal; and they are the heedless.*

From the chapter of the Cow (chapter 2, verse 7): *Allah has set a seal on their hearts and on their hearings, and on their eyes there is a covering; theirs will be a great torment.*

From the chapter of the Children of Israel (chapter 17, verse 36): *Indeed the hearing, the sight, and the heart — of each of those you will be questioned.*

Four verses, one structure. The same three instruments by which the call is heard, by which the signs are seen, by which truth is recognized inwardly. The first verse names the instruments as gift. The second names them sealed in those who refuse the call. The third names them covered. The fourth names them as the very things by which Allah will hold the listener to account. The instrument is the same instrument in every case. The difference is what the listener chooses to do with it.

The scholar continued: "He then placed the treasures of His wisdom and the specific characteristics of His will in the pure, chosen, and elite of his creation. He gave them the responsibility of worshiping Him and calling others, while imposing upon people the duty of responding to that call by using their instruments — so that He may distinguish the wicked from the good." He married our needs with His provision. He set up His chosen ranks — the Imams, the callers — as providers, created us with a need for that provision, and gave us the intellect to fulfill it. "Therefore there is no argument against Allah after sending his messengers, nor any excuse for straying after the right path has been clearly pointed out."

The Quranic phrasing the scholar quotes here is itself stitched out of the chapter of Women (chapter 4, verse 165, in the Pickthall rendering): *Messengers of good cheer and warning, in order that mankind might have no argument against Allah after the messengers.* The argument-from-the-instrument and the argument-from-the-messenger are two halves of the same case. Allah does not present a demand without first endowing the listener with the means to hear it and the messenger who delivers it. The listener who refuses cannot then claim deprivation. The court of the hereafter, in this design, is a court in which the defense of ignorance has already been retired before the trial begins.

## Closing

The disciple has been chosen. The sermon has been delivered. The instrument has been named. The young man's question stands open at the threshold of the work to come: he has heard the design, recognized the gate, named his own neediness, and asked for a way to life. The scholar's answer locates that way in three openings of the body — hearing, sight, heart — and three answering provisions of the divine order — the messengers, the imams, the callers. The chapter ends with the listener equipped and the listener accountable, the same instrument used for either accepting or refusing the call.
