# Rebirth of the Disciple and the Seven-Day Tawaf

*Episode twelve of* The Master and the Disciple. *The disciple and the scholar arrive at the door of the greater teacher — the Sheikh. The disciple has been carried by the dialogue across all the doctrine the book has so far laid down; now he comes to the figure to whom the scholar has been pointing all along. The dialogue begins with the Sheikh's praise of Allah — a sustained sermon that then turns into a personal exchange. The Sheikh asks the disciple his name and refuses every answer until one is true. He tells him a name can only be given after seven days, in honor of the new-born infant. And on the seventh day, after ablution and a fresh garment, the disciple is drawn close, taught the realities pens cannot write, and circles the House of Allah seven times in a circumambulation whose meaning the source unfolds as the love of the planets for their sun.*

## Where this dialogue picks up

What kind of teacher refuses to call a grown man by his own name — and makes him wait seven days, like a newborn, before giving him a true one? The two travelers reach the Sheikh and are admitted. The scholar, who has been the disciple's father in faith for the whole dialogue, now hands the disciple over to the figure he has himself been pointing toward — his own great spiritual father. The disciple has been told a name will come. He has been told he will become a gateway. He does not yet know how either becomes true. This is the moment when the Sheikh becomes the third character in the architecture the disciple has been hearing — the Sheikh who stands above the scholar and below only the one whose name is not yet uttered, the Imam. The Sheikh's first move is not a question. It is a praise of Allah that will set the tone of every exchange to come.

## The Sheikh's opening praise

The Sheikh begins with a sustained praise. It is offered as the opening of every serious teaching is offered — as a returning of language to its source before language is used to teach anything. The disciple hears it whole.

Praise be to Allah, who split the dark obscurity of hearts with His light, who out of His justice imposed upon His creatures only what they could bear. He harmoniously balanced His command between the two — doing what He commanded, and refraining from what He forbade — and named it "Justice." Creation is incapable of repaying the burden of what He deserves for all His blessings; yet He is only displeased by them when they are ungrateful. He exists everywhere, but not within boundaries. He is known, but cannot be described. He exists, yet cannot be seen. He is recognized, but cannot be fully known. The honor of people's intellect is its use in searching for Allah; among the rewards of seeking Him is finding Him; and a sign of having found Him is experiencing the sweetness of His presence. His distinctive sign is the truth, and the duties flowing from that truth are obligatory, as Allah Himself has said: "We will show them Our Signs in the universe and in their own selves, until it becomes manifest to them that this is the truth" (chapter forty-one, verse fifty-three, translation by Sahih International, *The Noble Qur'an*).

That praise is the doorway. Every sentence in it is a piece of the doctrine the dialogue has been building. Allah's command balanced between two — doing and refraining — and called Justice. Allah everywhere yet not bounded — present without limit. Allah known yet not described — recognizable through His signs but never circumscribed by speech. The honor of intellect named as its use in the search. The reward of the search named as finding. The sign of finding named as sweetness. The duties named as obligations grounded in the truth. The disciple receives this praise as the frame within which the next exchange will take place. The Sheikh has not yet asked him a question and has already given him an entire orientation. The opening cadence — Allah praised first by what He has done in creation, then by the paradoxes of His presence, then by the route by which intellect comes to recognize Him — sits in the long tradition of teaching sermons that begin with the same architecture. The collected sermons of the Father of Imams (may Allah be pleased with him), preserved by al-Sharif al-Radi in the book *The Peak of Eloquence* (Nahj al-Balagha, compiled in the late fourth century after the Hijra), open in this same way — praise that names the limits of describing the Praised before any other matter is taken up (William Chittick's annotated selection, *A Shiite Anthology*, State University of New York Press, nineteen eighty-one, pages thirty-one through forty-three, gathers several such openings in translation). The verse the Sheikh closes with is the keynote — the signs of Allah will be shown in the cosmos and in the self, and the showing is what manifests truth.

## The name that comes only after seven days

Then the Sheikh turns to the young man. "O brave youth," he asks, "what is your name?"

"Slave of Allah, son of the slave of Allah," the young man answers — the formula every Muslim shares.

"That is your description, not your name."

The young man tries again. "Then I am the freed man, son of the servant of Allah."

"Who set you free?"

The young man points to the scholar at his side.

"But if your teacher is owned by another and does not own you, has he any right to free you?"

"No."

The young man hangs his head. He has no answer. The exchange has not been an insult; it has been an exposure. He has presented himself by a shared description, then by a claim of freedom that does not survive scrutiny, and now he is at the bottom of the cup. The Sheikh, who has been waiting for exactly this moment, names what has happened. "How can we recognize something that has no name, even if it has been born?"

The young man understands. "I have been born to you," he says. "So give me a name."

The Sheikh's answer is the door to the rest of the dialogue. "That can only happen after the completion of seven days, in honor of the new-born infant." This is a ritual of birth-into-faith. A child is named on the seventh day. A disciple, newly born into recognition, will be named on the seventh day as well. The practice the Sheikh invokes is the Prophet's own. The canonical hadith collection of Sunan Abu Dawud preserves, in the section on the sacrifice for the new-born, the report that the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him) directed that the child be named, have its hair shaved, and a sacrifice be offered on the seventh day after birth (Sunan Abu Dawud, the section on the sacrifice for the new-born, hadith two thousand eight hundred thirty-seven, translation by Ahmad Hasan, *Sunan Abu Dawud*, Sh. Muhammad Ashraf, nineteen eighty-four, volume three, page eight hundred two). The Sheikh is not improvising the wait. He is treating the disciple's rebirth into recognition with exactly the protocol the tradition prescribes for the rebirth of a child into the world.

The number seven, the dialogue tells the disciple, is the number of life itself. The universe received its life through the divine command — "Be, and it is" — whose seventh letter, in its original tongue, begins life. The phrase recurs across the Quran as the formula by which Allah brings what He wills into existence (chapter thirty-six, verse eighty-two, translation by M. A. S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford University Press, two thousand four). All creation takes place in seven stages, as Allah Himself has described it: "We created the human being from a quintessence of clay. Then We made him into a sperm-drop in a secure place. Then We made the drop into a clinging clot, and We made the clot into a lump, and We made the lump into bones, and We clothed the bones with flesh; then We brought him forth as another creation. So blessed is Allah, the best of creators" (chapter twenty-three, verses twelve through fourteen, translation by Sahih International, *The Noble Qur'an*). Seven stages of the embryo. True creation, true life, comes from the seventh in every series of seven. One cannot name something until it is complete.

The young man tests one more question. "Will I own this name?"

"If you owned it," the Sheikh answers, "you would become the one worshiped. The name is your owner, and you are owned by it. For you cannot rise beyond your own spiritual rank." The name defines the rank, and the rank defines the limit. The disciple receives the principle that will govern the rest of his life as a disciple. He will not own his name; he will be owned by it. He cannot reach above his rank by wishing; the rank itself is what holds him in place.

## The seventh letter, the sun, and the tawaf

After the seven days have passed, the Sheikh orders the young man to perform a complete ablution and to dress in his finest garment. Then he draws him close. He speaks to him, the source records, of realities that cannot be comprehended by imagination, of which pens would fail to write — precious jewels unveiled only to the worthy. The disciple is given the heart of the teaching at the moment his rank can finally receive it. The book does not transcribe what he heard. It records its consequences. He came to know his master. He held on firmly to the unbreakable connection. He circled the House of Allah. He completed his pilgrimage through Allah's greatest sign.

That last sentence is what the rest of the dialogue unfolds. The circumambulation — the circling of the House of Allah seven times — has an inner meaning the bare practice does not state. When we go on pilgrimage and circle the House seven times, the source says, we simulate the motion of the planets orbiting the sun. The sun in this image symbolizes the Imam, or the House itself. We symbolize the planets, pulled in by the gravitational field of the sun — pulled in by the love of the Imam, by the love of Allah. The entire concept of esoteric pilgrimage symbolizes recognition of the Imam: orbiting him, staying within our lanes of obedience, moving in the designated direction at the designated speed. Love is symbolized by gravity. The inner reading of the pilgrimage rites as a map of the soul's relation to the Imam runs as a thread through the philosophical tradition the author belonged to. Henry Corbin traced this exact figure — the seven circuits as a cosmological reenactment that doubles as an inner ascent toward the pole around which the soul is held — in *Cyclical Time and Ismaili Gnosis* (Kegan Paul International, nineteen eighty-three, pages seventy-four through eighty-one). Farhad Daftary's survey *The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines* (Cambridge University Press, second edition, two thousand seven, pages two hundred forty through two hundred forty-three) gathers the parallel passages from the early philosophical authors of the mission in which the outer rite is read as a literal performance of an inner orientation. The author is not inventing the reading. He is handing the disciple a doctrine the tradition had already articulated.

The image will not loosen its grip on a careful listener. A planet does not orbit because it has decided to be near the sun. A planet orbits because a force it does not see has bound it to a centre it cannot reach. The orbit is the planet's life. To leave the orbit is to lose the centre and to drift into cold space; to fall toward the centre is to be consumed. The orbit is the shape in which the planet's love for the sun keeps the planet alive. The pilgrim's seven circuits of the House are, in this reading, a brief reenactment of the same. The pilgrim orbits the centre because the centre is what is holding him together. The seven circuits stand for the seven stages by which a life is given. And the recognition that the pilgrim is doing what the planets do is the recognition that brings the disciple, at the end of these seven days, to the object of his desire — meeting the Imam, and learning the true realities of existence.

The disciple, the source records, finally attained what he had been seeking. The dialogue that began with him unable to give his own name ends with him circling the centre to which his name will always belong.

## Closing

The Sheikh's praise opened this stage by naming the truth as the distinctive sign of Allah and the search as the honor of intellect. The naming exchange exposed the disciple to the limit of his own self-description and prepared him for the seven-day wait the new-born is given. The seventh day brought the ablution, the fine garment, the closeness, the unveiling of jewels pens cannot record, the holding firm of the unbreakable connection, and the seven circuits of the House that the source reads as the planets in love with their sun. The disciple has been named in a name he does not own, drawn into an orbit he did not choose, and brought to the meeting toward which the entire dialogue has been carrying him. The next movement of the book returns him to the world that did not know how to read any of this — the world of his own father, who will himself become the next person the disciple's new life calls toward the light.
