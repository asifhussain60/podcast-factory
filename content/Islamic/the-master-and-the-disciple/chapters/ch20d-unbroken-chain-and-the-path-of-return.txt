# Unbroken Chain and the Path of Return

*Episode twenty of* The Master and the Disciple. *The final episode of the book. Salih refutes the very notion of an interval between prophets by naming the chain link by link; he explains the concealment of the friends of Allah by the way of Moses fleeing into Midian; he names the three categories of people in relation to that concealment and locates the moral weight not on the enemies but on the sympathetic abandoners; he indicts the scholars who serve the oppressors as worse than the murderers themselves; and he offers the path of return, repentance accepted, the friends of Allah revealed when helped, the enemies defeated when abandoned. Abu Malik repents. The book closes with its seal.*

## Where this discussion opens

Abu Malik has accepted that Allah's argument continues through His just witnesses. The conspiracy formula has made his community's claim to closure look like a familiar pattern of severance. But a question remains and Abu Malik asks it. The Quran itself speaks of intervals between prophets, the long span between Abraham and Moses, the span between Moses and Jesus, the span between Jesus and the messenger who follows. If divine justice forbids the chain from being cut, what is to be done with the apparent absence of prophets in their own time, and with the language of an interval that the revelation itself uses?

Salih's reply does two things at once. It refuses the notion of an interval as the school understands it, and it gives a different reading of the verse that uses the word. The next two concept sections build on this. The third section closes the book.

## There was no interval: the chain by name

Salih refuses the very notion of an interval. He does not refuse it as a sentiment. He refuses it by reciting the names.

"What interval was there after the community of Abraham, when Allah sent after him Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Jonah, Shu'ayb, leading to Moses? At that time He revealed to them the commands for good action, calling to the community of Abraham, upholding prayer and giving charity, until He sent down the Torah upon Moses." The list is dense. Each name is a prophet the audience accepts. Between Abraham and Moses there is no empty span in which Allah's call was withdrawn from the earth. There is a chain of named witnesses, each calling to the community of Abraham, each upholding the prayer, each giving charity, until the next major book is sent down.

He does the same for the next span. "What interval was there after Moses, before the Gospel? After Moses Allah sent Joshua son of Nun, Elijah, David, Solomon, Saul, Zechariah, and John, leading up to Jesus. They all upheld the judgment of the Torah, worshiping by it and reciting its psalms, until He sent down the Gospel upon Jesus." Again no empty span. Again a chain of names. Again each link upholds the previous book until the next book arrives. The pattern is not interrupted; what changes are the books, not the continuity of the witnesses.

Then Salih takes the verse that uses the disputed word and reads it precisely. "O People of the Scripture, there has come to you Our Messenger to make clear to you after a period of messengers" (chapter 5, verse 19; translation by M. A. S. Abdel Haleem, *The Qur'an*, Oxford World's Classics, 2004, p. 71). The translation that the school takes as an absence of messengers is, Salih says, an account of a period of fear. "In this verse, the interval refers to the public manifestation of the divine call after a period of fear, when they were calling people to Allah in secret. The earth is never without a just witness from Allah for the blink of an eye, either publicly and openly, or in fear and concealment."

The reading is the hinge of the whole second half of the episode. The interval, in this hearing, is not the absence of the witness from the world. It is the absence of the public manifestation of the call. The witness is there. The call is being made. The two are simply being made in concealment because of fear, and then, when the conditions allow, the call becomes public again. The earth, Salih says, is never without a just witness from Allah for the blink of an eye.

The author is not innovating this doctrine. He is citing a witness chain backward into a sermon his audience already recognizes as authoritative. The Commander of the Faithful, in a passage preserved in the eleventh-century compilation of his sermons and letters known in English as *The Peak of Eloquence*, taught the same teaching in nearly the same words: "Yes, O Allah, the earth is never devoid of one who establishes the proof of Allah, whether openly and renowned, or fearful and concealed, lest the proofs and clear signs of Allah be invalidated" (sermon 147; translation by William Chittick, *A Shi'ite Anthology*, State University of New York Press, 1981, pp. 68–70). The two formulations agree term for term: the proof is never withdrawn; only its visibility changes; concealment under fear is itself the prophetic posture, not an interruption of it.

## Why the friends of Allah are in concealment

Abu Malik now asks the question the new reading invites. Why, then, are they hidden in our own time? Salih answers with the example of Moses. Moses fled. He hid from the people of Pharaoh, crossing into the land of Midian, into the city of the prophet Shu'ayb, because he feared for his life. The pattern is in the canon Abu Malik already accepts. The prophet of Allah, in danger of his life, did not stand on a corner to be killed. He withdrew, found refuge with another prophet's people, and waited.

Salih asks Abu Malik to consider the alternative. "Have you heard of any of Allah's just witnesses, the descendants of His prophets, who were murdered while persevering in their role?" "Yes," Abu Malik says, "a great many." Then which of them, Salih asks, would you want to reveal himself to the people of your time? A martyr resurrected after death? Or the one fleeing from you, while you seek to bring him to the death he is fleeing? "So Allah's just witness follows the accustomed way of His prophets: persevering under persecution, and remaining hidden until Allah judges between him and us." The concealment is not a failure of the divine economy. It is the divine economy operating as it has always operated under exactly these conditions.

The Quran itself preserves the original scene Salih is invoking. "And a man came running from the farthest end of the city. He said, 'Moses, the chiefs are taking counsel about you to slay you; so depart. Indeed, I am of those who counsel you well.' So he left the city, fearful and vigilant, saying, 'My Lord, save me from the wrongdoing people'" (chapter 28, verses 20–21; Abdel Haleem, Oxford World's Classics, 2004, p. 246). The prophet's withdrawal is not a lapse in the plan; it is the plan in its threatened mode. The modern historian of this tradition, Farhad Daftary, has documented at length how the same posture structured the early call across the centuries that produced the present book, leadership operating openly when conditions permitted, and through hidden representatives when they did not, never severing the chain but adjusting its visibility (*The Ismailis: Their History and Doctrines*, second edition, Cambridge University Press, 2007, pp. 87–128).

Then Salih names the typology of the three categories of people in relation to the hidden witnesses. It is one of the most pointed passages in the book.

"There is the relentless murderer, who never tires of hunting them down. Their deep-seated enemy, who bites his nails in rage and never forgets his hatred. And finally, those who dislike the enemies' actions and are sympathetic to the friends of Allah, even though they abandon them, who have no spiritual insight regarding faith, and cannot be relied upon to protect the secret of the imams. It is because of this third category that the friends of Allah have concealed themselves from you."

The shift in moral weight is what the line does. The murderers and the rageful enemies are the obvious cause. They are not, in this account, the operative cause. The operative cause is the sympathetic abandoner. The community member who feels for the friends of Allah, but does not stand by them; who would not lift a hand against them, but cannot be trusted with their secret; who agrees that what is being done to them is wrong, but is not present when it matters. It is for this third category, and not for the first two, that the friends of Allah have hidden. The diagnosis lands inside Abu Malik's community, not outside it.

The French scholar Henry Corbin, who devoted decades to the philosophical analysis of this tradition, identified this third figure as the structural pivot of the whole doctrine of concealment, the one whose presence makes hiddenness a moral necessity rather than a divine punishment. The friends are not withdrawn by a sovereign decree alone; they are withdrawn by the conditions the community itself produces (*Cyclical Time and Ismaili Gnosis*, translated by Ralph Manheim and James Morris, Kegan Paul International, 1983, pp. 84–93). The author's pastoral move and the philosopher's structural reading land on the same insight from opposite directions.

## Repentance and the seal of the book

Salih then confronts Abu Malik with the complicity of the scholars who serve the oppressors. Those who "blacken a flag or fill an inkwell" for the murderers of Allah's witnesses share in the bloodshed. The legal scholar who passes opinions enabling the oppressors is worse than the murderer himself, "for were it not for the opinions of these legal scholars, the oppressors could not maintain their dominion over the foolish." The oppressor's strength is borrowed from the scholar's pen. Without the legitimation, the force collapses; without the force, the oppression cannot extend; without the extension, the killing of witnesses cannot continue. The scholar's pen sits earlier in the causal chain than the executioner's hand.

Salih cites the verses that name the wrong he is describing. "Do not incline toward those who do wrong, lest the Fire touch you" (chapter 11, verse 113). "Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you" (chapter 4, verse 59), warning that the wrongdoers had been falsely treated as "those in authority." "Fight those who reject Allah, even if they are close to you" (chapter 9, verse 123; all three from Abdel Haleem, Oxford World's Classics, 2004, pp. 144, 55, 130). The pattern of misuse is exact. Inclination toward wrongdoers was forbidden; it was performed. Obedience was due to the rightful authorities; it was given to the wrong ones. Struggle was due against the rejecters; it was avoided. "You willfully disobeyed the one Allah commanded you to obey, and willingly obeyed those you were commanded to struggle against, all while assuming you were among those doing good."

The Prophet had taught the inverse posture in a tradition preserved across the canonical ninth-century hadith collections of the Sunni mainstream and the early collections of the Shia tradition: "The best struggle is a word of justice spoken in the face of an oppressive ruler" (recorded in the collection of Abu Dawud, book 38, hadith 17, and parallel chains in the collections of Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah; cited in Wilferd Madelung, *The Succession to Muhammad: A Study of the Early Caliphate*, Cambridge University Press, 1997, p. 22). The scholar who fills the inkwell for the oppressor inverts exactly this ruling, substituting a word of legitimation where a word of justice was due.

Then Salih offers the path of return. It is brief and exact. "If you were all to help the friends of Allah, they would reveal themselves; and if you were to abandon their enemies, they would not be victorious." The concealment is reversible. The oppression is reversible. The means are within the community's reach. Help and abandonment are the two acts; the result follows.

Abu Malik acknowledges that this is the true description of his community, and asks how to find salvation. "With Allah," Salih replies. "Seek protection with Him and He will give you refuge; turn to Him in repentance and He will accept it; ask Him for the path of His friends and He will rescue you through your own righteous actions. For Allah does not do an atom's weight of wrong, and does not hasten to punish even those who hasten to disobey Him. He loves those who turn to Him in repentance." Abu Malik turns. He repents. He seeks the means to please Him.

Salih's eyes filled with tears at the mention of repentance, and he concluded his speech. He consulted his spiritual father, the scholar, about Abu Malik and his companions. The scholar gave the closing counsel. "You know your companions better than I. If you recognize good in them, guide them as you were guided. But if you are apprehensive, test them by ignoring them, not harshly, and order your father to treat them kindly during their period of trial. For the one truly seeking good will not remain hidden." So Abu Malik and his companions passed through their levels of trial until their affair was complete and they recognized their right guidance. It was Allah's friend among them who took care of their guidance. They returned to their people, warning them, and through them Allah guided a great many of His servants to His religion.

The source then says of its own work what no part of this discussion could improve. "This was not a fictitious story, but rather the confirmation of what Allah has commanded. For in it is the confirmation of the divine messengers, the signs of their trustees, and the proper behavior of those who are seeking." The book is to be read as a model. The arc the listener has just followed, scholar finds disciple, disciple meets the Sheikh, disciple returns home and converts his father, father and son convert Abu Malik, Abu Malik and his group convert their community, is offered as a replicable arc of the call from individual conversion to community-building. Each stage is a stage in the listener's own potential journey. The story does not end at its own ending; it points at the listener and asks where, in the arc, the listener is now.

The book closes with its seal. Praise be to Allah, in the beginning and at the end. May Allah's blessings be upon His Messenger, our master Muhammad, sent to His creatures as a bearer of good news and a warner; and upon his trustee, the Father of Imams; and upon the imams from the people of his house upon whom Allah has bestowed His favors, may He take from them all impurity and purify them entirely. And Allah is sufficient for us, the best of trustees and the best of supporters. There is no strategy and no strength except through Allah, the Exalted, the Powerful.

## Closing

The book ends as it began, with a quiet handing of guidance from one person to another, and with the same theology of intermediation it has spent its length defending. The chain was never broken; the interval was never an absence; the concealment is a familiar prophetic posture under threat; the responsibility for ending the concealment lies with the community itself; the path of return is open. Abu Malik repents. His companions pass through their trial. They return to their people. Through them Allah guides a great many of His servants to His religion. The seal is recited. The model is offered. The next listener is the listener now hearing this.
