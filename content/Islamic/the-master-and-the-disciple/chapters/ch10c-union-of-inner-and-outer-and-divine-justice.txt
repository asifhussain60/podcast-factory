# The Union of Inner and Outer, and Divine Justice in Acceptance

*Episode ten of* The Master and the Disciple. *The disciple has heard the inner of the inner. The cosmology has been completed; the formula la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah has been opened; the dreams of Joseph and of the king have been read into the same architecture. Now the urgent practical question. If the inner is everything, what becomes of the outer — of prayer, of fasting, of the obligations of shari'a the disciple has been carrying all his life? The scholar answers in three movements. First the union of inner and outer as a single living religion, where each alone is a corpse or a wraith. Then the pale faces of the true scholars — not asceticism but the consequence of vigilant attention to the next life. And finally the doctrine of divine justice in acceptance: the instrument of knowledge is the same in every chest, the difference is in what each seeker chooses to do with it.*

## Where this episode picks up

The disciple has just been carried through the inner meanings of words his community has been reciting all his life. He could be forgiven for asking whether the outer practices still matter, whether prayer and fasting are anything more than the husk that hides the kernel. He almost asks it. The scholar reads the question in his face and answers it before it is fully formed. The verse comes in: do not let the life of this physical world deceive you. The scholar notices the precise phrasing — Allah did not say "do not let this physical world deceive you" but "do not let the life of this physical world deceive you." Because life, the scholar says, has four meanings: physical life in this inferior world and its passing away; spiritual life in the superior world and its lasting eternally. On the Day of Judgment, the Quran promises, man will say, "I wish I had prepared for my life" — meaning the eternal one. There is life through knowledge of the world's outer aspect, the lowest form. And life through knowledge of its inner aspect, which is the basis for true eternal life. Physical knowledge is forgotten at death; the soul takes with it only the spiritual understanding of physical realities. So the disciple's question is correctly framed: what makes a person carry the inner into the eternal? And the answer is the union, not the choice between.

## The union of inner and outer

The scholar then ranks the cases the disciple needs to hold in mind. The first is the seeker who has discovered the inner but neglects the outer obligations. He knows the meanings; he no longer prays, no longer fasts. The scholar's verdict is severe and his image precise. Such a person is in a terrible state. The inner cannot subsist when the protective outer is abandoned. It is like fruit whose covering is peeled before ripening — it rots. It is like a body whose limbs are cut — the spirit cannot remain in it. The seeker who keeps the inner without the outer the scholar calls fasiq, using the Arabic word in its root sense — fisq is the word used when something rots and turns ugly. Such a person becomes an enemy of Allah's friends and can never attain their company, for he has severed what Allah commanded to be joined.

The second case is the one who knows and upholds the outer but remains ignorant of the inner. The disciple might expect the scholar to call this case acceptable, since at least the outer is intact. The scholar says the opposite. This is the worst station of all. Like a body with no spirit breathed into it, the scholar says — numbered among the dead. The Arabic word the scholar reaches for is the same word the disciple has heard used for unbelievers — kafir — and the scholar surfaces its root meaning: the very same root that is used for a farmer who hides the seed under the ground. The one who knows only the outer hides the inner under the visible the way the farmer hides the seed under the soil — and the seed of meaning cannot grow under that covering because the covering has no opening.

And then the third case, which is the doctrine. Only through the union of both — the inner spiritual dimension and the outer obligations of shari'a — can a person succeed in the afterlife and be a person of faith, a mu'min. For such a person, the scholar adds with a small turn that surprises the disciple, this physical world becomes licit, and they are most deserving of it. The world is not refused by the believer; it is repossessed. The inner of the inner does not subtract from the daily life; it makes the daily life finally legible. The seeker who keeps both is the one who has the right to the world's good things because he sees them inside their meaning.

## The pallor of the true scholars

The disciple asks the question every student of contemplatives eventually asks. Why do the true scholars look pale and weak? Why do they appear to reject this world, when they are not sick? The scholar, the source records, wept before answering. Then he explained. Those people reflected on their souls with the true light of life. They were certain of the divine promise and threat. So they hastened to act while they had time. Their bodies grew weak from fasting. Their complexions paled from vigilant nights of prayer. They were distracted from luxury by reflection — al-fikr, the deep practice of thinking through what one knows — and engrossed in the sweetness of remembrance of Allah — dhikr Allah, the practice of holding the divine name in the heart at all times. The pallor is not a fast against the world. It is a side effect of attention to the next world.

The scholar gives one careful line that closes the disciple's misreading. "They rejected this physical world only because they were distracted from it by their concern for the next — not because they considered it forbidden." The body grew thin not as an ascetic feat but as the natural consequence of where the mind was. The world was not refused; the mind was occupied elsewhere, and the body recorded where the mind had been. The disciple is being given a gentle correction to two popular pictures at once: that the world must be hated to be transcended, or that the body must be punished to be purified. Neither. The scholar's pallor is the pallor of a long vigil for what matters more than sleep.

## The identical instrument and divine justice

The disciple then asks the deeper question — about the origin of creatures and why their opinions differ. The scholar's answer is one of the most exact moves in the dialogue. Bodies vary in language and color, he says, as a sign of Allah's power, so people may recognize one another. But the internal and external instruments and faculties of human beings are created identically. No scholar has three eyes. No ignorant person has only one. There is equality, the scholar says, in the creation of the physical instruments. And since the external design is identical, the inner instruments of will, of intellect, and of reasoning are identical too — by the pattern of divine justice. "For Allah would not treat His creatures justly in their outer aspect and unjustly in their inner aspects. He has not placed two hearts in the scholar's chest, nor forbidden any means of understanding for the ignorant. The instrument of knowledge is one."

It is by this single instrument, the scholar says, that Allah established His argument against the ones who do not use it. He provided what they failed to employ — as in the verse, "They have hearts but do not understand with them; eyes but do not see; ears but do not hear." The argument is not that the ignorant lacked the instrument and so are not at fault. It is that they had the instrument and did not use it. The difference between the scholar and the riffraff is not the equipment. It is the use.

The difference in passions and factions, then, the scholar continues, came not from the instrument but from the root being corrupted. All came from Adam, and Adam from dust. But through marriage they left behind the divine way — al-sunna, the inherited pattern; their progeny abandoned the divine creed and followed paths that led them astray. Once the root is corrupted, the branches are soon ruined. All were alike in seeking ease for their bodies and respect for their souls. But they differed in reaching the unified goal — like people shooting arrows at one target, alike in intention and instrument, yet differing in actually hitting it. One group accepted the messengers' promise of Allah's eternal recompense, though it was rare in this world. The other chose this world's fleeting but readily available pleasures. "Each one receives from Him what he seeks."

As people of this world have ranks, the scholar adds — kings and those who serve them — so the people of religion have ranks. And Allah, in His mercy, accepts from the weak whatever they can perform in good faith. He imposed the duty of knowledge and action on every person according to their capability. And He then joins them, in His justice, with the people of the most perfect faith — "Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be with the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous." The required effort, the scholar says, is acquiring knowledge and acting upon it to the furthest extent of one's capabilities. Sincerity and faithfulness are Allah's two gardens; none will be saved except the sincere and faithful. The foundation of action is obedience — carrying out all obligations and abstaining from all that is prohibited, which the scholar names as the zakat of the body, zakat al-abdan, that purifies the rest — staying in gratitude, and treating people as you would want them to treat you. And when the disciple presses on allowance under necessity, the scholar cites the verse — "Whoever is forced by necessity, neither desiring the forbidden nor willingly disobeying, there is no sin upon him" — and recalls the woman in the time of the second caliph who, thirsting for three days and fearing for her children, complied with a man's condition for water; Omar ordered her stoned, but the Father of Imams declared her free of sin by exactly this verse. Mercy under necessity is not a loophole; it is the verse itself.

## Closing

Three movements, one doctrine. The union of inner and outer as a single living religion, where each alone is fruit that rots or a body without spirit. The pale faces of the true scholars as a record of vigilant attention rather than punishment of the world. And the divine justice that places the same instrument of knowledge in every chest and judges each by what they did with it. The disciple has been told that the inner does not cancel his daily life. It makes his daily life finally a doctrine. The next episode will turn from this framing to the most concrete question the disciple can ask — what to do with his possessions, his money, his goods — as the dialogue prepares to part the scholar from his student and send the student toward the master beyond the master.
