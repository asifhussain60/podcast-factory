# The Master and the Disciple — Unified Reading Text

*A merged content layer of ⟪ar:کتاب العالم و الغلام⟫ (The Book of the Master and the Boy), the timeless story of a seeker's quest for true knowledge and the process through which he gradually achieved it. It is written by ⟪ar:سیدنا جعفر بن منصور الیمن قدس اللہ روحہ⟫, a very high-ranking ⟪ar:داعی⟫. He was a ⟪ar:باب الابواب⟫ — a master of ⟪ar:تاویل⟫ (esoteric interpretation). Through this book he guides us to the path of the friends of Allah — chosen people who possess the spiritual knowledge that makes them spiritually eternal. He explains how these friends of Allah receive this divine trust from their Lord, and how they fulfill their responsibility of transmitting this divine knowledge to their fellow human beings. He uses the Quran and HADEES as the source for all his arguments. What is most remarkable about this book is that it acts as a map — a guide that each of us will follow and interpret in our own way, in the light of our own individual experiences and insight; so what one reader draws from it will be very different from what another draws from it.*

*The book is a series of dramatic dialogues between the Master ⟪ar:العالم⟫ and his young disciple ⟪ar:غلام⟫. The word ⟪ar:غلام⟫ in Arabic refers to a young boy under the guardianship or supervision of an adult — it is NOT the Urdu ⟪ar:غلام⟫ meaning a slave; the Arabic word for slave is ⟪ar:عبد⟫. The dialogue unfolds in a very Aristotelian manner — breaking logic down into smaller parts and addressing each part individually.*

---

## Part One — The True Sources of Knowledge

*The opening of the story establishes the importance of gratitude and etiquette in seeking knowledge from the correct sources. It introduces the Master's design-of-creation sermon, the search for a worthy disciple, the proper etiquette of learning, and the covenant of allegiance that makes all religious action sacred.*

### Comportment, Gratitude, and the Three Thanks

It has reached us that a number of the truly faithful (⟪ar:مؤمنین⟫) and a group among those who call people to the true religion (⟪ar:دعاۃ الدین⟫) said to one of their scholars: "You have liberated us by helping us learn about an affair of great importance, and for that we feel obligated to express our gratitude for three reasons:

- ⟪ar:شکرک فیما دعوتنا الیہ⟫ — Our gratitude for your having called us to the true religion (⟪ar:دین⟫);
- ⟪ar:وشکر العِلْم الذی دللتنا علیہ⟫ — Our gratitude for the knowledge (⟪ar:علم⟫) you pointed us towards;
- ⟪ar:وشکر العَمَل الذی أمرتنا بہ⟫ — Our gratitude for the religious practices (⟪ar:عمل⟫) you commanded us to perform.

"So inform us how we should express our gratitude for these three blessings. In addition:

- ⟪ar:ثم عَرَّفنا من حدود الدین⟫ — inform us about the rights and duties that are obligatory upon us as directed by the ordinances of religion (⟪ar:حدود دین⟫);
- ⟪ar:وما یجب علی الطالب فی مسألتہ⟫ — tell us about the obligations of a seeker in his mode of questioning — the proper etiquette for seeking knowledge;
- ⟪ar:وعلی المطلوب فی جوابہ⟫ — tell us about the obligations upon the scholar in his response — how one should impart his knowledge to the one who seeks it;
- ⟪ar:وعرَّفنا من مذاھب الصالحین و آداب الطالبین⟫ — also inform us about the ways of the righteous and the proper behavior (⟪ar:آداب⟫) of the seekers."

At the very outset the author highlights two extremely important concepts: ⟪ar:شکر⟫ (gratitude) and ⟪ar:ادب⟫ (comportment). Comportment is the way in which you carry yourself in relation to someone or something. The force that opposes these two principles is desire. This is why our religion is so focused on ⟪ar:ایثار⟫ — helping others — because it draws people out of the traps of egocentricity. We are designed as ⟪ar:عبد⟫ for the task of ⟪ar:عبودیۃ⟫ — servants created for the task of service. Servitude is what makes life enjoyable as an experience, servitude to Allah being the greatest of all delights.

⟪ar:ادب⟫ is, in its essence, prioritizing someone else over the self. It is not merely "good manners." It is the force that opposes desire, the disposition that structures every act of worship as a form of self-surrender. So when one considers even the ⟪ar:آداب⟫ of eating, the whole process is designed to respect and prioritize that sustenance over the self — to humble the self in relation to the blessing of food. Rasul Allah (Salallahu alayhi wa aalihee wa sallam) said: "Sit before your food like a slave sits before its master" — honor it by covering your head, by using your right hand — because without that food we would be reduced to animals.

The Master replied: "Now the affair to which I invite you is the religion that Allah has bestowed upon his servants as an honor — a religion he has perfected for them and through which he honors whoever responds to Him.

⟪ar:فجعل لکل أول منہ آخر ؛ و لکل آخر منہ غایۃ ؛ و لکل غایۃٍ نھایۃ ؛ لا یبلغ أِمْدَھا⟫

"So for every beginning (⟪ar:أول⟫) he has placed within it an end (⟪ar:آخر⟫); and for every end he designed a goal (⟪ar:غایۃ⟫); and each goal has a limit (⟪ar:نھایۃ⟫) whose full extent cannot be attained. These are the way-stations (⟪ar:منازل⟫) of the people of true understanding. The key to success is the constant remembrance of Allah (⟪ar:ذکر اللہ⟫).

⟪ar:وأولھا بلوی، وأوسطھا ھدی و آخرھا تقوی⟫

"Their initial level is trial (⟪ar:بلوی⟫); their middle state is right guidance (⟪ar:ھدی⟫); and their final stage is piety (⟪ar:تقوی⟫)."

This is the three-stage spiritual path: Balwā → Hudā → Taqwā. The initial stage is ⟪ar:بلوی⟫, trial, because the very customized ⟪ar:نفس⟫ rebels — it gets in the way like a wild horse refusing to be tamed, pushing you toward what you want rather than what Allah wants. Once you succeed in taming this wild horse of the self, the next stage is ⟪ar:ھدی⟫: you find guidance and begin experiencing its benefits. The final stage is ⟪ar:تقوی⟫: a constant, deep-rooted awareness of the presence of Allah in your life. The tool that helps you persevere through these three stages is ⟪ar:ذکر اللہ⟫, the remembrance of Allah.

The Master continued: "Whoever seeks to learn the basics of Allah's affairs with the truest of intentions, and then discovers the ranks of the divinely determined (⟪ar:حدود الدین المقدور⟫), has sought guidance from the correct sources and has set upon it in the best possible manner. As for showing gratitude to the scholar, that is through obeying him — not a verbal thank-you, but obedience. As for gratitude for the knowledge, it is turning it into action (⟪ar:عمل⟫) and calling others to it. And the gratitude for the right practice is striving patiently (⟪ar:صبر⟫) in it and being consistent with it." The spiritual guide is like the doctor who prescribes a medication for an illness; praising the doctor will not cure you — what cures you is doing what he prescribes, consistently, for the prescribed duration.

These three thanks form the book's recurring three-part template: the call (da'wa) answered by obedience to the teacher; the knowledge answered by action; the action answered by perseverance.

### The Story of the Scholar and the Search for a Disciple

The author then narrates the story of a man from Persia who, though he had a rich heart, a brilliant intellect, and a decent education, was subject to the trials of spiritual ignorance, his ignorance outweighing his knowledge. He was restless in the burning heat of his thirst for knowledge, assuming the glimmering of mirages to be the reflection of water, until he reached it only to find it nothing — yet there he found Allah, and paid Him his account in full. Then Allah honored his abode and removed the veil from his intellect through a teacher who found him astray and guided him; who found him in need and satisfied him with knowledge. Thus he became one of the great scholars of Sinai, an angelic dweller in the well-populated temple (⟪ar:بیت المعمور⟫).

When his guidance was completed, he felt duty-bound to show gratitude to his Master and to exert himself for his Lord. He used to hear his spiritual father repeat a proverb that held the rank of soul to his body: *ان افضل الحسنات أحیا الأموات* — "The most excellent of deeds is giving life to the dead" — meaning imparting knowledge to the ignorant, for the ignorant is like a corpse. So he said to himself: "I used to be dead, and my master gave me life; I was ignorant, and he gave me knowledge. I am not the first to be ignorant, nor the last — so out of gratitude I must pass on this divine trust to those who come after me, just as those before me handed it down to me. For the origin of this affair is from Allah, and it reached me only through its many intermediaries (⟪ar:اسباب⟫), the first passing it on to the second, the second to the third,

⟪ar:حتی اذا ھوی من الملاء الاعلی الی الخلائق من اھل الدنیا⟫

"until it descended from the heavenly host to the creatures of this lower world.

⟪ar:ثم خرق الحجاب وتدافعتہ الابواب الی ان وصل الیَّ، فأکون أنا غایتہ ومُنتھاہُ؟⟫

"After that, did all this knowledge rip through the veil and pass through the intermediaries until it reached me, only so that I could be its final goal and ultimate end? No! Those who transmitted it before me are more deserving of precedence — in fact I myself am part of what they earned by teaching me. And their precedence does not relieve me from having to act:

⟪ar:وأن الآخر أمرہ فی الحکم مثل الاول⟫

"my final duty is similar to my initial duty."

So he traveled towards his Lord, leaving his people and possessions behind, passing among the Arab and non-Arab tribes, scrutinizing their faces for signs of spiritual aptitude and posing religious questions — but he found no response and met no seeker, until he reached the furthest part of the ⟪ar:جزیرۃ⟫. This is a teaching on discernment: the scholar did not just share his knowledge with anyone. He searched for the one who deserved it, who had the right desire — a burning desire — and the right attitude, aptitude, and capacity to bear that knowledge. Otherwise that knowledge would not only be wasted but could have an adverse effect.

There he entered a city where nobody knew him, and noticed a group of townspeople discussing religion without any guidance, recklessly following the inclinations of their passions. He sat nearby, out of sight, listening carefully and analyzing which of them was closer to the right path. Then he said to himself: "These people are more deserving of life — this spiritual knowledge — for three reasons: first, the true divine argument (⟪ar:حجۃ⟫) has reached them; second, they belong to the people of my community (⟪ar:الملۃ⟫); and third, they are closer to the right path by their keen interest in religion and the fact that they are inquiring about it.

⟪ar:ان الطالب للشیء أوشک أن یلقاہ⟫

"Indeed the person who is already searching for something is much closer to finding it."

When those people completed their discussion, they turned to him and asked who he was. The scholar had patiently waited for them to finish — he did not interrupt to correct them. He said he was a servant of Allah and one of the residents of His sanctuary; that his business — the acquisition of knowledge — was complete, and that he was now looking for work — the spreading of that acquired knowledge. They tested him about commendable and reprehensible speech, and he answered that commendable speech is that which is true: "speech whose origin comes from Allah and through which people are called towards Allah" — the word used being ⟪ar:صوابہ⟫ with a ⟪ar:ص⟫, meaning accurate — while reprehensible speech is "that based on the passions of the soul, used to call people to something other than guidance." He added: "I have no words of my own, for I am following the words of another." Recognizing the truth in his answers, they asked him to become "a gateway for us, through which Allah may send His loving kindness for us."

### Glorifying Allah — The Design of Creation

The scholar stood, faced the attentive group, and began his glorification of Allah:

"Indeed! He created with His power, then arranged everything perfectly. He determined a path through His generosity, then guided his creation towards it. He brought forth the paths of the pastureland (⟪ar:شوارع المرعی⟫), then made its outer form a dried-up brush (⟪ar:ظاہرہ غثا⟫) and its inner aspect broad in scope and content (⟪ar:وباطنہ أحوی⟫). His knowledge does not exist outwardly so that the unworthy could possess and alter it. Instead his knowledge is benevolent and well-protected (⟪ar:کریم مغمود⟫) because of its excellence; well concealed, its secrets guarded for those worthy of them. Its dignity (⟪ar:شرف⟫) is the splendid robe of guidance, and its perfection is the garment of piety (⟪ar:لباس التقوی⟫). It is marked out by the light of lights (⟪ar:نور الانوار⟫), entrusted safely in the hearts of the best and the chosen ones — the truly pious, the guardians, the protectors, the generous and the recorders. This knowledge is a trust which they safely pass on, and they do not transgress the divine ordinances. He honored his knowledge by hiding it and making it inward (⟪ar:باطن⟫), protected it by keeping it secret, veiled it with His obligations, and crowned it with His covenants."

This is the blueprint of Allah's design. He took all the spiritual realities and hid them in the shell of the exterior, so that everything we see in this world — the sky, the earth, the sun, the moon, the stars, the changing seasons, the winds, the oceans, the rivers — represents inner spiritual realities. He veiled the spiritual realities within physical creations, thereby immortalizing them: people will come and go, generations and entire nations will come and go, but these external and internal realities will live forever. The ẓāhir/bāṭin distinction is therefore not merely a textual or hermeneutical layer; it is a deliberate cosmic design principle, a mechanism of preservation. As the Master puts it: He veiled the spiritual realities within physical creations, "therefore immortalizing them." And he made these chosen ones — the trustees of his knowledge — the ones to unveil these realities; none of us can decipher these codes without them.

"For it cannot be reached by the aspirations of those who rely on the strength of their intellects (⟪ar:فلا تنالہ ھمم المتفکرین⟫), nor attained by those scheming for it (⟪ar:ولا تبلغہ فکر المحتالین⟫). Its outer aspect is a reminder and a sign for the rightly guided, while its inner aspect is a sweet drink gushing forth. His lofty heavens come down close, his earth is paved for those who inquire about Him, his guiding stars are organized for those who seek Him, and he has signs (⟪ar:آیات⟫) whose wonders follow each other in succession, pointing to Him through their symbols. His accustomed way is preferment (⟪ar:سیرتہ تفضیل⟫) — preferring the righteous over the unrighteous; his method is approached through distinguished ranks (⟪ar:ومنھاجہ تفصیل⟫). He cannot be accessed without his intermediaries; this is why we have the concept of ⟪ar:وصیلۃ⟫ in everything. He has made his ways (⟪ar:شریعۃ⟫) a guide and a path leading to good, with its outer aspect a protective shell and a sign pointing toward its inner dimensions."

As part of His design, Allah created this universe with all its components representing the dimensions of spirituality. He chose certain people based on their piety and capability and made them custodians of the knowledge of these spiritual realities — *و علم آدم اسماء کلھا*, "And Allah taught Adam all the names." Then, in His perfect order, He created our spiritual sustenance first, identifying His chosen people and giving them knowledge of everything, and only then created us with a need — giving us an intellect designed to seek things out, and creating us ignorant so that we would realize our need. So when our ignorance forces our intellects toward the acquisition of knowledge, these fountainheads of knowledge are already in place. "Blessed is the One Who placed the night and the day in succession, for those who reflect and those who wish to be grateful. May Allah's blessings be on the one He has specially chosen from His servants, and made a warner (⟪ar:نذیر⟫) for all the worlds."

Then he said: "Be mindful of Allah like the one who fears Him; and fear Him like the one who is hoping to please Him. Seek knowledge and you will learn; act according to that knowledge and you will receive Allah's loving mercy." His eyes overflowed with tears, and he broke off and bid the group farewell. The intensity of his speech overcame everyone. A young man from the group followed him — the youngest, yet of the best character, whose intellect was complete and whose heart had been awakened by reflection. This young man's father was a great Sheikh among the most distinguished of the Arabs by lineage, and one of the most devoted supporters of the people of his religion — a rival community that had rejected the Imam. With the scholar's permission the young man accompanied him to his inn, where the scholar ordered food, and after they ate they began to talk.

### How to Seek Knowledge — The Etiquette of Learning

The young man said: "⟪ar:یا حکیم⟫ O wise man! You spoke and we listened. You preached with perfect eloquence; you adorned religion with the most beautiful ornaments; you motivated us with the most beautiful incentives; you called us through the most eminent responsibilities, forcing our intellects to inquire about all that has struck our hearts. You are like a tree: its roots firm, its splendor towering over the worlds, its fruits hanging low for those who desire them. So I ask you to become one of those blessed branches for me." This was a perfect demonstration of ⟪ar:ادب⟫ — without flattery, for everything the young man recognized was actually present in the scholar's speech. Only after this ⟪ar:تمھید⟫ did he come to his question: "You said that our minds are forced by their ignorance to seek knowledge; now my mind is the most needy of all — so is there any way to life for me?"

The scholar replied: "Surely the One who created by his power did not create without purpose, nor did he create them fully knowledgeable, nor leave them wandering in ignorance. So he established a way out for them by means of an instrument — an intellect — around which he shaped their constitution. He made their hearing, their sight, and their hearts openings to accept the light of his knowledge, and by these same instruments he completed his argument for them."

The components of that instrument are ⟪ar:ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ⟫ — Hearing, Sight, and the Heart. This is why numerous verses of the Quran mention these three together:

*The Sovereignty [67:23]* ⟪ar:قُلْ هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنشَأَكُمْ وَجَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ ۖ قَلِيلًۭا مَّا تَشْكُرُونَ⟫ — "Say: it is He Who has created you, and endowed you with hearing, sight, and hearts. Very few of you are grateful for these gifts."

*The Bee [16:108]* ⟪ar:أُو۟لَٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ طَبَعَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ وَسَمْعِهِمْ وَأَبْصَرِهِمْ ۖ وَأُو۟لَٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْغَفِلُونَ⟫ — "They are those upon whose hearts, hearing and sight Allah has set a seal; and they are the heedless."

*The Cow [2:7]* ⟪ar:خَتَمَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ وَعَلَىٰ سَمْعِهِمْ ۖ وَعَلَىٰٓ أَبْصَرِهِمْ غِشَوَةٌۭ ۖ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ عَظِيمٌۭ⟫ — "Allah has set a seal on their hearts and on their hearings, and on their eyes there is a covering; theirs will be a great torment."

*The Children of Israel [17:36]* ⟪ar:إِنَّ ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْبَصَرَ وَٱلْفُؤَادَ كُلُّ أُو۟لَٓئِكَ كَانَ عَنْهُ مَسْـُٔولًۭا⟫ — "Indeed the hearing, the sight, and the heart — of each of those you will be questioned."

"He then placed the treasures of His wisdom (⟪ar:کنوز الحکمۃ⟫) and the specific characteristics of His will (⟪ar:خصائص مشئیۃ⟫) in the pure, chosen, and elite of his creation. He gave them the responsibility of worshiping Him and calling others, while imposing upon people the duty of responding to that call by using their instruments — so that He may distinguish the wicked from the good." He married our needs with His provision: He set up His chosen ranks — the Imams, the ⟪ar:دعات⟫ — as providers, created us with a need for that provision, and gave us the intellect to fulfill it. "Therefore there is no argument against Allah after sending his messengers, nor any excuse for straying after the right path has been clearly pointed out."

The young man begged to become his servant, and the scholar answered: "Surely Allah did not create his creatures as fully grown adults or scholars. He created them as infants — small and ignorant — so that they grow big after having been small, and come to know after having been ignorant. Their smallness is a precondition for their greatness, and their ignorance is a sign pointing toward knowledge. The same is true of spiritual knowledge: one can only attain its essence on the basis of preceding, lower forms of knowledge, which the hearts can quickly assimilate. That is Allah's accustomed way (⟪ar:سنۃ⟫) regarding His servants." Just as one cannot feed ⟪ar:بریانی⟫ to a newborn — for all it can do is suck milk — most of us are spiritual infants who must first learn history, then ⟪ar:شریعۃ⟫, then ⟪ar:تاویل⟫, before reaching ⟪ar:حقائق⟫. (Note here a key usage that runs throughout: ⟪ar:سنۃ⟫ — sunna — is used to mean Allah's *accustomed way*, the recurring divine pattern embedded in creation, not merely prophetic practice or a jurisprudential category. It allows the Master to speak of "the sunna of pairs" in cosmology and "the sunna of sending messengers" as one and the same divine habit.)

The young man said: "Indeed I am an infant in relation to what I wish to achieve, so raise and educate me." The scholar replied that compassion alone would not bring him closer without his own working for it: "For just as every word has a proper employment (⟪ar:تصریف⟫) that can only be accepted if it is sincere and true, similarly knowledge is of no benefit until it has been put into action, and action is of no benefit until it is accepted by Allah:

⟪ar:العلم لا ینتفع بہ حتی یستعمل، والعمل لا ینتفع بہ حتی یقبل⟫

"And the only thing Allah accepts is sincerity." He then advised on how to acquire knowledge: "Do not seek knowledge impatiently, and do not dismiss it out of negligence. Nor should you accept it out of blind imitation without a convincing argument — for then it would slip out of your heart at the first onset of doubt. But do not deny an argument once it has been firmly established, or you will become the pleader for all that is false. And do not stop inquiring about the knowledge you have attained, or your judgment will become sterile. You must trust me without prejudice, make every effort to understand without confusion, seek the path as you seek the truth, and constantly ask Allah's help in your endeavor."

### Allegiance to the Imam — The Covenant That Makes Religion Sacred

When the young man asked about the revealed paths of righteous acts, the scholar first imposed five conditions on him: do not neglect anything entrusted to you, nor take anything given to you lightly; do not conceal anything when questioned; do not come looking for him whenever you have a question; do not ask about anything until he himself brings it up; and do not mention him to your father.

The condition about the father was the heaviest. The young man's father was a well-known leader of his tribe and a strong supporter of a rival community that had rejected the source of true knowledge — the Imam. This is the political stake of the entire story: the disciple is the son of a sectarian leader who has rejected the Imam, and any premature disclosure of his da'wa could destroy the mission. The scholar instructed him not to move away from his father — for that would invite suspicion — but to stay with him, respect him, please him where possible, while protecting his innermost beliefs and keeping his spiritual concerns secret. "Indeed Allah will protect you from his, and other people's, hostility."

The scholar and the young man met occasionally over a period of time. The young man kept performing everything assigned to him from the ⟪ar:شریعۃ⟫, though without the underlying knowledge behind those actions, not even knowing the scholar's residence. This was his period of trial, and when his merit and perseverance became clear, a meeting was arranged. The young man prostrated himself in gratitude, and the scholar gently corrected him: gratitude before the deed gives rise to suspicion of insincerity. Then he prepared to teach.

"There is a key to religion," the scholar began, "which renders it either sacred or profane, like the difference between marriage and fornication." Both are the same act; what differentiates ⟪ar:حلال⟫ from ⟪ar:حرام⟫ is the formality of ⟪ar:نکاح⟫ — blessing it with Allah's name. It is the same animal we eat, but the ⟪ar:ذبیحۃ⟫, blessing it with Allah's name, makes it ⟪ar:حلال⟫. "That key," he said, "is the pact of Allah (⟪ar:عہد اللہ⟫), which confirms His rights, encompasses His obligations and safeguards His friends. It is the rope of Allah upon His earth and His guarantee of security among His creation."

He then took the oath of allegiance (⟪ar:عہد شریف⟫) of the young man, for without it all this knowledge of ⟪ar:تاویل⟫ would be ⟪ar:حرام⟫. This is the mechanism of walāyah — the covenant (⟪ar:عہد⟫) that makes religious action effective. True knowledge belongs to the Imam; accepting his walāyah is like paying the owner his due, while using that knowledge without the walāyah is theft — and stolen food is haram regardless of how halal the animal is. It is told of Imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq that a man stole five apples and gave them to charity, calculating that fifty rewards minus five sins left him forty-five net rewards. The Imam corrected him: "When you stole those apples you earned five sins; and when you gave them to the old lady you gained another five sins" — because he had propagated the haram to another person. Total: ten sins, zero rewards. This is why acts of piety performed without walāyah are ineffective: the act without its covenant is stolen, and what is stolen cannot be sanctified.

This is also why the title of the book is itself a teaching. Sharīʿa is a graduated discipline: ⟪ar:طہارۃ⟫ and ⟪ar:صلوۃ⟫ five times daily, ⟪ar:صوم⟫ one month yearly, ⟪ar:زکوۃ⟫ once yearly, ⟪ar:حج⟫ once a lifetime, and walāyah every breath. Sharīʿa prepares us for knowledge; it creates discipline within us. That is where the word "disciple" comes from — disciple from discipline, and discipline from Sharīʿa. The disciple of this book becomes the disciple precisely by submitting to that graduated discipline under the covenant.

So the scholar recited the oath, and the young man repeated it and bound himself to it, his eyes streaming with tears, until he knew with certainty that from that moment on he was a member of the party of Allah and the party of Allah's friends.

## Part Two — Spiritual Symbols: The Architecture of Creation

*Having taken the covenant, the disciple is taught the architecture of creation with mathematical precision: the root-principles, the 7-and-12 schema, the Bismillah seal, the spiritual hierarchy mirrored in the heavens and the earth, and the supreme symbol of Air that points to the Incomparable.*

### The Root Principles — From Spiritual Luminescence to the Seven

The first thing the scholar explained were the first principles of the manifest intermediaries (⟪ar:الاسباب الظاھرۃ⟫) and the beginning of their creation:

- ⟪ar:أن اللہ مبدع الاشیا و مَکَوْنَھا⟫ — Indeed Allah is the Originator of things and the One who gave them existence.
- ⟪ar:خلقُھا و فَأبْتَدَأھَا⟫ — He created them and then initiated them without a template, such that no prior entity served as a model for the later.
- ⟪ar:بل ھو أول کل أول و وارث کل کائن⟫ — Instead, He is the first of every first and the inheritor of all existence.
- He initiated His creation from a Light, with three words branching from that Light: the first was His will (⟪ar:ارادۃ⟫); from the will derived His command (⟪ar:الأمر⟫); from the command came His saying "Be!" so it became (⟪ar:کن فیکون⟫).

⟪ar:فکان أول الخلق أرادۃ أمر بقول⟫ — So the beginning of all creation was by Allah's will of a command, executed through His speech (⟪ar:أرادۃ، أمر بالقول⟫).

There is a movement here from subtlety to density: ⟪ar:ارادۃ⟫ (intention) led to ⟪ar:الامر⟫ (command, more dense), which led to ⟪ar:قول⟫ (speech, more dense still), each entity giving birth to the one that follows.

These first three primordial principles — ⟪ar:ارادۃ، امر، قول⟫ — are the Light, the ⟪ar:نور⟫, whose word has three letters. This ⟪ar:نور⟫ is *superior* to physical "light." Physical light is made up of photons; this primordial Nūr is pre-physical — call it "Spiritual Luminescence" to keep the two distinct. (The same distinction is sealed into the structure of Bismillāh: ⟪ar:ارادۃ⟫ is unique, like the single letter Alif, while ⟪ar:امر⟫ and ⟪ar:قول⟫ form a pair.)

From this Spiritual Luminescence, **air** was created — the first of the seven, singular, without any pair; mixing with everything and encompassing everything, but encompassed by none. Thus air symbolically points to the One who has no like unto Him: ⟪ar:لیس کمثلہ شئی⟫. Every other entity in this world has a pair — hot and cold, big and small, male and female, soul and body, heaven and earth, wet and dry — except air.

⟪ar:ثم خلق من تلک ثلاث الکلمات المآء والظلمۃ و النور⟫ — Next came the water (⟪ar:الما⟫), the darkness (⟪ar:ظلمۃ⟫), and the light (⟪ar:نور⟫) — this last being physical light, inferior to the Spiritual Luminescence.

⟪ar:ثم خلق من الماء دخاناً و خلق من الظلمۃ تکاثف الحماء و خلق من النور ناراً⟫ — Then He created from the water smoke (⟪ar:دخان⟫); from the darkness the thickness of mud (⟪ar:تکاثف الحما⟫); and from light, fire (⟪ar:النار⟫). These three were derived from the preceding three and became their pairs — water with smoke, light with fire, darkness with mud — and air, the seventh principle, governs them all.

Once creation was established on these seven principles, He shaped their outward forms: from smoke, the seven heavens and their signs; from the thickness of mud, the seven earths and their sustenance.

⟪ar:فرجعنا فی القول الی الکلمات الثلاث و حروفھن التی منھا خلق السبعۃ، وھی ارادۃ أمر بقول⟫ — Returning to the three words from which the seven were created — ⟪ar:ارادۃ أمر بقول⟫ — these comprise twelve letters.

⟪ar:وآیاتھا فی السماء اثنا عشر بروجاً و شواھدھا سبعۃ أملاک، و آیاتھا فی الارض اثنا عشر جزیرۃ و شواھدھا سبعۃ عشر ابحر⟫ — Their signs in the heaven are the 12 mansions of the Zodiac, their witnesses seven rulers (⟪ar:سبع املاک⟫); their signs on the earth are the 12 regions (⟪ar:جزیرۃ⟫), their witnesses the seven oceans.

So the command ⟪ar:کن فیکون⟫ comprises 7 letters, and ⟪ar:ارادۃ أمر بقول⟫ comprises 12 letters; everything that came into existence was therefore divided into sevens and twelves — 7 heavens, 7 continents, 7 oceans, 7 days; 12 zodiacs, 12 islands, 12 hours of day and 12 of night.

### The Bismillah Seal and the Spiritual Hierarchy

He then split the single piece of black mud with His light, separating the mixture, and revealed day and night as two distinct signs. He made the moon and planets the signs of night, and the sun's rays the sign of day; from both He created the revolving week of 7 days, with 12 hours pointing to each day and night. He made pairs of male and female — not merely masculine and feminine genders, but ranks of greater and lesser excellence: the one who influences and the one influenced. (Water influences smoke; a teacher influences students — regardless of physical gender; this is also why Allah refers to Himself as "male" in the Quran, because He holds ultimate influence and nothing can influence Him — it does not imply a gender.) "He made them symbols for his religion, and signs pointing to his infinite capabilities":

⟪ar:فدل بالاشیاء علی منشئھا ؛ ودل بالبریۃ علی باریھا⟫ — Because things (⟪ar:شئی⟫) point to the purpose of their creation (⟪ar:منشاء⟫), and therefore all creation points to its Creator.

"He separated them with His luminescence, originated them through His command, created them with His knowledge, and sealed them by their names, using the letters of ⟪ar:بسم اللہ الرحمن الرحیم⟫."

The Bismillah is a cryptographic seal that encodes the entire creation schema. A seal locks something in place and makes its content inaccessible to all but the intended recipient. The structure of ⟪ar:بسم اللہ الرحمن الرحیم⟫ mirrors creation exactly: it contains three names of Allah — "Allah," the noun, unique like His ⟪ar:ارادۃ⟫; and ⟪ar:الرحمن⟫ and ⟪ar:الرحیم⟫, a pair of attributes, like ⟪ar:امر⟫ and ⟪ar:قول⟫ — paralleling the three letters of ⟪ar:نور⟫. The letters of ⟪ar:بسم اللہ⟫ number 7, like ⟪ar:کن فیکون⟫, the 7 heavens, 7 continents, 7 oceans, 7 days; while ⟪ar:الرحمن الرحیم⟫ number 12, like the 12 zodiacs, 12 islands, and 12 hours within day and night. We recite Bismillah at the very beginning of any action — so it is fitting that Allah sealed His own first action with its structure, making these realities inaccessible to the unworthy: those who have rejected the source of true knowledge.

When He had perfected their shapes and arranged them in pairs, He purified for Himself abundant knowledge (⟪ar:علما کثیراً⟫) and an illuminating light (⟪ar:ونور مبینا⟫), and made that His religion.

⟪ar:ثم أکرمہ بالشرائع و الکتب و دل علیہ بالامثال و السنن⟫ — He ennobled it with the revealed paths and the books, and pointed to it through the symbols (⟪ar:امثال⟫) and recurring patterns (⟪ar:سنۃ⟫) of all creation.

⟪ar:ثم نصب لہ من خیار خلقہ أعلاماً و دلائل و حُکَّاماً⟫ — Then from the best of His creation He raised distinguished personalities, guiding figures, and wise judges.

⟪ar:فجعلھم بیوت نبوَّتہ و خزائن علمہ و حکمتہ وتراجمۃ وحیہ⟫ — He placed them in the houses of His prophethood, the treasures of His knowledge and wisdom, and made them the inspired translators of His revelation.

⟪ar:واستخلفھم فی ارضہ أدلۃ علی سبیل النجاۃ فیما ظھر وبطن⟫ — He made them His representatives on earth, a means of deliverance through the path of salvation, in both the outer and the inner aspects.

⟪ar:فمنھم الائمۃ و النطقاء و منھم الحجج و النقباء و منھم الدعاۃ الی الخیر، و العلماء⟫ — Among them are the Imams and the ⟪ar:نطقاء⟫; the ⟪ar:حجج⟫ (proofs) and ⟪ar:نقبا⟫ (chieftains); the ⟪ar:دعات⟫ who summon people to good; and the scholars (⟪ar:علماء⟫).

⟪ar:فھم فی منازلھم کنجوم السماء سمت بھم الاعمال وتناھت بھم الامثال⟫ — In their ranks they are like the stars of heaven; the actions of those they guide rise up to them, and the symbols of religion come to an end in them.

The hierarchy of the spiritual world thus has a clear structure, ranked by category and function: the ⟪ar:ناطق⟫ (the speaking prophet, who receives direct revelation) → the Wasi / Imam (who interprets, receiving esoteric inspiration) → the ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ / ⟪ar:باب⟫ (who condenses and distributes — twelve per Imam, one per island) → the ⟪ar:دعات⟫ (many per ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫, the "rivers and springs"). Each ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ has thirty ⟪ar:دعات⟫ beneath him, mirroring the thirty days of a month under the twelve months. This hierarchy is mirrored in the heavens:

⟪ar:فأمامھم کالشمس العظیمۃ فی الأنوار الممتنعۃ بحجبھا عن الابصار، فھی ظاھرہ و مثلہ⟫ — Their Imam is like the immensity of the sun among the heavenly lights; the sun is the symbol and outer aspect pointing to the Imam.

⟪ar:وحجتھم ، وباب امامھم کالقمر المنیر وھو ظاھرہ ومثلہ⟫ — His Gateway (⟪ar:باب⟫), who is his greatest proof, is like the illuminated moon, a symbol pointing to the gateway.

⟪ar:ودعاتھم کالنجوم الزواھر وھی ظواھرھم و أمثالھم⟫ — The shining stars are the symbols representing the ⟪ar:دعات⟫, who guide people to truth as stars guide travelers in the night.

Here the distinction between ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ (Proof) and ⟪ar:باب⟫ (Gateway) must be preserved, since the two are easily blurred: the Imam is the sun; the Bāb / Ḥujja al-Imām is the moon — his Gateway and greatest proof; the Duʿāt are the stars. Each Imam has twelve ⟪ar:حجج⟫ — one per region — while the Bāb is the single gateway to the Imam.

He then named the inner aspects of the seven heavens: the seven ⟪ar:نطقاء⟫, the possessors of determination among the divine messengers. These are ⟪ar:آدم، نوح، ابراھیم، موسی، عیسی، محمد، مھدی⟫ — the 7 ⟪ar:نطقاء⟫ symbolized by the 7 heavens of Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, and Moon. The inner aspect of the seven continents are the Imams of right guidance. Each ⟪ar:ناطق⟫ has twelve ⟪ar:نقباء⟫ (chieftains), symbolized by the twelve mansions (⟪ar:بروج⟫); each Imam has twelve ⟪ar:حجج⟫ symbolized by the twelve regions (⟪ar:جزائر⟫), one ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ per region; and each ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ has a number of ⟪ar:دعات⟫. Just as the physical sun governs days and nights, with a year of 12 months and each month of 30 days, so the spiritual structure has each Imam with 12 ⟪ar:حجج⟫ and each ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ with 30 ⟪ar:دعات⟫.

⟪ar:فھو من خلال ھؤلاء⟫ — It is through these intermediaries of Allah that His argument has reached His creatures, and through them His justice has manifested, outwardly and inwardly. For the inner aspect (⟪ar:الباطن⟫) is the true religion of Allah, while the outer aspect (⟪ar:الظاہر⟫) is the revealed paths (⟪ar:شریعۃ⟫) and its symbols. Religion is the soul and living spirit of those revealed paths, and the ordinances of ⟪ar:شریعۃ⟫ are the body. The body subsists only through the spirit, and the spirit subsists only through the body, which is its covering and a sign pointing to it. Thus there is not a single "letter" of the inner aspect, nor a friend (⟪ar:ولی⟫) of Allah, who does not have many visible signs in the outer world.

### Worldly Symbols Pointing to Spiritual Entities

The young man asked about this lower world — why some pursue it while scholars condemn it. The scholar explained that the world is only the outer aspect of the inner dimension (⟪ar:ظاہر الباطن⟫):

⟪ar:فمن عرف الروح طاب لہ الجسم⟫ — Whoever recognizes the spirit finds the body pleasing.

⟪ar:ومن لم یعرف الروح و اعترف بالجسم کان الجسم علیہ میتۃ و المیتۃ حرام⟫ — But whoever is ignorant of the spirit and recognizes only the body, for him the body is dead and decaying flesh, which is forbidden.

So scholars "forbade" this world only in the sense that Allah forbade eating the dead unless approached through sacrifice. "Every act without a meaningful outcome is child's play, and every speech without an essential meaning is falsehood — and Allah is exalted above that" (⟪ar:وکل فعل لا عاقبۃ لہ فھو لعب ، وکل کلام لا معنی لہ فھو کذب، واللہ اعز من ذلک⟫). Experiencing this world while recognizing its spiritual counterparts is allowed and recommended; what is forbidden is living for this world alone, as if it were created without purpose and would return to nothingness without accountability.

⟪ar:فأن الدنیا وجمیع أمثالھا ظاھر للآخرۃ وما فیھا، فھی روحھا و حیاتھا⟫ — This physical world and all it contains are symbols for the outer aspects of the spiritual world; the physical world is a body, and the spiritual world its spirit, bringing it to life.

⟪ar:فمن سعی فی الدنیا للدنیا بلا معرفۃ للأخرۃ کان سعیۃ ضلالاً ، اذ لا معنی لسعیۃ ولا عاقبۃ لہ⟫ — Whoever strives in this world for its own sake, without awareness of the spiritual world, his striving is aimless wandering.

⟪ar:ومن سعی فی الدنیا للأخرۃ و ھو مؤمن بھا ، کان لسعیہ معنی و عاقبۃ، وکان سعیہ مشکوراً⟫ — But whoever strives in this world for the next, with faith, his striving has purpose and result, and is met with Allah's acceptance.

The scholar then declared that not a single creature — not an insect, not a bird, nothing wet or dry, no mountain, stone, tree, or precious mineral — exists except as a symbol established by Him. Even the tiniest dust mote (⟪ar:ذرۃ⟫) is bound by six sides — front, back, right, left, top, bottom — while the mote itself is the seventh: a sign pointing to the six root-principles from which everything was created, with Air encompassing them all as their seventh and their king.

He then answered the young man's questions about the symbols one by one:

- The **Heaven**, towering above all and encompassing everything, symbolizes the ⟪ar:ناطق⟫, who towers above all spiritual creation by his excellence and knows the inner meanings of the revealed path (⟪ar:الشریعۃ⟫).
- The **12 Zodiac mansions** (⟪ar:البروج⟫) and their stars symbolize the 12 ⟪ar:نقباء⟫ — chiefs of the ⟪ar:ناطق⟫ — and the stars are their ⟪ar:دعات⟫.
- The **Earth**, a resting place and refuge, symbolizes the ⟪ar:باب⟫ of the ⟪ar:ناطق⟫ during his lifetime, and his designated ⟪ar:وصی⟫ (trustee) after his death — a refuge who knows the esoteric interpretation (⟪ar:تاویل⟫).
- The **12 islands** (⟪ar:جزائر⟫) symbolize the 12 ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ who assist the ⟪ar:وصی⟫.
- The **Rivers and Springs** symbolize the ⟪ar:دعات⟫.
- The **Rulers of the Seven Heavens** are the intermediaries (⟪ar:اسباب⟫) between Allah and the ⟪ar:نطقا⟫.
- The **Seven Oceans** are the intermediaries between Allah and the Imams following the ⟪ar:ناطق⟫.
- **Salty water** represents the knowledge of the outward aspect (⟪ar:علم الظاھرۃ⟫), which has no support for growth; **sweet, fresh water** — from springs or rain — represents the knowledge of the inner aspect (⟪ar:علم الباطنۃ⟫), conveyed by the ⟪ar:دعات⟫. Water became a symbol for knowledge because water is life for every living being, just as knowledge is life for every scholar.

### Air, the Loftiest Symbol — and the Instance Beyond It

When asked about the Air which encompasses everything, the scholar replied: "The Air is too exalted to be a symbol for any creation." Asked whether Air or the heavens and earth are greater, he said: "Indeed it is greater than both. The extent of the earth in relation to the Air is like a seal-ring (⟪ar:مہر کی انگوٹھی⟫) in relation to a great plain; the atmosphere in relation to the heavens is like a seal-ring in a vast desert; and the heaven in comparison to the full extent of the Air is like an egg in relation to an immense plain. What, then, could symbolize Air? There is none like Air." Its inner meaning is "a sign pointing to the One who has no likeness — neither in the outward aspects nor the inner."

The young man objected, citing ⟪ar:فَلَا تَضْرِبُوا۟ لِلَّهِ ٱلْأَمْثَالَ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَعْلَمُونَ⟫ — "So do not make any likenesses for Allah, for He knows and you do not." The scholar answered with ⟪ar:وَلَهُ ٱلْمَثَلُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ⟫ — "And Allah is the loftiest symbol" — and explained:

⟪ar:ان لکل شئی معنی، ومعنی الامثال انھا فی الظاھر دلائل علی الباطن⟫ — Every creation is a symbol with a meaning; the external meaning of symbols are signs pointing to their inner meaning.

⟪ar:فنھی تبارک وتعالی عن ان یقاس بشئی من خلقہ، فیقال ھذا مثل اللہ کما یقال ھذا مثل الامام، وھذا مثل الحجۃ، وھذا مثل الداعی⟫ — Allah forbade that He be compared to anything among His creatures, the way one says "this is a symbol for the Imam," "for his proof," or "for a ⟪ar:داعی⟫."

⟪ar:فصارت امثالھم دلائل علیھم وعلی تفاضلھم⟫ — For their symbols became signs pointing to them and to their respective dignities.

⟪ar:ولکن تؤخذ الأمثال علی معانیھا، فأن معانیھا لھا دلائل تدل صغارھا علی کبارھا، وکبارھا تدل علی المعانی العلیا، والعلیا تدل علی العلی الاعلی⟫ — Symbols are understood according to their meanings: the lesser pointing to the greater, the greater to the highest, and the highest to the Most Exalted.

⟪ar:فلذلک قال: وللہ المثل الاعلی⟫ — Therefore He said "And Allah's is the loftiest symbol" — meaning the supreme sign, for there is no sign pointing to anything higher.

⟪ar:وھو الھواء: الاعلی علی السماء ولاارض ، المحیط علی جمیع الاشیاء ولا یحیط بہ شئی منھا⟫ — And that symbol is Air: higher than heaven and earth, encompassing everything and encompassed by nothing.

⟪ar:فھو المثل الاعلی و ھو الدلیل علی من لیس کمثلہ شئی⟫ — It is the loftiest symbol, pointing to the One who has no likeness.

⟪ar:ولم یقل لیس مثلہ شئی اذ لا مثل لہ ولکن عنی ان لیس الدلیل علیہ کالدلائل، لہ المثل الاعلی⟫ — He did not say "nothing is like Him," since He has no likeness; rather, He meant that the sign pointing to Him is not like other signs.

⟪ar:ولیس کدلیلہ دلیل من أدلۃ غیرہ ممن وصفنا من خلقہ⟫ — None of the signs pointing to His creations is like His sign.

⟪ar:فتبارک اللہ رب العرش العظیم⟫ — So blessed be Allah, Master of the majestic throne.

The careful point: He is not saying that air is "like" Allah. He is saying that nothing in creation is like air — but then nothing, including air, is like Allah. The comparison demonstrates incomparability through scale.

The young man then asked whether anything existed before the Air, between it and Allah. "Yes," said the Scholar. "The Instance (⟪ar:الأوان⟫) — the instance of His divine will, through which He created what He willed and commanded what He wished." This is the ultimate limit of the dialogue, the point where even the metaphor of air breaks down. The young man pleaded: "Now you've carried me into the depths of the ocean of this loftiest assembly. Please return with me to the lower physical world... for I fear for my soul, that my intellect may have led me toward something whose essence I cannot grasp." The scholar replied: "You climbed high in your questioning, and my answers climbed with you to the very peak of these inner aspects. Therein you lost your vision and your intellect was perplexed. So what state would you have been in if I started unveiling the inner meanings of these inner aspects (⟪ar:باطن الباطنۃ⟫) for you? You and I would have been like Moses and the scholar [al-Khidr]."

## Part Three — Knowledge versus Action: The Three Levels and the Spiritual Economy

*The teaching deepens: the three levels of knowledge mapped to angels, humans, and beasts; the esoteric meaning of "no power nor strength"; the Yusuf dream; the necessary union of inner and outer; and the disciple's purification of his possessions before he parts from the Master and is drawn toward his rebirth.*

### The Three Levels of Knowledge — Angels, Humans, and Beasts

The young man asked whether there is an inner aspect to the inner dimension. "By my life, indeed there is," said the scholar. "It holds the highest of spiritual stations." The young man then grasped that there are three levels of knowledge: an outer dimension (⟪ar:ظاہر⟫), an inner dimension (⟪ar:باطن⟫), and an innermost dimension of that inner aspect (⟪ar:باطن الباطن⟫).

The scholar pointed to this from the Book of Allah, in the verse of the garment (A'raf 7:26): the clothing is the outer aspect (⟪ar:ظاہر⟫); its purpose, to cover the private parts, is the inner (⟪ar:باطن⟫); and the innermost is the garment of piety (⟪ar:لباس التقوی⟫), toward which the symbol "adornment" (⟪ar:ریش⟫) points. He then gave the intellectual proof: everything is understood through its name (⟪ar:أسم⟫), its attributes (⟪ar:صفات⟫), or its meaning (⟪ar:معنی⟫) — meaning being the real essence, the name belonging to it, the attribute pointing to it. "The moon rose": the disk is the meaning, "moon" the name, "rose" the attribute; none alone has meaning, but combined they reveal it. So Allah's religion has its outer aspect (its name), its inner aspect (its attributes), and its innermost level (its true meaning). The symbol is the egg: the shell is the outer aspect, the white is the inner, and the yolk is the innermost — its true substance.

Then the crucial mapping. "Creatures exist in three levels: the level of Angels; the level of human beings; and the level of the beasts. Knowledge of the outer aspect of this physical world is at the level of the beasts — whoever knows it alone, without its inner aspect, operates at the level of such animals. Knowledge of the inner aspect (⟪ar:باطن⟫) is the knowledge of the true descendants of Adam (⟪ar:انسان⟫) — the one who knows it has true faith and operates at the level of a human being. But knowledge of the inner dimension of that inner aspect is the knowledge of the angels: whoever knows that becomes purely spiritual in his knowledge, resembling the angels, yet remaining physical with a body resembling humans. Such a person is a prophet, whom Allah places as His vicegerent on earth." Such a one becomes the veil of the angels, the exemplar and interpreter of divine revelation, holding the keys to the gardens of Paradise and authority over Hellfire.

"In essence, there are only two types of human beings: the sanctified scholar (⟪ar:عالم ربانی⟫), who understands the purpose of all spiritual sciences and whose spirit is connected to the spirit of certainty (⟪ar:یقین⟫) — sanctified through his actions as well as his knowledge — and all others, who are still seeking along the path of salvation. The rest of mankind are riffraff and rabble (⟪ar:ھمج رعاع⟫) who don't really know anything: followers of every screaming voice, deluded by error, yet believing they do good."

The young man asked whether that lofty level could be reached by climbing through the levels below it. The scholar warned that the angelic level is specially honored by Allah and can only be attained through the most excellent and meritorious action, with the most sincere certainty and a pure attentive heart. "Surely their Allah is also your Allah, and there is no undeserved bond of nearness between Allah and his creation. Every creature comes close to Allah only by his spiritual mindfulness (⟪ar:تقوی⟫) and good actions (⟪ar:عمل الصالح⟫)." But he could not simply give it; like a farmer who fertilizes, plants, and waters but cannot make the plant grow, the scholar can teach but cannot create the harvest. "Hold tightly to the rope of Allah, through which you have been called and guided." When asked if Allah's rope is the Imam, he answered: "He is an outward aspect of that rope — your strongest support, reached through the intermediaries of his proofs (⟪ar:حجۃ⟫) and his gateway (⟪ar:باب⟫)."

The young man replied: "So there is no power and no strength except through Allah. Surely whoever is without a true spiritual guide would drown in the floods of ignorance, just as anyone without a ship would drown in the oceans of this physical world."

### Power and Strength — Ḥawl and Quwwa as Esoteric Names

"But what are that 'power' and 'strength' which exist only through Allah?" The young man first offered the common interpretations: that ⟪ar:حول⟫ means "a year" (citing ⟪ar:وَٱلْوَلِدَتُ يُرْضِعْنَ أَوْلَدَهُنَّ حَوْلَيْنِ كَامِلَيْ⟫ — "mothers shall suckle their children for two complete years"), and that "strength" refers to mules (citing the verse on the treasures of Qarun, ⟪ar:أُو۟لِى ٱلْقُوَّةِ⟫, where "band" means forty men and "strength" forty mules). The scholar pointed out the absurdity: "Then 'there is no power and no strength except through Allah' would mean 'there is no year and no mule except through Allah.'" The young man admitted this was weak and asked for the true inner meaning.

The scholar replied: "People do not utter a single word of truth without its having a source in the inner dimension (⟪ar:الباطن⟫). Nor is there any falsehood that did not once have a real meaning — only those words were turned away from their original meaning and so became absurd."

As for ⟪ar:حول⟫ (power): it refers to Allah's ⟪ar:ناطق⟫, or his Imam — the Master of the twelve chiefs (⟪ar:نقبا⟫), just as ⟪ar:حول⟫ as "a year" is completed only by twelve months. The Imam is called ⟪ar:حول⟫ because he transforms and conveys (⟪ar:تحویل⟫) the Creator's speech, converting its subtle essential meanings into human language; thus the words of wisdom become the body and form, while the Creator's speech is their true spirit. This is the communications model of the hierarchy: the Creator's speech is pure spirit; the Imam, as al-Ḥawl, converts it into the "body and form" of words of wisdom; the Bāb then condenses and distributes it to the Duʿāt in detailed form suited to their level — as a general gives orders to captains, who pass them to soldiers.

As for ⟪ar:قوۃ⟫ (strength): it refers to the Imam's ⟪ar:باب⟫ (gateway), to whom Allah has given strength to bear His mighty words. The ⟪ar:باب⟫ takes divine speech from the Imam in its condensed, all-inclusive form (⟪ar:مجملا⟫) and disseminates it to the ⟪ar:دعات⟫ in more detailed and particular form. So the ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ is called "strength," but has no strength except through Allah. Thus "there is no power and no strength except through Allah" means "there is no Imam and no ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ except through Allah."

⟪ar:حول⟫ has to do with scheming or strategy — a function of the spirit and mind; ⟪ar:قوۃ⟫ has to do with physical capability. The mind devises a strategy and the body executes it with strength. There are two contexts: in the first, the ⟪ar:ناطق⟫ is ⟪ar:حول⟫ (providing the strategy by transforming divine speech) and his ⟪ar:وصی⟫ is ⟪ar:قوۃ⟫ (bearing and disseminating it); in the second, the ⟪ar:امام⟫ is ⟪ar:حول⟫ and his ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ is ⟪ar:قوۃ⟫. So the formula ⟪ar:لاحول ولا قوۃ الا باللہ⟫ means that neither the ⟪ar:ناطق⟫ nor the ⟪ar:وصی⟫ — and neither the ⟪ar:امام⟫ nor the ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ — has the capacity to provide or disseminate revelation except that Allah enables it.

### The Story of Yusuf — The Dream and the King's Vision

This is why ⟪ar:یوسف علیہ السلام⟫ said to his father, describing his dream: ⟪ar:إِنِّى رَأَيْتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًۭا وَٱلشَّمْسَ وَٱلْقَمَرَ رَأَيْتُهُمْ لِى سَجِدِينَ⟫ — "Indeed I have seen eleven stars and the sun and the moon prostrating to me" (12:4). By the "sun" he meant the Imam, by the "moon" his ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ and ⟪ar:باب⟫, and the "stars" the eleven ⟪ar:نقبا⟫. Likewise Yusuf's "brothers" said: ⟪ar:لَيُوسُفُ وَأَخُوهُ أَحَبُّ إِلَىٰٓ أَبِينَا مِنَّا وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ⟫ — "Joseph and his brother are more beloved to our father than we, while we are a clan" (12:8) — referring to themselves as the twelve ⟪ar:نقبا⟫.

The doctrinal point: the twelve brothers of ⟪ar:یوسف علیہ السلام⟫ were not his biological brothers. They were the twelve members of the cabinet of ⟪ar:حضرت اسحاق⟫, the prophet of the time. The sun was ⟪ar:اسحاق علیہ السلام⟫ himself, the moon was Yusuf's own ⟪ar:باب⟫, and the eleven stars were the remaining cabinet members. So Yusuf's dream revealed that his rank would be elevated above all twelve members of Isḥāq's cabinet, including Isḥāq himself. The "brothers'" jealousy was the jealousy of cabinet members over rank, not a family quarrel — and Allah's affair was fulfilled, ending in their remorse and repentance.

As for the king's dream — ⟪ar:سَبْعَ بَقَرَتٍۢ سِمَانٍۢ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌۭ وَسَبْعَ سُنۢبُلَتٍ خُضْرٍۢ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَتٍۢ⟫, "seven fat cows eaten by seven lean, and seven green ears of wheat and dry ones" — the vision is like the outer dimension (⟪ar:ظاھر⟫) of religious action: a mirage that yields nothing when reached. The seven fat cows are Allah's seven heavenly intermediaries between Him and the ⟪ar:ناطق⟫; the seven lean cows are the seven ⟪ar:نطقا⟫ themselves, hungry for what comes from Allah through those intermediaries. The seven green ears are the seven intermediaries between Allah and the ⟪ar:وصی⟫ (fresh, full of healthy ingredient); the seven dry ears are the ⟪ar:اوصیا⟫ themselves, who would dry up without their sustenance. With respect to the foundation of Islam, these symbols are like a seed grown into seven ears of grain — from whose seed have come seven Imams.

### The Necessary Union of Inner and Outer

The young man asked about the verse ⟪ar:فَلَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَا⟫. The scholar noted Allah did not say "do not let this physical world deceive you" but "do not let the *life* of this physical world deceive you" — because "life" has four meanings: physical life in this inferior world and its passing away; spiritual life in the superior world and its lasting eternally. ⟪ar:يَلَيْتَنِى قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِى⟫ — on the Day of Judgment man will regret, "I wish I had prepared for my [eternal] life." There is life through knowledge of the world's outer aspect (⟪ar:حیات بعلم الظاہرۃ⟫) — the lowest form — and life through knowledge of its inner aspect (⟪ar:حیات بعلم الباطنۃ⟫), which is the basis for true eternal life. Physical knowledge is forgotten at death; the soul takes with it only the spiritual understanding of physical realities.

He then ranked the cases. The one who knows the inner aspect but neglects the outer obligations (prayer, fasting) is in a terrible state, for the inner cannot subsist when the protective outer is abandoned — like fruit whose covering is peeled before ripening, it rots; like a body whose limbs are cut, the spirit cannot remain. Such a person is ⟪ar:فاسق⟫ — the word ⟪ar:فسق⟫ used when something rots and turns ugly — an enemy of Allah's friends who can never attain their company, for he has "severed what Allah commanded to be joined" (⟪ar:وَيَقْطَعُونَ مَآ أَمَرَ ٱللَّهُ بِهِ⟫). And the one who knows and upholds the outer aspect but remains ignorant of the inner is in the worst station of all: like a body with no spirit breathed into it, numbered among the dead — the literal Arabic being ⟪ar:کافر⟫, the same root used for a farmer who hides the seed under the ground. Only through the union of both — the inner spiritual dimension and the outer obligations of ⟪ar:شریعۃ⟫ — can one succeed in the afterlife and be a person of faith (⟪ar:مؤمن⟫). For such a person, this physical world becomes licit, and they are most deserving of it.

### Differences in Opinions and Actions; Divine Justice in Acceptance

When the young man asked why the true scholars look pale and weak, rejecting this world though they are not sick, the scholar wept, then explained: those people reflected on their souls with the true light of life; certain of the divine promise and threat, they hastened to act while they had time. Their bodies grew weak from fasting, their complexions paled from vigilant nights of prayer; they were distracted from luxury by reflection (⟪ar:الفکر⟫) and engrossed in the sweetness of Allah's remembrance (⟪ar:ذکر اللہ⟫). "They rejected this physical world only because they were distracted from it by their concern for the next — not because they considered it forbidden."

Asked about the origin of creatures and the difference in their opinions, the scholar explained: bodies vary in language and color as a sign of Allah's power, so people may recognize one another — but their internal and external instruments and faculties are created identically. No scholar has three eyes, no ignorant person one — there is only equality in the creation of the physical instruments. Since the external design is identical, the inner instruments of will, intellect, and reasoning are identical too, by the pattern of divine justice: "For Allah would not treat His creatures justly in their outer aspect and unjustly in their inner aspects. He has not placed two hearts in the scholar's chest, nor forbidden any means of understanding for the ignorant. The instrument of knowledge is one." It is by this instrument that Allah established His argument against them — He provided what they failed to employ — as in ⟪ar:لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ لَّا يَفْقَهُونَ بِهَا⟫: "They have hearts but do not understand with them; eyes but do not see; ears but do not hear."

The difference in passions and factions, then, came not from the instrument but from the root being corrupted. "All came from Adam, and Adam from dust... but through marriage they left behind the divine way (⟪ar:السنۃ⟫); their progeny abandoned the divine creed and followed paths that led them astray. Once the root is corrupted, the branches are soon ruined." All were alike in seeking ease for their bodies and respect for their souls, but they differed in reaching the unified goal — like people shooting arrows at one target, alike in intention and instrument, yet differing in actually hitting it. One group accepted the messengers' promise of Allah's eternal recompense, though it was rare in this world; the other chose this world's fleeting but readily available pleasures. "Each one receives from Him what he seeks."

As people of this world have ranks (kings and those who serve them), so the people of religion have ranks. Allah, in His mercy, accepts from the weak whatever they can perform in good faith — "He imposed the duty of knowledge and action on every person according to their capability." He then joins them, in His justice, with the people of the most perfect faith: ⟪ar:وَمَن يُطِعِ ٱللَّهَ وَٱلرَّسُولَ فَأُو۟لَٓئِكَ مَعَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِم⟫ — "Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be with the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous." The required effort is "acquiring knowledge and acting upon it to the furthest extent of your capabilities." Sincerity and faithfulness are Allah's two gardens; none will be saved except the sincere and faithful. The foundation of action is obedience — carrying out all obligations and abstaining from all that is prohibited, which is "the ⟪ar:زکوۃ⟫ of the body (⟪ar:زکوۃ الأبدان⟫)" purifying the rest — staying in gratitude, and treating people as you would want them to treat you.

When the young man asked about allowance under necessity, the scholar cited ⟪ar:فَمَنِ ٱضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍۢ وَلَا عَادٍۢ فَلَآ إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ⟫ — "Whoever is forced by necessity, neither desiring the forbidden nor willingly disobeying, there is no sin upon him" — recalling the woman in the time of the second ⟪ar:خلیفۃ⟫ who, thirsting for three days and fearing for her children, complied with a man's condition for water; Omar ordered her stoned, but Maulana Ali (AS) declared her free of sin by this verse.

### Purifying Possessions and Parting from the Master

The young man then asked about his obligations regarding his possessions. The scholar instructed: take only what was lawfully acquired, in which no one was wronged; take out ⟪ar:زکوۃ⟫ from the whole; then purify it by taking one-fifth (⟪ar:خمس⟫) from its finest portion that you love most and spending it on those most deserving. "You purify your possessions through charity just as you purify your body through righteous acts. Righteous acts are a protection; charity is a purification." The remainder he should spend on himself and family in the accustomed manner, and give the surplus in charity to the needy.

The young man then revealed that he had divided all his possessions into five portions — a practical spiritual economy, each with its own pedagogical weight:
1. One portion for its purification (⟪ar:زکواۃ⟫);
2. One for his atonement (⟪ar:کفارۃ⟫) for the harm he had done his soul, to be kept so he keeps remembering his sins and avoids complacency;
3. One in gratitude (the "shukr" portion) for the knowledge acquired, to be held as a trust until he reaches spiritual maturity;
4. One for the needy among his brothers, to be given by his own hand so they recognize his generosity;
5. And one to support the scholar in his travels.

The scholar, moved to tears, refused his own portion: "I have no need of it; I have left behind worldly possessions and fled from the conflicts and temptations of wealth." He instructed the young man to give the brothers' portion with his own hands, to keep the atonement portion as a remembrance of his sins, to hold the gratitude portion as a trust until maturity, and to keep the purification portion entrusted to him by its rightful master — "until you meet him; for he is the most rightful judge, for you and against you." Asked who that noble person was, the scholar spoke of the Imam: the one whose right Allah has made obligatory upon every believer, in whose hands are the keys of the heavenly gardens, the guide of angels (⟪ar:معالم الملکونت⟫), whose palms reveal the light of the mountain of Sinai (⟪ar:طور⟫). "I am his spiritual son," said the scholar, "and one of his beautiful works (⟪ar:حُسنَۃُ من حسناتہ⟫) — constantly seeking his satisfaction and fearing his wrath."

Then the scholar departed and traveled until he reached his own spiritual father (⟪ar:والدہ الاکبر⟫), the Sheikh, and narrated the young man's story. The Sheikh recognized his qualities, was pleased, and said: "Hurry back with this young man, for I desire for him to become a gateway of loving kindness that Allah may open for the people of his time."

When the scholar returned, he found the young man in a very different state — his body weak, his complexion pale. "That is from the desolation and grief that overcame me after you left," the young man said. "Your separation made me lonely; and the fear of not being able to meet you again made me sick." These descriptions are descriptions of extreme love — this is ⟪ar:عشق⟫, what love does to a lover parted from his beloved. The physical deterioration is not illness but spiritual longing. The scholar reassured him: "Allah knew what was in your heart, so He made your trial easy and placed a special loving mercy in the heart of His friend for you. Start preparing for your journey, for I am taking you to the doorstep of your ultimate goal — and there is no power but through Allah." Hearing this, the young man sighed so heavily with joy and awe that his spirit almost left his body.

## Part Four — The Disciple Becomes Master

*The disciple meets the Sheikh, is spiritually reborn and named, completes his pilgrimage of recognition, returns home to convert his father al-Bakhtarī, and faces the great scholar Abu Malik — the Kaʿb al-Aḥbār of his age — in a debate on the proper way to seek the truth.*

### Rebirth of the Disciple — The Naming After Seven Days

They traveled to the greater scholar — the Sheikh — and were admitted. The Sheikh began with a sublime praise of Allah, who "split the dark obscurity of hearts with His light," who out of His justice imposed upon His creatures only what they could bear:

⟪ar:فاعتدل الامر بین الامرین فسمی عدلا⟫ — He harmoniously balanced His command between the two — doing what He commanded and refraining from what He forbade — and named it "Justice."

"Creation is incapable of repaying the burden of what He deserves for all His blessings; yet He is only displeased by them when they are ungrateful." He exists everywhere but not within boundaries; is known but cannot be described; exists yet cannot be seen; is recognized but cannot be fully known. "The honor of people's intellect is its use in searching for Allah; among the rewards of seeking Him is finding Him; and a sign of having found Him is experiencing the sweetness of His presence." His distinctive sign is the truth, and the duties flowing from that truth are obligatory, as ⟪ar:سَنُرِيهِمْ آيَتِنَا فِى ٱلأٓفَاقِ وَفِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ حَتَّىٰ يَتَبَيَّنَ لَهُمْ أَنَّهُ ٱلْحَقُّ⟫ — "We will show them Our Signs in the universe and in their own selves, until it becomes manifest to them that this is the truth" (41:53).

Then the Sheikh turned to the young man: "O brave youth (⟪ar:فتی⟫), what is your name?" "⟪ar:عبد اللہ ابن عبد اللہ⟫ — Slave of Allah, son of the slave of Allah." "That is your description, not your name." "Then I am ⟪ar:حُر⟫, the freed man, son of the servant of Allah." "Who set you free?" The young man pointed to the scholar. "But if your teacher is owned by another and does not own you, has he any right to free you?" "No." The young man hung his head, unable to answer. "How can we recognize something that has no name, even if it has been born?" "I have been born to you," said the young man, "so give me a name."

"That can only happen after the completion of seven days," said the Sheikh, "in honor of the new-born infant." This is the birth-into-faith ritual. The number 7 represents life: the universe received its life through ⟪ar:کن فیکون⟫, whose seventh letter began life; all creation takes place in seven stages, as in ⟪ar:وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلْإِنسَنَ مِن سُلَلَةٍۢ مِّن طِينٍۢ . ثُمَّ جَعَلْنَهُ نُطْفَةًۭ فِى قَرَارٍۢ مَّكِينٍۢ . ثُمَّ خَلَقْنَا ٱلنُّطْفَةَ عَلَقَةًۭ فَخَلَقْنَا ٱلْعَلَقَةَ مُضْغَةًۭ فَخَلَقْنَا ٱلْمُضْغَةَ عِظَمًۭا فَكَسَوْنَا ٱلْعِظَمَ لَحْمًۭا ثُمَّ أَنشَأْنَهُ خَلْقًا آخَرَ ۚ فَتَبَارَكَ ٱللَّهُ أَحْسَنُ ٱلْخَلِقِينَ⟫ (23:12-14) — the seven-stage creation of the embryo. True creation, true life, comes from the seventh in every series of seven; one cannot name something until it is complete. The young man asked, "Will I own this name?" "If you owned it, you would become the one worshiped. The name is your owner, and you are owned by it — for you cannot rise beyond your own spiritual rank." The spiritual rank defines and limits the person.

After the seven days, the Sheikh ordered the young man to perform a complete ablution and dress in his finest, then drew him close and spoke "of realities that cannot be comprehended by imagination, that pens would fail to write" — precious jewels unveiled only to the worthy. The young man came to know his master; "he held on firmly to the unbreakable connection; he circled the house of Allah; he completed his pilgrimage through Allah's greatest sign." This is the esoteric meaning of the Ṭawāf: when we go on pilgrimage and circle the house seven times, we simulate the motion of the planets orbiting the sun. The sun symbolizes the Imam, or the ⟪ar:کعبۃ⟫, and we symbolize the planets, pulled in by the gravitational field of the sun — pulled in by the love of the Imam, by the love of Allah. The entire concept of esoteric pilgrimage symbolizes the ⟪ar:معرفۃ⟫, the recognition of the Imam: orbiting him, staying within our lanes of obedience, moving in the designated direction. Love is symbolized by gravity. Thus the young man finally attained the object of his desire — meeting the Imam and learning the true realities of existence.

### Returning Home — The Six Counsels and the Conversion of al-Bakhtarī

The young man and his spiritual father visited the Sheikh regularly to study, until the Sheikh recognized that "the young man's roots had been well watered and his branches had grown tall." Then he gave him leave to depart, for three reasons: the Sheikh was about to travel; the young man had been long separated from his father, causing him great sorrow; and it was now the young man's obligation to pass on what this divine favor required. The Sheikh advised him to be mindful of Allah, to uphold the oath (⟪ar:عہد⟫), to safeguard and assist his spiritual father, and to keep his identity concealed. He gave permission for the scholar to accompany him home.

As they reached the outskirts of the city, the scholar gave his own final counsel and named his residence so the young man could find him. He gave six parting counsels — a da'ī's operating manual:
1. Once you have resolved upon something, entrust yourself completely to Allah;
2. Speak with words of wisdom when you call others to good;
3. Do not become angry when you are wronged;
4. Do not become upset when you are called a liar;
5. Remain patient with anyone who responds to your call;
6. Make sure you become the best example of that to which you are calling others.

The young man thanked him "for three things: for the Imam whose argument you have upheld; for the ⟪ar:حجۃ⟫ whose trust you have fulfilled; and for this corpse, that you have so graciously given life to." At their separation he sighed as though his soul had taken flight, kissed the scholar's feet, and returned home, where his grief was so great that his neighbors grew annoyed.

Then his father confronted him: "Is this how sons repay their fathers? You chose that stranger over me and supported his cause over the path of your forefathers. If what you found in him is truth, you betrayed me by concealing it; and if it is false, you destroyed yourself." The young man replied with gentleness, asking for one of two things: either to be left to his beliefs, remaining an obedient son, or to be allowed to debate, with the assurance that his father would not become angry. The father, refusing the hypocrisy of outward agreement amid inner difference, agreed to debate. The young man then pressed his argument: "Over the past forty years, either you were a scholar of truth who withheld my share of your knowledge — in which case you cannot criticize me for seeking it elsewhere — or you were ignorant of the truth I have now learned, in which case you are even more in need of that stranger than I am, for you should have preceded me in learning from him." The old man's heart was touched, his eyes overflowed with tears at the truth, and he too entered the path. Through father and son, Allah gave life to a great number of people in that country.

### The Arrival of Abu Malik — The Kaʿb al-Aḥbār of His Age

The name of the young man was ⟪ar:صالح⟫ (Ṣāliḥ), and the name of his father was ⟪ar:البختاری⟫ (al-Bakhtarī). They enjoyed great respect among their people for their generosity and the knowledge they had acquired, and their religious position spread until it reached a group among the followers of their former beliefs. Al-Bakhtarī had been generous to these followers, and they feared losing his favors now that he had left their sect. So they sought the help of a scholar of their sect named ⟪ar:عبد الجبار بن ابو مالک⟫ — known among them as ⟪ar:کعب الاحبار⟫ ("glory of the yearned") for his scrupulous piety, great understanding, and passionate devotion. He was a 7th-century ⟪ar:یمنی⟫ scholar famous for his knowledge and his ability to defeat people in debates; the title signified the highest scholarly reputation in the community. This is no naive questioner but the very best the opposing community can field.

They told Abu Malik that Salih, son of al-Bakhtarī, had left their sect under the influence of a persuasive stranger, and that al-Bakhtarī had now joined his son. They had avoided the two men for three reasons: their gratitude for al-Bakhtarī's past favors; the fear that, if these people followed something authentic, they themselves were at fault for not following it; and the fear that, if these people had strayed, they were at fault for not preventing it.

Abu Malik responded with striking fairness. He refused to condemn al-Bakhtarī and his son without first hearing them, for that would be injustice; nor would he blindly assume they were rightly guided, for that too would be injustice. He warned against following the passions of "the false idols of previous religious communities, who were pleased by their own sound intellect and rejoiced in the knowledge they possessed, assuming they had encompassed everything from Allah's command — for they said Allah will never send a messenger after their messenger, and did not realize there was a way of proceeding beyond the knowledge they had attained." Their choice of a new belief, he said, could only be for one of three reasons: they were still on the old religion with an added element of tradition or poetry; or the stranger had brought them something pleasing in addition to their religion, in which case "knowledge is the lost camel of the believer" and an increase in knowledge is better than a lack of it; or their guidance came from a true source of prophethood, in which case they followed clear guidance.

### The Proper Way to Seek the Truth

Abu Malik continued: "Pride in one's own opinion is blindness, and neglecting to seek Allah is a sin. But there is no fault for those who continue searching." Here he articulated the inversion of signs — why truth is so hard to recognize: "The distinctive signs of falsehood manifest themselves in hypocritical appearances and in conformity to accepted opinions. But the clear signs of truth only manifest themselves through tests and afflictions, and through the opposition of passions. Only those with a sound heart (⟪ar:قلب سلیم⟫) can renounce their passions and persevere through the onset of tests." Falsehood appears attractive and easy; truth appears harsh and demanding — which is why the majority always follow the easier-appearing path. A sound heart, he said, is "one that does not insist on denying what occurs to it, until the truth reveals its superiority and falsehood unveils its defects." And true understanding of the search means knowing that you are in need of what you seek, and approaching it with the humility that comes from that need — a humility that must come *before* the truth becomes clear, for only through it can one witness the manifestation of truth.

His companions, their hearts softened, asked permission to go to Salih and al-Bakhtarī. Abu Malik insisted they go together, that he would not spare himself what he asked of them, and that they must act today — not in three days, not in two. "I will do something good tomorrow is a satanic thought; the only time to do something is today and now." So they repented of their sins, repaid their debts, purified themselves, and set out the same day, saying, "There is no power except through Allah."

### Abu Malik Debates Salih — Emptying the Cup

They reached al-Bakhtarī's residence. When Abu Malik asked after Salih, al-Bakhtarī replied: "Today Salih is my father, and I am his son, and Salih is with his Lord." Abu Malik, assuming Salih had died, recited ⟪ar:إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَجِعُونَ⟫. "No," said al-Bakhtarī, "Salih is not dead, nor will he ever die; indeed he abides with Allah for all eternity." Abu Malik found three perplexities in this — the son become father, the Lord existing on earth with a servant, the mortal living forever — and asked to meet "the righteous slave." The father, now subservient to his own son, sought Salih's permission.

Salih received them with perfect ⟪ar:آداب⟫, praying that Allah would open the ears of their hearts. The debate began. Salih asked which of three kinds of seeker Abu Malik was: the one who already knows, the one beginning to know, or the one who desires to know. Abu Malik admitted he did not know the modes of seeking at all, and Salih taught them: among the treasures of the other world there is the ⟪ar:عارف⟫ — the sanctified scholar (⟪ar:العالم الربانی⟫) who seeks the dead to give them life; the student (⟪ar:المتعلم⟫) who has some knowledge and seeks its final stage; and the one just beginning, the ignorant who recognizes only his own need. Abu Malik placed himself in the third.

What Salih is doing here is applying a basic principle of debate: emptying the cup. He breaks Abu Malik down — not to humiliate him, but to make him realize that he truly does not know, creating an opening, a vastness for him. The technical term for this is ⟪ar:کسرۃ⟫ — demolition. There is a formal rank in the spiritual hierarchy called ⟪ar:مکاسر⟫ (the Demolisher), defined by this word: his main function is to demolish existing ideas so they can be replaced with new ones. This is why Salih spends so much of the debate breaking down Abu Malik's prior beliefs before offering alternatives — it is a recognized spiritual pedagogy, not intellectual cruelty.

Then Salih drew the critical distinction between merely *knowing* and truly *understanding*: ⟪ar:عالم⟫ versus ⟪ar:عارف⟫, knowledge versus gnosis. "Knowledge (⟪ar:علم⟫) is simply a report (⟪ar:خبر⟫); while true understanding (⟪ar:معرفۃ⟫) is a vision (⟪ar:عیان⟫)." The report is the case of an ignorant person who only learns he is ignorant when a scholar informs him; true understanding is when a man knows by his own intellect that he cannot find the answers himself, so his heart becomes constricted by his ignorance and his only hope of freedom is to learn. The ⟪ar:عالم⟫ knows that heaven and hell exist; it stays in the category of "good to know." But the ⟪ar:عارف⟫ experiences a desperation to act — a deep realization and powerful longing to reach heaven and avoid hell at all cost. Many operate at the level of ⟪ar:علم⟫ their whole lives; few develop the desperation of ⟪ar:عرفان⟫. Abu Malik confessed he found that intense neediness in his own soul.

Salih cautioned him against rushing into blind imitation, "performing an injustice to wisdom and to yourself — like the merchant who measures from the bottom of the barrel." Abu Malik replied that he hoped for "the truth that would confirm itself," so that disputation — which gives rise to discord, malice, and the contest of egos — could be avoided. Salih praised this: "Indeed the truth confirms itself by its own excellence and nobility. Yet every seed for which the ground has not been prepared will not grow, and every knowledge for which the hearts of students have not first been purified by proper argument will not flourish." The ground had now been prepared.

### Knowledge as Jewels, and the Description of Religion

Abu Malik invited Salih to state his arguments: "Words of wisdom are like jewels — observed in the sunlight, they sparkle; left alone, they do not lose value." Salih agreed, but only where there is no confusion between a real jewel and forged glass. He recounted how the jewel of his former faith, presented to "the jewelers who are experts in the knowledge of gems" — his teacher and the people who receive divine knowledge from the heavens — had been exposed as a forgery of glass. Spiritual knowledge, he said, "cannot be accepted as genuine until it has been presented to those who receive divine knowledge from the heavens; only if they accept it can it be deemed genuine."

Abu Malik asked who these people of divine knowledge could be "after the prophets and messengers," since revelation had ended and all communities now worship by transmitted reports. Salih answered: "Every religion in which worship is based solely on transmitted reports should not be named 'religion' at all. For religion is one, purely upholding what is due to Allah without difference; while transmitted reports all differ from and deny one another." He cited the confusion of moon-sighting: communities that eat on the first day of ⟪ar:رمضان⟫ (which is ⟪ar:حرام⟫) and fast on the day of ⟪ar:عید⟫ (also ⟪ar:حرام⟫), justifying it by the HADEES of moon-sighting — which has turned into the hadees of moon-fighting.

The true meaning of "follow the moon" is a teaching the bare text never states outright. When Rasul Allah (Salallahu alayhi wa aalihee wa sallam) was asked, before Ramadan, "who do we follow when you are gone?", he answered "follow the moon" — meaning follow Maulana Ali (AS), since the moon is the established symbol of the ⟪ar:وصی⟫, just as the sun symbolizes the ⟪ar:ناطق⟫. He used the moon as a generic formula — "in all matters of your life, follow Ali" — not limiting it to Ramadan, because the moon consistently follows the same path across the sky as the sun. The ancient Arabs were capable of understanding this symbolism, but out of hatred and jealousy for Maulana Ali (AS) they reduced it to the literal physical moon, producing the present chaos. The Quran calls the days of fasting ⟪ar:أَيَّامًۭا مَّعْدُودَتٍۢ⟫ — clearly counted days (2:183-184) — and declares ⟪ar:ٱلشَّمْسُ وَٱلْقَمَرُ بِحُسْبَانٍۢ⟫: "The sun and the moon follow precisely calculated paths." ⟪ar:حسبان⟫ is the superlative of ⟪ar:حساب⟫ (calculation): they never deviate, which is exactly what makes a fixed lunar calendar possible. Salih illustrated the absurdity of transmitted reports with the man who came to Abu Malik after divorcing his wife once and then breaking his oath: Abu Malik told him "some say she is divorced, some say she is not — act according to whichever you like," leaving the man not knowing whether his act was permitted or forbidden. "Is that how wise judgment should be in Allah's religion?"

Abu Malik conceded: "By my life, ⟪ar:شریعۃ⟫ has been corrupted and rendered ineffectual, and we are only clinging to the name of religion. So what is the state of religion in your opinion?" Salih first drew out Abu Malik's own definition of religion (commands, prohibitions, prophetic traditions) and of ⟪ar:شریعۃ⟫ — and showed that Abu Malik had placed them at the same level, so that the corruption of Sharia meant the corruption of religion itself, leaving him nothing. Then Salih gave his own description: "Religion is the veil Allah has strengthened against those who sow corruption, its gates opened to those seeking truth. It is a rope extending from the heavens to the earth, continuous and without breakage; the firm handhold of those who adhere to Allah; the ark of the divine presence; the ship of salvation; the light of life. Its knowledge is well-protected, and its jewels have their distinctive signs." Abu Malik replied: "If Allah has a religion by which He is worshiped, and a day for His rewards and punishments, then indeed this would be its essential meaning. But what about the reality of what you have described?" And so the way was opened for the final part of the debate.

## Part Five — Unity and Justice: The Living Witness

*The climax of the debate: the unity of Allah beyond names and adjectives; the syllogism of divine justice that requires a living Ḥujja in every age; the conspiracy by which communities cut themselves off from guidance; the unbroken chain of prophets with no true "interval"; why the friends of Allah are in concealment; and Abu Malik's repentance and the story's conclusion.*

### Who Is Allah? — Beyond Names and Adjectives

Salih said: "I am calling you to Allah's justice and to the assertion of His unity." Abu Malik thought this familiar, but Salih corrected him: "The school you subscribe to describes Allah's justice without any clear understanding of *who* is being described, and strayed regarding His names without any real understanding of His unity." When Abu Malik offered his creed — that Allah is one, just, commanding by giving a choice and forbidding by warning — Salih said: "Those are only names and descriptions, of no benefit without their essential realities. It is like saying 'fire is hot' — true, but you cannot cook food with the words, nor brighten darkness without the reality of fire. If nouns and adjectives could take the place of realities, your tongue would burn at the mere mention of fire, and your hunger would be satiated at the mention of food."

Then Salih pressed the letters themselves: "You say Allah is one (⟪ar:واحد⟫), written with four letters — two joined and two separate. Which is 'one'? And the word ⟪ar:اللہ⟫ is also written with four letters — which of them is 'Allah'? Are they Allah together, or are they signs pointing to Allah?" Abu Malik conceded they must be signs pointing to Allah. "But then which letters point to Him? When you say ⟪ar:اللہ⟫, you separate the initial ⟪ar:الف⟫ and join the remaining three (⟪ar:للہ⟫). Which of these points to Allah — the one by itself, or the others joined?" Abu Malik did not know what to say.

Salih's point exposed a major flaw in how people approach theology: the obsessive analysis of structure, pronunciation, and presentation — composing beautiful tracks of Allah's names, perfecting the form of ⟪ar:وضو⟫ and ⟪ar:رکوع⟫ and ⟪ar:سجدۃ⟫ — while forgetting the essential meaning. "In the end it all boils down to one simple fact: do you understand who Allah is, the reality behind His unity, or do you only have a textbook definition of that ⟪ar:توحید⟫?"

Salih then asked where Abu Malik's understanding of Allah came from — from a name or an attribute? Abu Malik said from a name: "Allah." "Is 'Allah' a name, or someone named?" "Someone named." "Then what is his name?" "Allah." "Allah is a name for Allah? Then what is the difference between the name and the one named, and which do you worship?" Abu Malik, bewildered, finally said he had concluded, from observing that everything manufactured has a manufacturer, that this immense universe must have a Creator who does not resemble His creations.

Salih corrected even this: "You have taken us away from your original meaning. Your difficulty in comprehending Allah's unity forced you to seek Him through logical assumption, and so you forged a likeness for Him. But Allah is unique and exalted beyond that, for everything is made from a pre-existing substance, while Allah creates from no substance. As for worldly manufacturers, you became aware of them by observing how they constructed things — but Allah did not make you witness His creation, nor does He have a likeness whose creation you could observe. And your saying 'there is nothing like His image' implies He *has* an image — whereas if you truly meant absolute unity, you would have said 'there is none like Him.' He is far exalted beyond your implicit analogy. Eyes cannot tolerate the light of the sun, which is only one of this lower world's lights — so how can they perceive the Creator?" Abu Malik confessed: "My words have left me. Intellects are bewildered, imaginations incapable of thinking about Him."

### The Syllogism of Divine Justice — The Necessity of Intermediaries

Salih then led Abu Malik through a formal argument. Can the One of unimaginable immensity be perceived by our vision? "No, He is far above that." Can we hear His speech? "No, for speech comes from a speaker described by his speech, and Allah is beyond such attributes." Then would this All-Embracing, Exalted One be unjust to His creatures — imposing what they cannot bear, commanding what they cannot perceive, then punishing them? "Anyone who imposes such an impossible obligation cannot be just," Abu Malik admitted; "and Allah is far exalted above that."

This is the backbone of the book's da'wa argument: (1) Allah cannot be seen or heard directly; (2) He is not unjust; (3) therefore He cannot impose obligations without a just witness to communicate them; (4) therefore a living Ḥujja must exist in every age. Salih built it step by step: "Would the One removed from injustice be pleased with ignorance as a companion for His servants? Did He create them as perfect scholars, or ignorant? Then how did they receive the knowledge of the gratitude desired from them — so that they could earn His pleasure and amnesty from His wrath? Such knowledge could not be perceived except through *intermediaries* between Him and His creatures. Their existence is His act of justice, for and against His creation. He selected them from His knowledge, purified them above the rest, sent them as messengers and arguments, and made them just witnesses between Him and His servants. Willing obedience to them becomes part of obeying Allah; disobeying them entails His wrath. Otherwise, how else would the divine command extend from Allah to His creatures?"

Abu Malik affirmed: "This is the very foundation of justice." Salih then established that Allah's wise judgment is one and unchanging: He would not point out His command to one group through a just witness and impose the same command on another without one — "for justice is unified by its nature; if a judgment had two meanings, one would be unjust." So the continuous succession of messengers necessarily imposes on every age the duty of willing obedience. These witnesses have ranks, one subordinate to another, yet all are equal in reward. And just as the superior messenger is distinguished by his knowledge of the unseen in the revealed book, the subordinate (the ⟪ar:وصی⟫) is distinguished by his knowledge of the unseen in his *esoteric interpretation* of the book — for both the revealed word and its interpretation come from Allah through revealed inspiration. The subordinate is called "subordinate" only because of his obedience to, and need for, his superior — as Allah's inspiration to ⟪ar:لوط⟫ came by means of ⟪ar:ابراھیم⟫, and to ⟪ar:اسماعیل⟫ and ⟪ar:اسحاق⟫, while the blessing and excellence were assigned to Ibrahim. "In His justice, He does not like His commands to be cut off from His servants after His blessing to them, for that would stop their worship and legitimize ignorance."

### The Conspiracy Formula — How Communities Went Astray

Abu Malik then asked the decisive question: if Allah obligated obedience to the prophets of resolution and their inspired interpreters, "what is the matter with these people who sit in their assemblies pretending to resemble the prophets of resolution, agreeing that they alone uphold Allah's command? How is it that the learned scholars of this community have abandoned inquiry?"

Salih answered with a historical-sociological analysis — the conspiracy formula. "They no longer inquire as they should, because it has been imbued in their hearts that they are the true possessors of Allah's command, and that there will be no prophet after their prophet. This is exactly what was said by those who strayed before them, for every community claims there is no prophet after its own." He named the mechanism: "Every period has started with three types: a rebellious devil, a willing oppressor, and a hypocritical religious scholar." By propagating "no prophet after our prophet," these three cut off the outward traces of prophecy from its living reality so they could exploit those traces as a commodity. They insinuated into their communities that no one superior to their own prophet would ever come — so the people abandoned seeking true religion without even realizing it, became attached to their own prophet while denying those who followed, and even imagined this denial drew them closer to him. Thus the devils who lead astray became established, the oppressors gained power to kill the prophets, and the evil scholars made it all attractive.

Salih traced the same formula across communities: the Zoroastrians cling to their fire-temples, "declaring that Allah will never send a messenger after Zoroaster"; the community of the Torah tend to their calf and submit to their judges, "declaring that Allah will never send a messenger after Moses"; the community of the Gospels venerate their priests and worship their prophet as God, "declaring that Allah will never send a messenger after Jesus"; and "now your community has followed in the ways of those who preceded them." They inherited the book from those unworthy of it, followed the seductions of their leaders, established prayers at the wrong times, and paid alms to the undeserving — all while proclaiming that Allah would never send a messenger after their messenger. "That is how you and those like you followed the great majority, terrified by scholarly consensus and the tyranny of rulers."

Salih then turned the consensus argument back: "If we must acknowledge this community on the basis of its consensus that Allah has cut off His proofs, then the earlier communities are even more deserving of acknowledgment, on three points: they preceded you in adhering to Allah's religion; you confirm the truth of what their prophets brought, but they confirmed it before you; and you all attest that they followed the true messengers, while none of them attest to your prophet. So they hold precedence through your mutual consensus, while you stand alone. — However, if the Creator is the One who judges wisely, abrogating and confirming as He wills, then everything these groups say collapses; for He has more right to create and command, and is engaged in a new work every day — so His actions could not be denied even if He sent a warner every day."

### The Unbroken Chain — There Was No "Interval"

Abu Malik, accepting that Allah's argument continues through His just witnesses, asked how to reconcile this with the apparent absence of prophets in their own time — comparing it to the "intervals" between Ibrahim and Moses, or Moses and Jesus. Salih refuted the very notion of an interval, listing the continuous chain by name:

"What 'interval' was there after the community of Ibrahim, when Allah sent after him ⟪ar:اسماعیل، اسحاق، یعقوب، یونس، شعیب⟫ leading to Moses? At that time He revealed to them the commands for good action, calling to the community of Ibrahim, upholding prayer and giving charity — until He sent down the Torah upon Moses. And what 'interval' was there after Moses, before the Gospel, since after Moses Allah sent ⟪ar:یوشی بن نون، الیاس، داؤد، سلیمان، طالوط، زکریا⟫ and ⟪ar:یحیی⟫, leading up to ⟪ar:عیسی⟫? They all upheld the judgment of the Torah, worshiping by it and reciting its psalms, until He sent down the Gospel upon Jesus." For Allah only said: ⟪ar:يَٓأَهْلَ ٱلْكِتَبِ قَدْ جَآءَكُمْ رَسُولُنَا يُبَيِّنُ لَكُمْ عَلَىٰ فَتْرَةٍۢ مِّنَ ٱلرُّسُلِ⟫ — "O People of the Scripture, there has come to you Our Messenger to make clear to you after a *fatra* (period) of messengers" (5:19). "In this verse, the 'interval' refers to the public manifestation of the divine call after a period of fear, when they were calling people to Allah in secret. The earth is never without a just witness from Allah for the blink of an eye — either publicly and openly, or in fear and concealment."

### Why the Friends of Allah Are in Concealment

Abu Malik asked the meaning of their absence in the present time. Salih answered with the example of Moses, who fled and hid from the people of ⟪ar:فرعون⟫, crossing to the land of ⟪ar:مدین⟫ and the city of the prophet ⟪ar:شعیب⟫, because he feared for his life. "Have you heard of any of Allah's just witnesses, the descendants of His prophets, who were murdered while persevering in their role?" "Yes," said Abu Malik, "a great many." "Then which of them would you want to reveal himself to the people of your time? A martyr resurrected after death? Or the one fleeing from you, while you seek to bring him to the death he is fleeing? So Allah's just witness follows the accustomed way of His prophets — persevering under persecution, and remaining hidden until Allah judges between him and us."

The concealment, then, is caused not only by external enemies but by the community's own passivity. Salih laid out the precise typology of the three categories of people in relation to the Imam's hiddenness: "There is the relentless murderer, who never tires of hunting them down; their deep-seated enemy, who bites his nails in rage and never forgets his hatred; and finally, those who dislike the enemies' actions and are sympathetic to the friends of Allah, *even though they abandon them* — who have no spiritual insight regarding faith, and cannot be relied upon to protect the secret of the imams. It is because of this third category that the friends of Allah have concealed themselves from you." This shifts moral responsibility onto the sympathetic abandoners — the community itself.

### Oppression, Repentance, and Conclusion

Salih then confronted Abu Malik with the complicity of the scholars who serve the oppressors: those who "blacken a flag or fill an inkwell" for the murderers of Allah's witnesses share in the bloodshed — indeed, the legal scholar who passes opinions enabling the oppressors is worse than the murderer, "for were it not for the opinions of these legal scholars, the oppressors could not maintain their dominion over the foolish." He cited ⟪ar:وَلَا تَرْكَنُوٓا۟ إِلَى ٱلَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا۟ فَتَمَسَّكُمُ ٱلنَّارُ⟫ — "Do not incline toward those who do wrong, lest the Fire touch you" (11:113); ⟪ar:أَطِيعُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُوا۟ ٱلرَّسُولَ وَأُو۟لِى ٱلْأَمْرِ مِنكُمْ⟫ — "Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you" (4:59), warning that the wrongdoers had been falsely treated as "those in authority"; and ⟪ar:قَتِلُوا۟ ٱلَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُم مِّنَ ٱلْكُفَّارِ⟫ — "Fight those who reject Allah, even if they are close to you" (9:123). "You willfully disobeyed the one Allah commanded you to obey, and willingly obeyed those you were commanded to struggle against — all while assuming you were among those doing good."

Salih then offered the path of return: "If you were all to help the friends of Allah, they would reveal themselves; and if you were to abandon their enemies, they would not be victorious." Abu Malik acknowledged that this was the true description of his community, and asked how to find salvation. "With Allah," replied Salih. "Seek protection with Him and He will give you refuge; turn to Him in repentance and He will accept it; ask Him for the path of His friends and He will rescue you through your own righteous actions. For Allah does not do an atom's weight of wrong, and does not hasten to punish even those who hasten to disobey Him. He loves those who turn to Him in repentance." Abu Malik turned to Allah in repentance, seeking the means to please Him.

Salih's eyes filled with tears at the mention of repentance, and he concluded his speech. He consulted his spiritual father, the scholar, about Abu Malik and his companions. The scholar advised: "You know your companions better than I. If you recognize good in them, guide them as you were guided; but if you are apprehensive, test them by ignoring them — not harshly — and order your father to treat them kindly during their period of trial. For the one truly seeking good will not remain hidden." So Abu Malik and his companions passed through their levels of trial until their affair was complete and they recognized their right guidance; and it was Allah's friend among them who took care of their guidance. They returned to their people, warning them, and through them Allah guided a great many of His servants to His religion.

"This was not a fictitious story, but rather the confirmation of what Allah has commanded. For in it is the confirmation of the divine messengers, the signs of their trustees (the imams), and the proper behavior of those who are seeking." (This is the seal of the whole work: the book is read as a *model* — a replicable da'wa arc from individual conversion to community-building — not merely as a story. The scholar finds the disciple; the disciple meets the Sheikh; he returns home and converts his father; father and son convert Abu Malik; Abu Malik and his group convert their community — each phase a stage in the listener's own potential journey.)

So praise be to Allah, in the beginning and at the end. May Allah's blessings be upon His Messenger, our master Muhammad, sent to His creatures as a bearer of good news and a warner; and upon his trustee, the imam of those who are mindful of Allah; and upon the imams from the people of his house upon whom Allah has bestowed His favors — may He take from them all impurity and purify them entirely. And Allah is sufficient for us, the best of trustees and the best of supporters. ⟪ar:ولا حول ولا قوۃ الا باللہ العلی العظیم⟫ — And there is no strategy and no strength except through Allah, the Exalted, the Powerful.
