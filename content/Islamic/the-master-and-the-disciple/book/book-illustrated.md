# The Book of the Master and the Boy

## How to Read a Conversation Made of Doors

What you are about to read is a conversation. Not a treatise, not a sermon, not a catechism — a conversation, set down in writing more than a thousand years ago, between a teacher and the seekers who came to him, and then, inside it, another conversation between a learned man of Persia and a boy who walked up to him one day and asked a question he did not expect. I am the one who recorded it. My name will mean little to you; what should mean something to you is that I believed, then as I believe now, that the most important things a human being can learn are almost never delivered as lectures. They arrive in exchanges. They arrive through a door someone opens by asking.

I want to tell you, before you step in, what kind of room you are entering. The believers and the preachers who first approached the Master did not come asking for information. They came already grateful — and worried about their gratitude. They told him, in effect: *you have set us free by what you made us see, and now we owe you a debt on three counts at once.* Thanks for the call itself, the invitation. Thanks for the knowledge you placed in our hands. Thanks for the work you laid upon us. They wanted to know how a person actually pays a debt like that. They wanted, too, the etiquette of the road: what is owed by the one who asks a question, and what is owed by the one who answers it; how the righteous have walked this path before; how a seeker is supposed to carry himself.

The Master's answer is the seed of everything that follows, and I will tell it to you plainly, because the rest of the book is an unfolding of it. He said that this matter — the matter to which he had summoned them — was the one God Himself has honored above every other, completed for His servants, and dignified those who answered Him in it. He said that God has arranged reality so that every first thing has a last, and every last has an end, and every end opens onto a further end whose horizon no one reaches. These, he said, are the dwellings of the people of understanding — the *houses of the hearts*. Their keys are remembrance. The first of them is trial. The middle of them is guidance. The last of them is piety. Whoever has wanted to know how things are built, and has stood squarely on the limits of what is required of him, has already aimed at guidance along the markings of the search, and is walking the road in the best manner there is.

> شُكْرُ الْعَالِمِ طَاعَتُهُ، وَشُكْرُ الْعِلْمِ الْعَمَلُ بِهِ وَالدَّعْوَةُ إِلَيْهِ، وَشُكْرُ الْعَمَلِ الصَّبْرُ عَلَيْهِ وَالدَّعْوَةُ إِلَيْهِ.
>
> "Thanks to the teacher is to obey him. Thanks to the knowledge is to act upon it and to call others to it. Thanks to the work is to be patient with it and to call others to it."

Notice what he has done. He has turned every form of gratitude outward. None of the three thanks is something you feel; each of them is something you do, and two of the three end in the same place — *calling others*. Gratitude, in his mouth, is contagion. A debt you pay by passing the gift along.

This is the doorway. The rest of the book — the Persian man whose heart was sound but whose inheritance was ignorance, the parched traveler who mistook the shimmer of the desert for water until he stumbled upon God instead and was given his reckoning in full, the father's single sentence that the son carried like his own soul in his body — *that the best of good deeds is the giving of life to the dead* — the boy who walks up and asks his question, the patient unfolding of how a teacher receives a stranger and how a stranger becomes a son — all of it is the Master's three lines being lived out, by real people, in real rooms, with real consequences.

I am bringing this conversation across to you, across the centuries and across a language, because the questions inside it have not aged. You are still a person who has been given something — a teacher, a book, a moment of seeing — and you are still trying to work out what you owe for it. You are still, whether you know it or not, looking for the houses of your own heart, and you have not yet found all the keys. This book is a conversation made of doors. Read it slowly. Walk through them in order. And when you reach the last one, do for someone else what was done here for you.

## 1. The Persian Who Was Dead and Revived

Let me tell you now about the doctrines of the righteous and the manners of those who seek — and let me tell it through one man, because a single life carried well will teach you more than a hundred maxims.

Word reached us of a man from among the people of Persia who had been struck, early, by the calamity that I have been describing to you: the calamity of ignorance. Do not picture a dull man. He had a heart that held what it was given, an intellect that ran true, and a refinement that he himself had labored to acquire. None of that saved him. Ignorance, in the particular sense we have been speaking of, had simply prevailed in him — it had reached him before knowledge did, and he had grown up inside it. So he lived as a man lives who is staggering through heat, mistaking the shimmer at the edge of the plain for the whiteness of water. He kept walking toward it. And when he arrived where the water had seemed to be, there was nothing — and there, instead, was God, waiting, the reckoning already prepared. Then his Lord honored the place he had been brought to. He lifted the covering off him. He found him astray and guided him; He found him destitute and made him rich, not with property but with knowledge. And by that grace he became one of the scholars of the Mount, one of the dwellers of the Frequented House.

When his guidance was complete, and he had reached the furthest point of what he had ever hoped for himself, a second obligation rose up in him — the obligation of thanks, and the obligation to keep striving toward the One who had given. Now he had heard, long ago, a single sentence from his father, and that sentence had stayed with him the way the soul stays with the body. It was this:

> إِنَّ أَفْضَلَ الْحَسَنَاتِ إِحْيَاءُ الْأَمْوَاتِ
>
> "The best of all good deeds is to bring the dead back to life."

He turned it over inside himself and said:

> قَدْ كُنْتُ مَيِّتًا فَأَحْيَانِي، وَجَاهِلًا فَعَلَّمَنِي
>
> "I was dead, and He revived me; I was ignorant, and He taught me."

And then he reasoned with himself the way an honest man reasons. I was not, he said, the first human being to be ignorant — so I cannot pretend that I am the one through whom knowledge enters the world. And I am not the last — so I cannot pretend that teaching is permitted to end with me. It falls on me, then, to be one of those who give thanks for this blessing by carrying the trust forward, handing it to whoever comes after me, exactly as it was handed to me by those who came before. For this matter began with God; it did not arrive at my door except through a long chain of causes — the first delivering to the second, the second to the third — descending from the highest assembly down toward the people of this world. The veil was breached for it. Door after door was pushed open for it. And at last it reached me. Am I, then, its destination? Am I where it stops? No. The ones who carried it and were faithful to it have the older claim on its merit; their reward is theirs, not mine to lean upon. I am simply the one who profited from their labor. And the fact that they were generous does not exempt me from working — I am as poor in front of the obligation to act on knowledge as I once was in front of the obligation to seek it. He understood, in that hour, that what was owed by him was the mirror of what had been given to him: his last command, in the divine accounting, was identical in weight to his first.

So he left. He left his family and his wealth and walked out toward his Lord — answering, supplicating, calling others toward the same good, intending that the thanks he owed should be paid in the only currency that fits such a debt. He moved through the peoples of the non-Arab lands and through the tribes of the Arabs, studying faces, putting his questions to whoever would hear them. No one answered. No fellow seeker came forward to meet him. He kept going until he reached the far edge of the peninsula, and there he entered a city at an hour when its people were inattentive to themselves. While he was still recollecting the signs and reading the marks of the place, his eye fell on a group of its inhabitants in the middle of an argument — quarreling about religion without any guide to steer them, plunging about in the surf of their own opinions. He did not interrupt them. He sat down a little way off, by himself, and listened, watching their faces, trying to read which of them was closest to the right road.

## 2. A Stranger in the City

I watched them a moment longer and said to myself: these are the ones most alive of anyone I have met on the road, and for three reasons. The first is that they bother to make an argument at all — they are trying to carry a case across to one another, which is more than most. The second is that they are of the people of this religion; whatever their confusion, the family is the right family. And the third is that they are turned toward the matter together — looking at it together, hunting it together, disagreeing about it because they care what the answer is. And the one who is hunting a thing is already close to finding it. So I waited.

When their argument ran itself out, they turned and noticed me. One of them said, "Who are you, boy, and where are you from?"

I said:

> أَنَا عَبْدُ اللَّهِ، وَمِنْ سُكَّانِ حَرَمِهِ.
>
> "I am a servant of God, and one of the dwellers of His sanctuary."

They said, "What is your need, then, and what is your work?"

I said, "My need has already passed me by. The work I am at now is the work of seeking it."

They said, "Did anything in what we were saying just now please you?"

I said:

> كُلُّهُ عَجِيبٌ لِمَنْ يَتَعَجَّبُ.
>
> "All of it is a wonder — to one who knows how to wonder."

They said, "But wonder has two senses, you know — there is wonder that is praiseworthy and wonder that is to be condemned."

I said, "Yes, I know that. And speech is the same — praiseworthy and condemnable."

They said, "Then what, in your view, is the praiseworthy kind?"

I said, "The kind that is correct."

They said, "And what is the correct kind?"

I said:

> مَا كَانَ أَوَّلُهُ مِنَ اللَّهِ وَدُعِيَ بِهِ إِلَى اللَّهِ.
>
> "Speech whose beginning is from God and which calls toward God."

They said, "And the condemnable?"

I said:

> مَا كَانَ أَوَّلُهُ هَوًى وَدُعِيَ بِهِ إِلَى غَيْرِ الْهُدَى.
>
> "Speech whose beginning is appetite and which calls toward anything other than guidance."

They said, "You speak truly. Will you not let us hear something of your own?"

I said, "I have nothing of my own to say. I am only a follower of the saying that is already true."

They said, "Then at least let us hear, in your words, what your way is. Your bearing has moved us, and we would be glad to count you a brother. We are hoping you may be a door of mercy God has opened for us."

I said yes. I sat up straight to speak, and the people fell silent for me, and I turned my face full toward them.

Then I said:

> إِنَّ الَّذِي خَلَقَ بِقُدْرَتِهِ فَسَوَّى، وَقَدَّرَ بِجُودِهِ فَهَدَى، هُوَ الَّذِي أَخْرَجَ شَوَارِعَ الْمَرْعَى.
>
> "He who created by His power and gave each thing its measure, who apportioned by His generosity and so guided — He is the One who has laid out the highways of the pasture."

He made the outward of His way a thing in plain view, and the inward a hidden green. The road is not a tangle, and its signs are not worn away — if it were, imaginations would lose their hold trying to reach Him, and minds would give up on ever arriving. He is not so exposed in being that those who do not belong to Him could come into possession of Him, or so accessible that the line of His way could be redrawn by anyone who pleased. He is generous, but veiled. His knowledge is hidden precisely because of its preciousness. His secrets are guarded for the household they belong to. Their adornment is the plumage of guidance; their full dress is the garment of God-consciousness, marked with the light of lights — a trust deposited in the breasts of the righteous, the keepers, the noble scribes:

> أَمَانَةً يُؤَدُّونَهَا، وَحُدُودًا لَا يَتَعَدَّوْنَهَا.
>
> "A trust they discharge, and limits they do not overstep."

This is the law of the Judge and the gifts of the Merciful. He has honored that trust by concealing it; He has guarded it by veiling it; He has made it content with its own rights, and crowned it with His covenants. So the ambitions of the speculators cannot reach it, and the schemes of the cunning cannot arrive at it. Its outward is a reminder and a sign for the guided. Its inward is a clear drink — its sky bends low for those who would gaze, and its ground is paved for those who would search; even its falling stars have known stations for those who are looking. He has watering-places for those who travel to Him, and signs whose wonders come one after another, pointing to Him by their likenesses. His path is preferred above every other path. His method is set out in full detail. His rulings are obligations. His Sunna is a body of law. Truthfulness has ennobled the doctrines of that law, and justice has made clean the gains it permits. For every good thing He has provided a leader and a road; and He has made His path itself both a shelter and a guide into its inner chamber. Then He pressed minds — by their ignorance, by their need — to come out seeking knowledge and seeking room to live. And:

> تَبَارَكَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ اللَّيْلَ وَالنَّهَارَ خِلْفَةً لِّمَنْ أَرَادَ أَن يَذَّكَّرَ أَوْ أَرَادَ شُكُورًا.
>
> "Blessed is He who made the night and the day a succession, for whoever would remember, or would give thanks."

And may God's blessing rest upon the one He chose from among His servants and set as a warning to the worlds.

Then I said:

> اتَّقُوا اللَّهَ تَقْوَى مَنْ يَخَافُهُ، وَخَافُوهُ خَوْفَ مَنْ يَرْجُوهُ. وَاطْلُبُوا الْعِلْمَ تَعْلَمُوا، وَاعْمَلُوا تُرْحَمُوا.
>
> "Fear God with the fear of one who is afraid of Him; fear Him with the fear of one who is also hoping in Him. Seek knowledge — you will come to know. Act — and you will be shown mercy."

And as I said it my eyes filled and overflowed, and the words broke off in my throat. I said my farewell to them through tears and rose to go back to where I was lodging. Weeping broke over the whole gathering at the heat of what had come out and at the weight of the counsel; they began to stand up to come with me. I waved them off — it was a matter of the courtesy owed to such a moment, that they not press in on me all together. They did not quite know what I needed of them in that instant, and so they went back to their houses. But one of them did follow me — the youngest of them in years and the steadiest of them in temper, a boy whose upbringing had finished the work of forming his mind, and in whose heart reflection had already struck its spark — and on top of all that there was now what he had just heard.

## 3. The Boy at the Door — Limits and Conditions

He came along behind me with my permission, and I let him keep my pace until I reached the place where I was staying. I brought him in with me, called for food, and when we had eaten and the room had quieted enough for talk, he was the first to speak.

"Wise one," he said, "you spoke and I heard, you preached and the words went home. You set the religion in its finest ornament and made it desirable in the noblest way, you summoned us to it under the noblest of its trusts, and you made the mind in me ache for the question — by the cracks your words struck in our hearts. Such an excellence could only show itself in one whose inward life has been gathered up into a teacher, and whose outward life has grown wide enough to hold it. That kind of man is a wellspring of wisdom from a fixed root, his shade lies across the worlds, and the branches of his branches bear fruit for whoever comes asking. I have hopes that you are one of those blessed branches — for what I can see in you of clear judgment, sound substance, and a finished mind. You said yourself, in front of all of them, that minds are driven by their own ignorance to seek knowledge and the room knowledge makes. Mine is among the neediest of them and the most destitute. Is there a way from where I am to anything like a life?"

I answered him: "The One who made the creatures by His power did not make them for nothing. He did not bring them out of the womb already scholars, and He did not abandon them in the back-country of ignorance to wander. He was not content that stupidity should be their breeding, nor the rags of ignorance their clothes. He laid down for them a road out of all that — the very instrument by which He composed them and finished their making. Hearing, sight, the heart — He opened them with lights, as a dignity to them from Him, and as an argument He could one day hold against them. Then He placed the treasures of His wisdom and the marks of His own preference inside certain doctrines that please Him in the elect of His creation. He charged those elect to carry the religion and to call others into it, and He imposed on the rest the answering of the call by means of that same instrument He gave them — so that He could sort the foul from the good. The one who seeks shows himself by his seeking; the one who lags shows himself by his lagging. After the Messengers, no one has an argument left against God, and after the proofs, no one has an excuse for going astray."

"The lack of any excuse," he said, "has frightened me, and the existence of the proofs has made me want it. I am asking you to be my guide, and I will be a servant at your feet."

"God did not create men as scholars," I said. "He created them as little children, ignorant ones who knew nothing. There is no greatness except after smallness, and there is no scholar except after ignorance. Smallness is the foreword to greatness; ignorance is the road-sign to knowledge. And knowledge itself cannot be carried whole into a mind — it must be brought in by a knowledge that goes before it, something quicker into the heart than the thing itself, something smoother for the understanding to swallow, so that the first imaginings can take hold, the limbs can be broken in to the obligations, the ribs can shudder at the depth of it, and the mind can finally bear the end the lesson is moving toward. This is God's way with His servants. We have no right to step outside that way with our own opinion, and we have no right to be led by anything other than that way."

"Then I am too small," he said, "for what I want — so raise me. I am ignorant of the thing I am asking for — so teach me. I am hard pressed for your kindness — so have mercy on me. You were once where I am now, and God brought the favor to you through the hands of someone whose trial in you was great and whose thanks were obligatory upon you. You know what it is to be inside the narrowness of ignorance, and to feel a needy man's poverty of any way out."

"The state you describe," I said, "is indeed the one I was in, and I am not stingy with you about it. But my mercy toward you is not going to be the kind that lets hope outrun work. Every kind of speech has its sorting — only what is truthful passes through. So with knowledge: it gives no benefit until it is acted on, and the act gives no benefit until it is received. The truthfulness of speech is its justice; the proof of knowledge is the open roads its actions take, and the agreements with the standing examples — the ones held across the whole compass of the world and tested by the alternation of night and day. Do not chase an argument as if it were a stray. Do not throw one away as if it were nothing. Do not take one on by imitation without a reason — the first whisper of doubt will tear it out of your heart. Do not refuse one after the proof has stood up under it — you will end up an advocate for the false. And do not abandon the inquiry and the deep look at it — your opinion will go barren. Think well of me without flattery. Press your case without suspicion. Seek the religion the way one like you ought to seek it, and ask God's help in your business. God does not waste the wage of one who seeks, and He does not strip the reward off a man He sees going after the good."

"You have done right by yourself in beautiful action," he said, "and pointed the way to the truth with the best of speech. I will ask God's help. Now I ask you about the thing you are calling me to: what is it, whose is it, and over whom does it hold?"

"It is the religion of God," I said, "the one He has accepted. It is from Him, so He will accept nothing in its place. He gave it first to the elite among His Messengers, and through them, in the wider sense, to all His creation. On the one specially favored, three things fall: thanks for the favor, the practice of it, the call to others to come to it. On the one cut off from it, two things fall: the seeking, until he knows, and obedience to whoever guides him."

"This favor God has marked out for His friends," he said, "is it one thing in all its forms — are they all equal in excellence — or do some of them outrank others?"

"Did we not already say," I told him, "that every form of knowledge has a knowledge behind it that ushers it in, and that every favor has its bearers, so that they stand in ranks before their Lord, each one charged with what he has earned in knowing? And then each man of merit is given his merit — by way of God's justice with His creatures and His mercy on His servants."

"This thing you are calling me to," he said, "is it that favor itself, or something other than it?"

"It is neither the favor in its fullness," I said, "nor something other than it. It is from it — like a single cupped handful of water from the width of the Euphrates. I am calling you to what God has accepted for His servants and honored on the day He completed it. It is precious, guarded, generous, kept hidden. He has dignified it with reward as a tribute to the one who upholds it, and He has shamed it with punishment as a humiliation to the one who lets it slip. Its outward face is markings, its inward face is sciences; its rule is lit from within, and its justice is plain. This is what I am calling you to."

"Then what is it," he said, "that makes you more entitled to this affair than anyone else, and more deserving of it than the others? You yourself say it is from God for the whole of creation — and yet every group claims it for itself, against every other. What is it that makes you, more than them, deserve the name of truth — since this matter did not come to you by some road that bypassed them?"

"There is no scholar anywhere," I said, "who speaks one true word that he has not drawn from the same origins we drew ours from. In that, they are with us — they confess it from their side, we confess it from ours. What divided us from them was that they parted from those origins and we held to them, that we obeyed where they disobeyed, that we kept what we were entrusted with where they squandered it, that we stood up for what we were charged with where they let it fall. They agreed with us in confession and broke from us in deed. Their confession is a witness for us, against them. Their squandering is the sign of their poverty toward us, and of their need for what is in our hands. They are like brothers who inherited a great fortune from their father and divided it equally between themselves — and some of them wasted their share until they fell into want, while others kept theirs, protected it, traded with it, and the wealth in their hands doubled. So the poor among the brothers come, in the end, to the rich among them, to serve them and to ask for a little of what they have. So it is with those who lost the road their Messengers laid down: their ignorance drives them to come asking for knowledge at the door of the people of the noble origins. The proof that we are more entitled to the truth than they are is their need of us and our being able to do without them. Our scholars answer the call of their ignorant; our cloak covers their bareness; our plenty feeds their hunger. So the truth is due to the man of merit, the credit is due to every bearer of a blessing, and the thanks are due to every doer of good."

"Your superiority over them," he said, "I see now, and the argument from the truth has stood up against any honest man among them. But what is your argument against me — when I already grant what you have said, hold to what you have described, deny what you have denied, and know the truth and its merit, and the false and its people?"

"My argument against you," I said, "is the same as my argument against them — because you differ from them only in description, and you agree with them in fact."

"How can that be," he said, "when I am acknowledging the truth to its owner and they are denying it?"

"If you parted from them by acknowledging where they denied," I said, "you have come back to them by withholding where they withheld. Acknowledgment without surrender does no good, and denial after the proof does no harm — to the proof."

"Then take me to surrender," he said, "to the people of the truth — let me know them, since I have recognized them — or to a deputy who stands in for them. Show me which of the two is open to you."

"Do you not see," I said, "that you have been forced to need a proof of who actually holds the truth, or of his deputy — just as the wider public has been forced to need a proof of the truth itself? Your action has divided you from them; your ignorance has joined you to them."

"You are right," he said. "Make it plain."

"Have you not already granted that my argument has stood up against you, and against the public?"

"I have," he said.

"And can anyone prove for himself a truth that belongs to someone else?"

"No one can do that," he said.

"Then the argument is over against you, and over against the public. Nothing is left but to submit, and to turn back to the one you cannot do without and who can do without you."

"My poverty," he said, "I confess. As for the one who is rich and does not need me — I am asking after Him. Have mercy on me."

"Have you ever seen anyone driven by his poverty to a man of means," I said, "get anything from him except by earning it or by being given it as a favor?"

"No," he said. "It is one of the two — earning or favor."

"And do the men of favor receive anything from the share of the men who earn?"

"No," he said.

"Then seek this thing by the work of it, and you will come into it as something you have earned. Do not seek it by flattery and come into it as a charity — that is the road to being cut off."

"You are the key to the good," he said, "and its guide. Show me the more meaningful of the two roads, and the nearer one to guidance. Great need has tied me up at your door."

"My son," I said, "there is no distance for you here, and no rebuke against you. What you have hoped for from me will reach you. But there are limits not to be crossed, and conditions not to be forgotten."

"Set down whatever limits you love," he said, "and whatever conditions you see fit. I hope you will find me patient under what you put on me, and grateful for the kindness."

"The first of the limits," I said, "is to hold up the outward face of the Book and its laws, and to act on what you already know of it. For whoever acts for God on what he knows, God will guide him to what he does not.

> مَنْ عَمِلَ بِمَا عَلِمَ وَرَّثَهُ اللَّهُ عِلْمَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ
>
> Whoever acts upon what he knows, God will bequeath to him the knowledge of what he does not know."

"But the Books are many," he said, "and all of them are from God, and each of them has, from among His creatures, a scholar who holds to it — and all of those scholars agree on protecting their Book and living by its religion."

"We have come back to where we started," I said, "and we are being made to settle a thing that should not have needed settling."

"How is that?"

"From your saying that the Books are many, and all of them from God, and each has its own scholar — do you imagine that God's words and God's Books cancel one another out, that the first of them gives the lie to the last, and the last gives the lie to the first?"

"I do not imagine that," he said. "But what is the proof?"

"If the creatures had done what was in the first Book," I said, "it would have pointed them to the second. If they had done what was in the second, it would have pointed them to the third — so that the working through ends, of necessity, at the last of them, because the last is what the earlier ones were already pointing to. All of them are from God, yes — but the later is the closer to the present age, and the clearer in its road, because it stands as the abrogator of what came before it, and nothing has come after it to abrogate it."

"You have spoken the truth," he said, "and made the knowledge of the truth, and the mark of truthfulness, both plain. I will keep the limits you have ordered me to keep. What are your conditions on me?"

"My conditions on you," I said, "are five. Do not fail me when I send for you. Do not hide anything from me when I ask you. Do not press me until I answer you. Do not request anything until I have begun it with you. And do not mention any of our business to your father."

"All of that I will do," he said, "and it is easy on me, set against the size of what I am hoping from you. But what is the way through, with my father? He is one of the heaviest of the trials laid on me, and one of the most testing. Will you give me leave to move away from him?"

"Withdrawing from him," I said, "would be a sign to anyone watching. Stay where you are. Honor him. Please him with a measure of outward agreement. Keep your secret kept and your business hidden. God will be enough for us against his harm and the harm of anyone else."

For a long stretch of our lives after that, the boy and I met and parted, met and parted. He was kept in a kind of perplexity about his own affair — he knew nothing past what the law had brought him to the threshold of, he did not know where I was settled, and under the condition I had laid on him, he could not come looking. The absence ran long. His patience and his endurance for the cost of it showed me what he was. And at last, on a quiet night, when the world had gone still and the eyes of the watchers had grown drowsy, we were alone in our sitting together — and his need pressed close on him, and he saw that his moment had come. He went down in a prostration. When he raised his head, I said to him:

"I saw you prostrate in thanks. Thanks given before the thing is done makes the gift it is given for suspect, and thanks cut off after the thing is done cancels the very acts it was meant to honor."

"Every word of yours is true," he said, "and every act of yours is mastery. What I was thanking was the kindness that has already come from you, and what I was hoping, by the thanks, was for more of it from your side."

"Thanks does have the right," I said, "to draw down more on those who give it. Your right has come due. Now turn your heart toward me, bring your understanding fully present, and listen to what I am laying before you."

"I have brought the instrument of understanding," he said, "and I have taken up the instrument of hearing. Say what seems good to you."

"Religion has a key," I said, "that opens it and shuts it — the way the distinction between unlawful union and lawful marriage opens one and shuts the other."

"Such a key is a great thing in God's sight," he said, "if it is what separates the lawful from the forbidden, and the truth from the false. What is it?"

"It is the covenant of God," I said —

> عَهْدُ اللَّهِ الْمُؤَكَّدُ لِحُقُوقِهِ، الْجَامِعُ لِفَرَائِضِهِ، الْجُنَّةُ لِأَوْلِيَائِهِ، حَبْلُ اللَّهِ فِي أَرْضِهِ، وَأَمَانُهُ بَيْنَ عِبَادِهِ
>
> "the covenant of God, sealed for His rights, gathering up His obligations, the shield of His friends, the rope of God on His earth, His safe haven among His servants."

"I will recite it to you, and I will take it upon you."

"Yes," he said. "Take whatever you love from me. I am not refusing your judgment, and I am not stepping outside your way."

Then I began to recite the covenant to him, calling it out word by word and binding him by it, and the boy could not hold himself together from awe — his tears coming down hard from the depth of what was being put into him — until I came with him to the end of it. He praised God, and he honored Him, and he gave thanks for what had reached him. And he knew, with a certainty in him now, that by accepting their pledge he had come inside the Party of God,

> حِزْبُ اللَّهِ
>
> "the Party of God,"

and into the company of His friends. Then he fell still — composed — and I began to set out the explanation and the unfolding of it.

## 4. How the World Was Made

And so I began at the beginning — not the beginning he might have expected, not the day his own life started or the day the letter was first put into a seeker's hand, but the beginning before any of that. The beginning of the world. Because a pledge to walk a road means little until you have seen the country the road runs through; and the country we were entering was nothing less than the made order itself, the whole architecture of what is.

Listen, I told him. God is the One who brings things into being and the One who arranges them. He made them and He started them. He is not the first of every first thing — as if some earlier creation had given Him a template to copy from, as if He needed a precedent. No. He is the First of every first, and the Inheritor of every thing that is. When everything is gone back into the ground He will still be standing. Hold that in your mind before we go further, because everything I am about to tell you opens out of it.

He began the work of creation out of light. And from that light, three words branched. The first was *will*. From will branched *command*. And from command branched *speech* — the saying, to whatever He wills, "Be," and it is.

> إِنَّمَا أَمْرُهُ إِذَا أَرَادَ شَيْئًا أَن يَقُولَ لَهُ كُن فَيَكُونُ
>
> "His command, when He wills a thing, is only that He says to it: Be — and it is."

So the first creation is a will, then a command, then a saying. Three words. And from those three words is derived *Kun* — two letters — and *fa-yakun* — five letters. Two and five: seven letters. And from seven letters, seven things branched into being.

From the light He brought forth, first, the air. The air is the eldest of them. It stands alone, without a partner, unique among the seven — it mingles with everything and surrounds everything, and nothing surrounds it. The air, in its very solitude, is a sign pointing back to the One concerning whom it is said —

> لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ
>
> "there is nothing like Him."

Hold that. The first created thing testifies, by what it is, to the One who is unlike anything.

Then, from those three words, He brought forth three more: water, darkness, and light — the light of the heavens and of the earth. And then a further generation, three from three. From water He drew out smoke. From the dense heat of darkness He drew out a thickening, a condensation. From the light He drew out fire. Three things branching from three things before them, and each of the second three became a consort to the one it had come from, because it had been drawn out of it. And over all of them, the seventh — the air — presides and contains. This is how creation began. This is the beginning of things.

When the creation had been set on these seven principles, and the way of forms had been laid down on the law of likeness and analogy — like answering to like, the visible echoing the invisible — He drew out, from the smoke, the seven heavens and their signs. And He drew out, from the condensation, the seven earths and their sustenance. Seven branches from seven roots. The order is exact.

Then we turn and look. What are the signs of the heavens, and what are the sustenances of the earth? What stands behind them? We come back, as we always come back, to those three words and their letters — *the will to command by saying*. Count them: twelve letters. And their signs in the heavens are the twelve constellations. Their witnesses are seven kingdoms. Their signs upon the earth are twelve islands. And their proofs are seventeen seas. These are the signs of the heavens and the earth — the ones people walk through every day —

> وَكَأَيِّن مِّنْ آيَةٍ فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ يَمُرُّونَ عَلَيْهَا وَهُمْ عَنْهَا مُعْرِضُونَ
>
> "and how many a sign there is in the heavens and the earth which they pass by, while they are turning away from it."

That is the heart of what I want you to grasp: the signs are not hidden in some far place. They are the ceiling overhead and the ground underfoot. People walk through them without seeing them.

Now consider what came next. The heavens and the earth were closed up, sewn together, veiled in a darkness that came up from the black heat — and that heat is from the dominion of the earth.

> أَوَلَمْ يَرَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَنَّ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ كَانَتَا رَتْقًا فَفَتَقْنَاهُمَا
>
> "Have those who disbelieved not seen that the heavens and the earth were a closed mass, then We tore them apart?"

He pierced that closed mass with His lights, and light and darkness — those two opposed realities — were threaded into each other. Then He separated them by means of two signs: the night and the day. He made the night, and gave it as its sign a *way* of darkness — a road through which darkness is apportioned. He made the day, and gave it as its sign a way of light — a road through which light is apportioned. He covered the night with its darkness, and gave it the moon and the stars as its signs. He brightened the day with its light, and gave it the sight of the sun as its sign. And out of the alternation of night and day, like a current passing back and forth between two poles, the days were born — seven of them, a rotation. And the sign of each day is twelve hours, and the sign of each night is twelve hours. Twelve again, and twelve. The same number keeps coming up because the same three words keep speaking.

When the creation of things had been built up on these principles, He made them pairs — male and female. Hear me carefully: I do not mean male and female the way a man and a woman are male and female. I mean it in the deeper sense — the active and the receptive, the giving side and the receiving side, the one that imprints and the one that takes the imprint. That is the pairing I am pointing at. And once that custom of pairing-in-creation was laid down, He brought forth from the essences of those pairs every living thing — humans, beasts, birds, the creatures that creep on the ground. The same custom then ran on into language itself, and there too things came in pairs: attributes and what they describe, names and meanings. Glory to the One who created all pairs —

> سُبْحَانَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ الْأَزْوَاجَ كُلَّهَا
>
> "Glory be to the One who created all pairs"

— and made them outward showings of His command, parables of His religion, evidences of His power. He points by means of created things to their Originator; He points by means of the made order to its Maker.

Then He pierced it with His light, opened it with His command, made it by His knowledge, and sealed it among His names with the letters of —

> بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ
>
> "In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful."

When He had completed the whole of it by His craft, and arranged it into pairs by His measure, He chose for Himself — out of the very choicest of His will, out of the inner secrets of His hidden things — a great knowledge and a clear light. He made those two a religion: a religion by which God is served, by which one draws near to Him. Then He honored that religion with revealed laws and with books, and He pointed to it through parables and through the sunna. And then — this is the part that joins everything I have been telling you to everything we are about to live — He set up, from among the choicest of His creation, banners and signs and rulers. He made them the houses of His prophecy, the treasuries of His knowledge and His wisdom, the interpreters of His revelation. He left them as His vicegerents in His earth, evidences of the road of salvation in both what is seen and what is hidden. Some of them are the imams and the speakers — the *natiqs* who give the revelation its voice. Some of them are the proofs and the chiefs — the *hujaj* and the *nuqaba*. And some of them are the summoners to the good and the learned — the *du'at* and the *ulama*. In their stations they are like the stars of heaven — the deeds rose up by them, and the parables found their measure in them.

Their imam is like the great sun among lights, hidden from sight by its own brilliance — by the veils thrown out by its own glory. He is their visible face and their likeness. The proof and the gate of their imam is like the lit moon — visible, likened to him. Their summoners are like the bright stars — visible, and likenesses of what stands behind them. Look up tonight, I told him, and you are looking at the diagram of the religion.

Now, every pair I have described to you has an outside and an inside, a figure and the thing the figure stands for. The outward of the seven heavens is what your eyes see; the inward of them is the seven Speakers — and the foremost of those Speakers are the messengers, the ones called *ulu'l-azm*, the bearers of resolve. The outward of the seven earths is parable; the inward of them is the imams of guidance. For every one of the Speakers there are twelve chiefs, and their likeness in the heavens is the twelve constellations. In the very same way, every imam of guidance has twelve proofs by which God is established upon his people — and their likeness in the earth is the twelve islands. Those islands are where the chiefs dwell. On every island a proof stands. And under every proof a number of summoners, many or few — He increases in creation what He wills, to make the proof reach —

> لِئَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلنَّاسِ عَلَى اللَّهِ حُجَّةٌ بَعْدَ الرُّسُلِ
>
> "so that the people may have no argument against God after the messengers."

That sentence is the spine of everything. The whole structure I have just walked you through — the seven words branching to seven and to seven again, the heavens and their constellations, the earths and their islands, the speakers and the imams and the proofs and the summoners — the whole of it exists so that no one can stand on the day of standing and say, *I was never told.* The proof has been laid down, layer on layer, sign on sign.

By these causes — the *asbab* of God I have been describing to you — the proof of God reaches His creation. His justice appears among them, outwardly and inwardly. The inward is the religion of God, the thing the friends of God have bound themselves to. The outward is the laws of that religion and the parables of it. So the religion becomes a soul and a spirit to the laws, and the laws become a body and a guide to the religion. There is no standing for a body without a soul — the soul is its life. And there is no standing for a soul without a body — the body is what carries it. And so it goes with the outward forms — the laws and everything outward — they have no standing except by means of the inward religion, because the inward is their light, their meaning, the very spirit of life inside them. And the inward has no standing except by means of the outward, because the outward is its body and its evidence. The knowledge of this world cannot be seen except through the outward; the knowledge of the next world cannot be seen except through the inward. Not one letter of the inward, not one of the friends of God, is without many witnesses in the outward — because the parables are many, and the laws are wide.

I tell you, the more one speaks about this, the more there is still to say. Every time wisdom answers, its truth steps further back, and its last word makes you forget its first — because of how precious it is, and how pure the substance of it. The lights of wisdom cover one another the way the light of the sun covers the light of the moon and the stars, and dims them. That is how the words of wisdom work. That is how they adorn the tongue of a wise man — one light folding into the next until the seeker forgets where he started, and finds himself standing inside the country whose road he had only meant to ask about.

## 5. The World, the Hereafter, and the Speech of Parables

So the boy, hearing this, did not flinch from it — he stepped further in. He had a question waiting that he had been carrying for some time, and he asked it now.

This world, he said — this world in which the great creation has been set down, peopled by multitudes, the first generations and the last, all of them disagreeing about what it is — what do you say of it? Some have given themselves to it entirely and chased after it and seen no merit except in piling up more of it. Others reproach it, practice abstention inside it, and yet cannot quite do without it — though all that is forbidden of it is much, and only a little of it is forbidden. From you I have heard no judgment on it at all. Where do you stand?

I told him: no one truly reproaches this world. Not the learned, not the ignorant. The ignorant man recognizes its worth by his very ignorance of anything beyond it, and the learned man recognizes its worth by the meaning he can read in it. Only an ignorant person ever truly censures the world — and how would it be permitted for a creature to censure the act of his Creator? Say rather that the world has been set up for the one who is willing to look at it, and what he is meant to see in it is seven things, each branching from seven origins: heaven, earth, darkness, light, vessels, prosperity, and misery. Seven qualities. And from each of those seven, seven more branch out.

The heavens are seven. The earths are seven.

The light moves through seven days, turning over seven nights. Saturday is for stillness and rest. Sunday is for the limits and the sunna. Monday is for the doubled meanings within the greater meaning, *al-mathani fi al-ma'na al-kabir*. Tuesday holds three houses — the apparent, the inward, and the inward of the inward. Wednesday is for the four sanctified months. Thursday is the fifth of the causes of God. And Friday is the greatest law of God.

The darkness is the seven nights paired to those seven days — a night for each day, like a garden lying beneath each of them.

The vessels are seven, by which the regions of the earth are crossed: camels, horses, mules, donkeys, elephants, oxen, and ships. Camels for the sand. Horses for the plains. Mules for the mountains. Donkeys for short errands. Elephants for the marshes and the wastelands. Oxen for the salt-flats and the thickets. Ships for the seas. Seven mounts for seven domains.

The blessings of this world rest on seven: air, water, light, darkness, food, clothing, and marriage. Air is the open space between sky and earth, where winds rise and breaths come and go. Water is the life of every living thing. Light is the substance by which sight works. Darkness is the increase of what grows. Food is the body's livelihood. Clothing is the shelter that veils the parts a person should not display. Marriage is the seed of generation and the cause of every line of descent. Some of these are the keys to good, and some of these are the mines from which blessing is dug. Which of them, then, is to be blamed? They are all from the hand of God and from His favors, and His servants owe Him thanks for them.

And opposite these blessings, also seven, the modes of hardship: heat, cold, hunger, thirst, fear, sickness, and death. Seven things to be borne with patience — once you know what they mean.

He said: these are the attributes of the world, and I have no doubt that not one of them is anything other than what the Creator has made and composed. Then why do the scholars censure the world, when they cannot do without it, while the ignorant praise it? It looks to me as if the ignorant have struck the meaning even while remaining ignorant — and if that is so, their being right forces the scholars into the wrong, since the scholars contradict the people of ignorance in every sense.

I said: the scholars did not err in censuring the world. The trouble is that nobody knows what they mean by their censure. And the ignorant did not strike anything right when they praised the world; they were praising it without knowing what they were praising — and a man who is ignorant of a thing only ends up at odds with it.

Then, he said, show me what the scholars mean, so that their truth may be plain to me — and mark out the speech of the ignorant by what reveals it as false, since on the surface the matter looks the other way. My chest has tightened around this confusion, and I hope for no release except through knowledge of it.

Listen, I said. This world, and everything in it that I have described to you, is an outside for an inside. Its outside has no standing except by its inside, because the inside is to it what the soul is to the body. The man who knows the soul finds the body sweetened to him. The man who does not know the soul and clings only to the body is clinging to a corpse — and the corpse is forbidden. That is why the scholars forbade the world to themselves: because what they were forbidding was the carcass, the same thing God has forbidden, until you take the world from the side God means it to be taken from. And that is why the ignorant lie when they praise the world, because they have not known its inside, and have not known what God intended by it. They imagine God created it for some meaning other than the meaning it has — but God did not create anything in vain. If the world and what is in it had been created only for the world, then He would have brought into being a creation that dwindles away to nothing — without consequence and without meaning. Every act without consequence is play. Every speech without meaning is a lie. And God is too exalted for either.

He said: now the truthfulness of the scholars stands, their virtue stands, and the people of ignorance and their falseness stand exposed in the open part of the argument. There remains the inside of the world and what is in it. And I notice that the tightness I had been complaining of to you has eased.

I asked: what is the proper return to one who has brought you out of the tightness of poverty into the openness of plenty?

Thanks and praise, he said.

Then what is the proper return, I said, to one who has brought you out of the tightness of ignorance into the openness of knowledge?

Obedience and action, he said.

Then I said: this world, and everything that looks like it, is an outside for the hereafter — and the hereafter is the soul of it and the life of it. A man who chases this world for the world's own sake, without any knowledge of the hereafter, is lost in his chasing; his pursuit has no meaning and no consequence. But a man who pursues this world for the sake of the hereafter, and believes in the hereafter, has meaning and consequence in his pursuit, and his pursuit is praised.

He said: I believe. Now show me the features of this world and what is in it, and set the parables of it next to the things they stand for, drawn from inside.

Hear me, then. God — blessed and exalted — has not created anything in this world, no creeping thing on the earth, no bird that flies on its wings, nothing wet and nothing dry, nothing among the stationary inanimate things like mountains and stones and trees and the minerals such as gold and silver and gem — nothing in any of this, great or small, that is not itself a parable struck for something else. Look at the speck of dust, for all its smallness and for all how subtle it is — even the speck has limits. It has what is in front of it, what is behind it, what is to its right, what is to its left, what is above it, what is below it. That is six limits, and the speck itself is the seventh. And so it is with everything God has made — from the ant and the mosquito up to whatever is above them, the heavens and the earth and what lies between them, the great and the small. Each has six limits, and the thing itself is the seventh: a sign of the six origins from which things were brought into being, and the air around them, encompassing them all, is the seventh. If your chest is wide enough for questions, and for studying the parables, and for matching each thing to its mate among the hidden pairs, then ask what you like.

He said: what is the likeness of the heaven, with its height over things, and the breadth of its quarters, until it has gathered all things under itself?

It is, I said, the likeness of the Speaking Imam, *al-Imam al-Natiq*, who has been raised above all creation by his virtue and by the height of his stations, and has gathered into himself all the rulings of the Shariah by the breadth of his knowledge.

And the constellations and the rest of the stars?

The twelve constellations are the twelve chiefs, the *nuqaba*, the chiefs of the Speaking Imam. The stars within them are his summoners, his *du'at*, who call to the good with their Lord's permission.

And the earth, in its breadth, since it has been made a resting place for this creation and a cradle?

It is, I said, the likeness of the *bab*, the gate of the Speaking Imam during his life, and of the *wasi*, his successor, after his death. It is a place people return to and a settled ground for whoever seeks guidance — and they are people who know the parables and the inward interpretation, the *ta'wil*.

The twelve islands in the earth?

Parables of the twelve *hujaj* — the twelve arguments of the successors.

The earth's rivers and its springs?

Parables of those who summon to the good — the *du'at*.

The kingdoms of the seven heavens and their orbits?

Those, I said, are the causes between God and the *natiqun*, the speakers, and they belong to Him: seven kingdoms and two orbits — the great causes of God.

The seven seas?

Causes too — between God and the imams of the speaker — beneath the order of the first.

The salt water and the fresh water, and their being different upon the earth?

The salt water is like the knowledge of the outside, in which there is no *da'wa* and no caller — for the salt water grows no crop and nothing else. The fresh water is like the knowledge of the inside. Some of it is springs hidden in the earth, and some of it is water that runs on the surface. Knowledge is like that: some of it is the open kind, in which the *du'at* go about their work, and some of it is sealed away, hidden.

And why has water become an image of knowledge?

Because water is life for every living thing, and knowledge is life for every knower.

What about the air that encompasses all things?

The air is too great to be set up as a parable for any thing it might stand for.

He said: is it greater than the heaven and the earth, since those are parables?

Glory be to God, I said. The air is greater than both. The breadth of the earth inside the atmosphere is like a ring on a flat plain. The breadth of the atmosphere inside the sky is like a ring on a flat plain. The breadth of the sky with its quarters inside the breadth of the air is like an egg lying on a flat plain. What signified thing could possibly catch up to that, to become its sign and its likeness?

Then what does it mean? he asked.

It is, I said, the sign of the One to whom nothing is like — glory to Him, and exalted is He above what they associate with Him.

But, he said, has not the Creator Himself said —

> فَلَا تَضْرِبُوا لِلَّهِ الْأَمْثَالَ
>
> "So do not strike parables for God"?

And has He not also said, I answered —

> وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَىٰ
>
> "And to God belongs the highest likeness"?

Glory be, he said, to the One who has placed riddles in His Book so that one would come at it through its proper door — and you are one of its doors. Tell me, then. How does this stand together?

Everything, I said, has a meaning, and the meaning of the parables is this: the outside is evidence of the inside. He — blessed and exalted — forbade that He Himself be measured against anything of His creation. One does not say, "This is like God," the way one says, "This is like the Imam," or "This is like the *hujja*," or "This is like the *da'i*." Their likenesses became evidences pointing to them and showing the difference between them. It is not fitting, given His greatness, that the Creator be measured against any creature of His, in word or in act. One does not say, "This is like God." But parables are taken according to their meanings, and the meanings carry evidences: the lesser points to the greater, the greater points to the loftier, and the loftier points to the Loftiest of All. That is why He said —

> وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَىٰ
>
> "And to God belongs the highest likeness" —

meaning the highest evidence: there is no evidence pointing to anything greater than the evidence pointing to God. And that highest evidence is the air: highest above the heavens and the earth, encompassing all things, while no thing of them encompasses it. That is the highest likeness. It is the evidence of the One to whom nothing is like. He did not say "there is nothing like Him" in the verse — for of course there is nothing like Him — but rather He meant: there is no evidence for Him like this evidence. To Him belongs the highest likeness. No evidence among the evidences taken from any of the creatures we have described compares to this evidence. Blessed is God, Lord of the great Throne.

The boy said: the meaning has grown vast, and the field has opened. Was there, before the air, something else standing between God and what you have described — given the greatness of what you have just laid before me?

Yes, I said.

What is it?

The age. The *awan*.

And what is the *awan*?

The age of the Will — the will by which He created what He created and commanded as He pleased.

You have plunged me, he said, into the depths of the seas of the High Assembly. Bring me back to the sciences of this world and to the parables of it, so that I can lean on them for my own affairs and grow strong enough by them to carry what I am supposed to carry. I have begun to fear for myself, to feel sorry for my own mind, lest it be set wandering in something it cannot reach the bottom of.

You rose by your asking, I said — you rose well — and the answer rose along with you, up to the high reaches of the outward meanings, where your opinion lost itself and your mind became bewildered. What, then, if I had opened the inward meaning to you? You and I would be Moses and *al-alim* — Moses and the one he met by the sea.

Then is there, he said, an inside even more inward than this inside?

By my life, I said. The inside has an inside, more elevated in station, more vast in capacity, more complete in guidance — it is the furthest of all evidences along the road to deliverance.

Then I see here, he said, three layers of knowledge — outside, inside, and the inside of the inside. Is there a proof of this?

Where else, I said, would the proof be — except in this very thing, and in what it points to?

Then show me, he said, an image of these three layers.

I will show you, I said, from the Book of God, from the argument of the intellect, and from the body of parables that point to it.

I had not imagined, he said, that beyond what I had reached, there was still a doctrine waiting. God's matter is indeed greater than what the imaginings of those who labor over speech can climb toward.

As for the Book of God, hear what He said:

> يَا بَنِي آدَمَ قَدْ أَنزَلْنَا عَلَيْكُمْ لِبَاسًا يُوَارِي سَوْآتِكُمْ وَرِيشًا ۖ وَلِبَاسُ التَّقْوَىٰ ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ
>
> "O Children of Adam — We have sent down upon you a garment to cover your shame, and a plumage; and the garment of God-mindfulness, that is best."

That is three: the outside, the inside, and the inside of the inside.

He said: you have spoken truly.

And as for the argument of the intellect, I said: a thing is known only by a name, an attribute, or a meaning. The meaning is what the thing actually is. The name is what it is called. The attribute is what points to it. So too with the religion of God: it has an outward name and an inward evidence, and neither stands without the other. Nothing is pointed to except by a name or by an attribute. Take the speaker who says, "The moon has risen." The meaning is the disc itself. "Moon" is its name. "Has risen" is its attribute. If he were to say only "disc" and fall silent, no meaning would land. If he were to say only "moon" and fall silent, no meaning would land. Only once the name and the attribute have been joined — once he says "The moon has risen" — does the meaning come clear and the speech stand. And so when you say "outside," it presses for an answer: outside of what? When you say "inside," it presses for the same: inside of what? But when you say "the outside of the religion and its inside," the speech becomes intelligible, and you have a religion possessing an outside and an inside — exactly as a thing has a name and an attribute. As He said:

> وَذَرُوا ظَاهِرَ الْإِثْمِ وَبَاطِنَهُ
>
> "And leave off the outward of sin and its inward."

So sin became a thing with an outside and an inside. And so too with the greatest religion of God — the religion of which one says, "The laws are its outside, and the inside is its inside" — each subsisting in itself. To know its meaning there are many parables. Take the sealed egg: it has an outside, which is the shell that keeps it. It has an inside, which is the white fluid within. And it has an inside of the inside — the yellow yolk — which is its essence and its meaning. The whole egg is held by a shell you can touch. Open it, and there is a clear that surrounds. Reach in further, and there is a gold heart at the center. That is the order of the religion of God, and the order of knowledge: outside, inside, and the inside of the inside — and the kernel inside the kernel is the place where the meaning lives.

## 6. Three Layers of Knowledge

And so the boy, hearing how the egg holds its shell, its white, and its golden heart, turned back to the scholar with a thought already half-formed. He said: *Are not this world and the Hereafter a pair? And the soul and the body a pair? And the knowledge of the apparent and the knowledge of the inward a pair? Are not all these things — and many more besides — paired in the same way?*

The scholar accepted it. He said yes, but pressed him further: *Are they pairs of a single kind?*

The boy thought a moment and answered: *The pairs point to the religion of God.*

The scholar accepted that too. And then he laid out the structure plainly, as if drawing it in the sand between them. Every pair has an outward name and an inward attribute, he said, and the attribute is what signifies the knowledge of God and His religion — and that is the inner. So look at the creation itself. Creation is built in three houses. First the angels were made. Then the children of Adam. Then the beasts. The outward knowledge — the surface, the visible law — is the limit of the beasts; whoever stays at that level, knowing nothing beneath it, lives at the level of a beast. The inward knowledge is the proper portion of the children of Adam; whoever takes hold of it becomes a believer, a true human being. And the inward of the inward — the kernel beneath the kernel — is the knowledge of the angels. Whoever reaches that has become spirit in his learning while still flesh in his body; he is a prophet sent by God, by whom God reasons against His creation. He is the veil before the angels. He is the cipher of revelation and its interpreter for the children of Adam. With him are the keys of Paradise — and no one enters except through his obedience. With him is also the passage past the Fire — and no one escapes it except by his hand.

Then the scholar said something that has stayed with me ever since. *The people,* he said, *are only two kinds of men.*

One is the master of his own learning — a man who has been brought to the very end of knowledge, whose soul has been touched by the spirit of certainty itself. He is a scholar by what he knows, and he is a master by what he does. The other is the seeker, the student walking the road for the sake of his own rescue. As for everyone else — the noisy crowd, the masses who know nothing, who follow whichever voice calls loudest — they are the rabble of every false guide, deceived by his error, seduced by his ignorance. They think they are doing fine work. They imagine they are doing well. But the warning-examples of the people before them have already been set out, and God will not break His promise, and the disbelievers shall have the like of what came down upon those who went before.

The boy was moved. He said: *So this is what excellence looks like. This is what knowledge looks like, and the speech that opens it. Blessed are the friends of God. Blessed are those who follow them with goodness. Can I really reach a station as great as that — that highest house, the one above all the others?*

The scholar answered him with care. *My son, that is a station God has honored above every other rank and degree. You cannot reach it except by virtuous work. If you do its work, and if you seek it with honest certainty and a clear, awake heart, I hope you will reach what the chosen friends of God have reached.*

*And who,* the boy pressed, *can even aspire to a station like that — the station to which God lifts His own friends?*

The scholar said: *Their God is your God. There is no kinship between God and anyone. Nothing brings a creature near to God except God-consciousness and good action. And the knowledge by which God has raised His friends — you are at its beginning. If you act upon it, you will reach its end. It is binding on God, in His own promise, that He bring you to where He brought the righteous, and that He hold back nothing from you of what He gave them.*

The boy then asked the natural question: *Will you help me into that, the way you helped me at the start?*

And the scholar said no — but in the gentlest way. *This,* he said, *is a station in which nothing avails except the work of its own people.*

*How can that be,* the boy asked, *when you helped me before?*

So the scholar gave him two pictures. *The farmer can prepare the soil. He can plant. He can water. But he cannot draw the green sprout out of the earth himself. A craftsman can ply his trade whenever he chooses, but he cannot make what he wills appear out of the air. It pains me, my son, that you ask my help in a thing I cannot give. May God open your chest and light your heart with guidance. Have God-consciousness. Do what is good. God does not waste the wage of one whose work is fine. You are already on the road of safety, the road of guidance, the road of the people of piety — your road, the one your feet are already on. Hold to the rope you have been called to, until you are guided from a rope of God to the rope of God itself — for that is the very end of every seeker's search.*

The boy said: *Is not the rope of God the very Imam to whom you have called me?*

The scholar said: *He is the visible face of it. He is your rope, the most trustworthy handhold, your argument, the door of your Imam.*

*Then,* the boy asked, *what is the rope of God? And what is a rope reaching from God to that rope?*

The scholar answered only this: *That is the end of your guidance, and the end of all who know.*

And the boy, hearing the limit set, said:

> لَا حَوْلَ وَلَا قُوَّةَ إِلَّا بِاللَّهِ
>
> "There is no power and no strength except in God."

He added: *He has drowned in the depths of ignorance who has no guide — just as he has drowned in the seas of the world who has no ship.*

But the scholar would not let the phrase pass without examination. He said: *And what is this hawl, and what is this quwwa, that you say belong only to God?*

The boy answered with what he had been taught. *We say it means: I have no means to draw a benefit to myself, and no strength to push away a harm I dislike, except by God.*

The scholar pressed harder. *Then why do you say* la hawla *and not simply* la hilata*— no stratagem except by God?*

The boy admitted what he had been told. *In our sciences,* he said, *we have it that the* hawl *is the year. God Himself uses the word that way:*

> حَوْلَيْنِ كَامِلَيْنِ
>
> "two complete years."

*Two years. And as for* quwwa *— we have it, and the common people have it, that it means mules. Because God says:*

> وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْكُنُوزِ مَا إِنَّ مَفَاتِحَهُ لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ
>
> "And We gave him such treasures that their very keys would have weighed down a band of strong men."

*They say* the band *is forty men, and the* power *is mules, and that the keys of his treasures were the load of forty mules.*

The scholar looked at him. *So then,* he said, *by your words "there is no hawl and no quwwa except in God" you mean: there is no year and no mules except by God? Is that the meaning you want to stand on?*

The boy answered honestly. *I have no other reading. And I confess it is weak. It barely resembles the speech of wisdom at all. I am sure the meaning is something stronger than that, something firmer in the mind. Tell me — is there an inward meaning beneath the surface?*

The scholar said: *Creation has not spoken one true word without that word having a root in the inward. And there is no false word that did not at some time carry a true meaning, before its sense was turned aside. When speech is shifted off its meaning, the meaning becomes impossible. What is dislodged from its place is altered. And truth, when altered, becomes falsehood. The word stands as the instrument by which we reach what is behind it by analogy and pattern — just as we reach what is in front of it by likeness and parable.*

*Tell me, then,* the boy said. *What do these two words mean, and others like them?*

This is the moment in their conversation I want you to mark. Here is the door swinging open. Listen to what the scholar says.

*The* hawl *— understand him this way. He is the Imam of God, His truthful Caliph, the master of the twelve appointed captains. Just as the* hawl *of the calendar — the year — is not complete except by twelve months, neither is he. He is called* hawl *because he turns* — *yuhawwil* — *the speech of the Creator, in its delicate inner reality, into the speech of human beings, so that it can be known and understood. By his turning, the speech becomes outward, audible. The words of wisdom take on a body, a frame, while the speech of the Creator carries within them the spirit of life and the light of salvation. The reason the speech of wisdom is more excellent than every other speech is precisely this: that the speech of the Creator lies inside it. That is why the Imam was named* hawl *— for the turning of the word. And he does not turn it except by God's permission.*

*And the* quwwa*,* the scholar went on, *is the Imam's argument and his door — the one God has strengthened to carry the heavy weight of utterance. He receives the speech from the Imam in summary, and he distributes it to the callers in detail, each according to his station. He was named* quwwa *— strength — because he is strengthened, and he is strengthened only by God. That is why the phrase reads "there is no hawl and no quwwa except by God": there is no Imam and no proof except by God. From God comes their light. From God comes their support.*

*As for* the band, the usba *of strong men in Joseph's story — those are the twelve proofs. And it is exactly so that Joseph said to his father:*

> يَا أَبَتِ إِنِّي رَأَيْتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ رَأَيْتُهُمْ لِي سَاجِدِينَ
>
> "O my father, I saw eleven stars and the sun and the moon — I saw them prostrating to me."

*The sun there is the Imam. The moon is his proof, his door. The stars are the twelve captains in full. And when Joseph's brothers said:*

> لَيُوسُفُ وَأَخُوهُ أَحَبُّ إِلَىٰ أَبِينَا مِنَّا وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ
>
> "Truly Joseph and his brother are dearer to our father than we are, though we are a band."

*They meant: the twelve captains. As if to say,* let our father pick one of us instead*. The shallowness of their judgment about Joseph showed itself there, and the first edges of envy appeared in them when God favored him. But the decree of God concerning Joseph was fulfilled, and the end of their plot was regret, and a turning back to him.*

The boy said: *Praise God, who cleansed them of that stain and brought them back to repentance. Now what about the king's dream — when he said:*

> إِنِّي أَرَىٰ سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ
>
> "Indeed, I see seven fat cows being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others dry."

*What were they? And what is a vision in sleep, anyway?*

The scholar said: *A vision in sleep is like the outward of a work — like a mirage which, when you come up to it, you find nothing in it at all. That is exactly what outward work without inward meaning is. Nothing of it is accepted, however much there is. And the same is true of the dream: nothing is realized from what you see, no matter how often you see it. The king said "I see" — and in the people's account it points to seven things turning upon seven. The seven fat cows are the causes by which God reaches His creation. The seven lean ones are the seven appointed Speakers, who are reduced by need to receiving what comes from God through those seven causes. The seven green ears are the causes between God and His friends. The seven dry ears are the friends themselves, who must wait on what comes from God at the hand of those causes. Blessed is God, the Lord of the worlds.*

*And what is the likeness of all this in us?* the boy asked.

*This,* the scholar said, *is the foundation of Islam. And what came out of him is like a single grain that sprouts into seven ears — meaning seven imams came out of his loins.*

Then the boy asked the question every reader is going to ask. *If that is so, how does God forbid the world to us? He says:*

> فَلَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا
>
> "Let not the life of this world deceive you."

The scholar said: *God speaks truly. Look at what He actually said. He did not say "let not the world deceive you." He said "let not the* life *of this world deceive you." Life is of four meanings. There is a life evident in this world, whose end is annihilation, and a life of the Hereafter, whose end is permanence. He is saying: do not labor for the life that perishes — labor for the life that endures. This is His meaning when He has the regretful one say:*

> يَا لَيْتَنِي قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِي
>
> "Would that I had sent something ahead for my life."

*Meaning: would that, in the perishing life, I had sent something forward for the lasting one. And the two lives have a second sense: the life of the outward knowledge and the life of the inward. The knowledge of the outward is the life of this world — that is the lower knowledge. The knowledge of the inward is the life of the Hereafter. For that reason He said "let not the life of this world deceive you" — meaning, do not be deceived by the outer of knowledge and a practice of it alone. He will accept nothing from you unless the inward stands together with the outward. He does not accept the outward without the inward.*

The boy was quick. He asked the converse. *What about a man who knows the inward knowledge but does not know the outward and does not practice it? Where does he stand among the people of religion?*

The scholar said: *A poor station indeed. His inward cannot stand. If he abandons what he was commanded to do, and tries to leave his inner self bare like a fruit whose rind has been peeled off before it is ripe — the fruit spoils, and nothing afterward will set it right. The body whose limbs are cut off cannot hold the soul in it for an hour. So with religion: if its outward duties are dismantled, the inward will not stand for the one who owns it. And besides — when a man drops the outward without the permission of the One who imposed it on him, his case can only be one of two: either he abandoned it from incapacity, in which case he is more incapable still for the inward; or he abandoned it with the strength to keep it, deliberately, in which case he has rebelled against the One who commanded him. The rebel is a transgressor. The transgressor is no neighbor to the friends of God. He is their enemy and they are his — because he has cut what God commanded him to keep joined.*

*And one who knows the outward knowledge and practices it, but does not know the inward,* the boy asked. *Where does he stand?*

*A worse station yet,* the scholar said. *He is like a body that has been formed but into which no soul has been breathed. He is counted among the dead. The dead are the dwelling of the disbelievers. And one who disbelieves in the signs of God is an enemy to religion and to its people.*

The boy gathered what he had learned. *Then I see,* he said, *that the outward is not made good except by the inward, and the inward does not stand except by the outward. The world is not lawful except for one who has known the Hereafter — for the Hereafter is the world's true life and its inward. And religion is not granted in full to its people unless they establish both its outer and its inner.*

*Yes,* the scholar said. *That is the meaning, and on that the action stands. Establishing all of what God commanded is what earns His pleasure. Dropping any part of what God commanded exposes one to His anger.*

*Then,* the boy said, *whoever knows the outward together with the inward, and establishes them both — is he a true believer?*

*Yes,* the scholar said. *A true believer.*

*And whoever learns,* the boy went on, *and works for the Hereafter — does not the world also become good for him? Does not its lawful enjoyment fall to him, and the turning of its gains?*

*Yes,* the scholar said. *The world is made good for no one except them. They are the most deserving of the world and the foremost in it.*

And here the boy raised the question that had been waiting behind his other questions all along. *Then why,* he asked, *do I look at the people of this description — the very scholars of this matter — and I see them refusing the world, refusing what is in it, until the ignorant among them think the world has been forbidden to them outright? They look away from this world. They glance at it sideways. Their souls find it heavy. Their faces are pale though they are not sick. Their bodies are wasted. Their skin clings to their bones, so that it looks as though they don't eat — though they are eating?*

The narrator says — and I trust him in this — that when the scholar heard the boy describe his own brothers, his own kind, he choked on his answer. His eyes filled with tears. For a while he could not speak from the weight of it. The boy began to cry too, just because the scholar was crying, until the scholar grew afraid that some enemy might come upon them and catch them like this. He took hold of himself. He let his heart settle inside him. And then he turned back to the boy.

*My son,* he said, *the people you have just described looked at themselves by the light of life, and they worked on their own affairs by the certainty of salvation, and they did not flatter themselves. My son: this world is seen with the eye of wealth and children. The Hereafter is seen with the eye of knowledge and certainty. My son: the faith of these people in the unseen is sound. They have believed the promise and the warning. Their souls were drawn to what they were promised, and they sought it. The fear of the warning shook them, and they labored. They dreaded the daze of death, and they ran ahead with deeds. Their bodies were weakened by the rigor of their fasting. Their faces were yellowed by the length of their vigils. They do not sleep when others sleep — they are too afraid. They do not eat their food except as a bitter thing. The hunger of thinking on what they desire has emptied them of the pleasure of living. The sweetness of remembrance has pulled them away from the term of sleep. Their souls have grown numb to appetite, so the world has lost its taste for them. They remember death often, and so life cannot torment them. They have seen the blessings of the Hereafter as a certainty, and so they look at this world sideways — only sideways. They have refused the world out of preoccupation with what is greater than it, not because the world was made forbidden to them.*

*That is the true report,* the boy said. *And in this sense the scholars hated the world, while the people stayed heedless of them — supposing that those scholars had forbidden themselves what God had made lawful. You have described for me the deceit of the people of this world about their world, and their forgetfulness of the Hereafter.*

The scholar said: *If the people charmed by this world would only think — about their own bodies, about how their states keep turning, weakness into strength and strength back into weakness, pleasure into ending, life into death — and if they would look at the generations that went before them, where the good and the wicked alike were laid into the same earth, and they saw no clear reward for the good in this world, and no clear punishment for the wicked in this world — they would know they were not made for this. But the labor of seeking weighed heavy on them. So they would not load themselves with the effort of the search. They grabbed this little portion in front of them. They were content with the comfort of this world as a substitute for the Hereafter. That is their work. That is their meaning. They have no argument before God. They have no claim against His friends. And the warning-reports that have come down to them are filled with what should have broken their pride.*

## 7. The Five Shares and the Long Road to the Shaykh

The boy heard all of that, and then he asked the next question — the one a younger mind would ask only after the older mind has already shaken it. *Praise be to God,* he said, *who has guided us to His religion. We could not have been guided except by His favor.* Then: tell me about the beginning of this creation. Is it one thing or many? Because what I see in the world is a riot of opinions and appetites, and I cannot reconcile that with a single Maker.

The scholar answered him slowly, the way a good teacher answers a question he has been waiting for. The bodies themselves, he said, are obviously different — different tongues in the mouth, different colors on the skin — and that variation is not chaos, it is a sign. It is how a father knows his son and a son knows his father, how a man knows his own wife and no other, and how a woman knows her own husband and no other. That much variety is mercy disguised as difference. But underneath the bodies, the *instrument* — the senses by which a human being meets the world, the outward eyes and ears and the inward heart and mind — that is one creation, identical from person to person. Have you ever seen a learned man with three eyes, as if knowledge had given him an extra one? Have you ever seen an ignorant man with one eye, as if his ignorance had taken one away? Has anyone ever been issued more apparatus than his neighbor? No. The outward apparatus is level across humanity, and the inward apparatus follows the same rule. God would not be just on the outside and unjust on the inside. He did not give the scholar two hearts in one chest, and He did not strip the ignorant man of any organ the rest of us have. The instrument is one. The composition is one. And that is exactly what He throws back in our face when He says:

> لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ أَعْيُنٌ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ آذَانٌ لَا يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا
>
> "They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, ears they do not hear with."

Do you imagine — the scholar asked him — that God in His generosity has loaded human beings with a weight they cannot carry? Not at all. He is fairer than that.

The boy accepted it. *I don't see it any other way,* he said. *I don't argue it.* But then he came right back at the question from the other side. If the origin is one and the instrument is one, then where do the appetites get their differences? Why does one man go one way and the next man another?

The scholar said: the origin is in fact one. All of them are from Adam, and Adam is from dust — formed by his Lord, and breathed into from His own Spirit. But somewhere along the line the marriages went off the Sunna. Children were born outside the way and grew up outside the guidance. And I have no doubt that among them are children of illegitimacy who claim fathers who are not their fathers. When the roots foul, the branches foul. The return road back to the truth gets harder and harder to find — and on top of that, you have arrogance refusing to obey, laziness refusing to worship, and envy of the people God has befriended. So they no longer kept to the line between what is permitted and what is forbidden.

The boy pressed again. If the root is one and the instrument is one, and even the *purpose* of seeking is one — because every human being is reaching for what he thinks is his own good — then where exactly does the divergence open up?

By my life, the scholar said, you are right that the purpose of the seeking is one. The divergence is not in the seeking. It is in the *striking*. Picture archers all aiming at the same target. They have agreed on the target, they have agreed on the courage of the shot, they have agreed on the bow and the arrow — and still some of them hit and some of them miss. That is what is happening with the hearts in our chests. The hearts agree on the use of intellect; the intellects agree on the protection and the dignity of the body. The moment the call of truth came down, the hearts split. Some of them looked at the messengers and said: answering these men, lowering myself to them, is itself a form of self-respect — it protects me from foolishness. Others looked at the same messengers and said: rejecting these men, refusing to bend, is itself a form of self-respect — it protects me from the narrowness of worship. The original impulse was identical on both sides: ease for the body, dignity for the soul. One group took what the messengers promised — the reward of God — even though in this world that reward looked rare and costly, because at least it lasts. The other group took the pleasure of this world — even though it perishes — because it is present, it is easy to ask for, and the payoff arrives quickly. The same kind of heart that reached for the bliss of the Hereafter in the one man reached for the bliss of this world in the other. Each one looked at himself. Each one believed he was advising himself well. So they came together in the seeking and separated in the striking. And that — the scholar said — is the justice of God and the openness of His giving. Every seeker gets what he sought. Whoever wants the reward of this world is given from it. Whoever wants the reward of the next is given from it.

The boy took this and turned it. The people of this world, he said, are stratified inside it. Some are independently wealthy, kings on their piles. Some are poor and circle the wealthy serving them for crumbs. But in the end, the pleasure of the world flattens them into one row — are they all level in the bad ending? Or do some of them stand out from the rest?

It is part of God's justice, the scholar told him, that He attaches them to one another. He does not separate a man from his friends and his familiars. They are equal in His punishment because they were already united in His disobedience.

The boy then turned to the other side of the same question. What about this religion that God has honored and perfected, that He has chosen as the religion for Himself and laid as an obligation on His servants — does He accept *some* of it from a man and not the rest, so that the religious are stratified the way the worldly are? Or does He accept only from the man who brings it whole?

If the worldly have classes within the world, the scholar said, then the religious have *degrees* within the religion. Justice requires that the Creator be worshipped to the measure of His own will — the magnitude of His sovereignty and the magnitude of our need for Him. But His mercy has stretched out toward us. He knows there are weak ones among us. So He accepts the easy striving when that is what a person can manage, and He accepts effort in obedience whether the amount is small or large. He charges each soul according to its capacity and its work, and He gives each man of merit his merit, so that they are at degrees with their Lord. And then — and this is the line that should knock the breath out of you — He has fixed it so that the end of obedience, for the weak ones, is that they will be *joined* to the people of the complete religion and the highest work. He said:

> وَمَن يُطِعِ اللَّهَ وَالرَّسُولَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ مَعَ الَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِم مِّنَ النَّبِيِّينَ وَالصِّدِّيقِينَ وَالشُّهَدَاءِ وَالصَّالِحِينَ ۚ وَحَسُنَ أُولَٰئِكَ رَفِيقًا ۝ ذَٰلِكَ الْفَضْلُ مِنَ اللَّهِ
>
> "Whoever obeys God and the Messenger — those are with the ones God has favored: prophets, truthful ones, martyrs, righteous ones. What excellent companions. That is the favor from God."

They reach that company by their effort, the scholar said. Not because their work is the equal of the righteous — it isn't. It's a mercy from God to them, even though they are weak and their works are slight.

*Praise be to God for that,* the boy said. *Is there then any worry that someone might be punished for something that wasn't disobedience — even if his work was thin?*

God is fairer than that.

*Then is there hope that anyone might enter Paradise with no work at all — like the insane, like little children?*

That, the scholar said, falls inside the grace of God and His generosity and the width of His mercy.

*Then what is the limit of "striving in obedience" and the standard of "sincere work" — the threshold at which God is bound to accept a man's excuse for everything else?*

The full measure of his knowledge. The utmost reach of his strength.

The boy did not let it stay abstract. *Describe to me, then, the obligation of knowledge and the obligation of action, and the goal of each, so that I can test myself against them. If I can reach the goal in seeking my Lord's pleasure, I will press on. If I can't, I will stop at the limit of my capacity, and God is the first to excuse what I could not do. I trust God's justice and feel safe from any injustice from Him — what I am afraid of is the evil inside myself.*

The scholar said: don't be afraid where you've already come to believe, and don't feel safe where you should still be afraid. Strive for God with your person and with your wealth. God does not accept what He hates and does not hate what pleases Him. He hated disbelief for His creation and was pleased with gratitude from His servants and with justice in His ruling — so He accepts no substitute. Truthfulness and faithfulness are the two gardens of His Paradise; only the people of truthfulness and faithfulness are saved at His door.

*Then show me the goal of work in my own self, and what God has bound on me there.*

The obedience of God dignifies its people even when their roots are humble, the scholar said, and good work is the crown of the workers. As for what is owed in your own person: discharge every obligation, abstain from every forbidden thing. That is the spine of the religion and the alms of the body. And the gist of what wins you God's pleasure and acceptance is this — extend to others what you would wish extended to you, and refrain from doing to others what you would wish them to refrain from doing to you. That is justice. And God is pleased by justice.

*Yes,* the boy said — *that is the binding truth, and a man who doesn't act on it has nothing with God. Is there any latitude in what you have said, when necessity strikes?*

God has loosened it for His servants, out of compassion and mercy, the scholar told him. He has said:

> فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ
>
> "Whoever is forced, neither craving nor transgressing — there is no sin on him."

*Praise be to God,* the boy said, *who has not made the religion a hardship on His servants. I have made up my mind to hold to what He has commanded, and I ask His help for what He has ordered me to do. Now — what about my wealth, which you paired with my person when you told me to strive with both?*

Your wealth, the scholar said, is whatever God has put into your hand from any kind of earning. Take it only from a lawful source. Spend it only in its rightful place.

*What is the lawful source it comes from, and what is the rightful place it goes to?*

Its lawful source is honest earning — earning in which you have wronged no one and which you have not mixed with a lie. Its rightful claim is its alms. Look at everything that has come into your hand, small or large, and take out a fifth of it — and take it from the best of what you own, what you yourself would most love to receive — and hand it to the one who has the binding claim on you. That is how wealth is purified, the way the body is purified by work. Work is a shield. The alms is purification. *Whoever pays the alms has succeeded.*

*I will do it,* the boy said, *and I repent to God for what has already passed. What about the rest of the wealth?*

Spend it on yourself and your household with good sense. If there is a surplus, single out the needy among your brothers from it, and offer your kindness to anyone willing to come to you for it — because a man without surplus of his own *needs* someone else's surplus. Be quick to every good and out in front of every act of generosity. That is the goal of work — if you have the patience for it.

*Yes,* the boy said, *patience and then patience. How can a man in deep need not be patient with the One who has no need of his work? Every needy man bears patiently and every favored man should be grateful.*

The scholar, when he heard the boy say that, was delighted by him. *Praise be to God,* he said, *who has guided you to the right speech. And I ask Him to help you bring it through to completed action. That is the fullness of grace.*

And then the boy said the thing that I want you to hear carefully, because the whole arc of him stands on it.

*I used to look for surplus to come at me from other people,* he said. *I used to want it to flow in from outside. Now that the surplus has finally fallen to my own hand, I find I cannot bear to deprive myself of giving it away — nobody in the world could cheat me of the chance the way I would cheat myself. So here. This is all my wealth, in front of you, in five shares. One share is its alms. One share is expiation for what I have wasted. One share is thanksgiving to God for what I have come to know. One share for the poor among my brothers. And one share for you, for you to accept by your grace, as provision for the road. Take all of them. God will replace them in their kind.*

The narrator says: the scholar's heart broke open for the boy when he saw that the jewel inside him had finally come clear, that the ordeal had brought a praiseworthy outcome to the surface, that his merit was visible, and that his work was clean.

And the scholar said — with the tears running — *My son. For one like you, scholars should travel the road. For one like you, the earth should hold itself steady and the heavens should stay where they are. For one like you, the bonds of kinship should reveal themselves, and the rain should come down out of the bellies of the clouds. So receive what God has given you with joy, my son — He has opened your chest to Islam. Take the glad tidings of a light from your Lord and a swift opening. Accept God's gift to you and the gains of His favor upon you, with vast work and noble thanks.*

But then he had to address the five shares.

*My own share — I have no use for it. I came out from wealth. I am running away from its temptation. It would not be right of you, my son, to push me back into what I have walked away from. Your brothers share — keep it in your hand. When they come asking you for things, hand it to them through your own hand, so they see your kindness and thank Him for your goodness to them. The share of expiation — hold it back for now. Keep it with you until you really know what you have done wrong. The share of thanksgiving — that is a deposit with you, held for God, until you grow up into your full mind and rise to the end of what you have learned. The share of alms — keep that too, held in trust for whoever its rightful claimant turns out to be. Take all of them back to yourself, my son, until you meet him. He is the one who rules on it, both for you and against you.*

The boy did not miss the word *him*.

*Who is this generous one in your eyes,* he said, *whose judgment you do not exceed and whose command you do not overrun?*

The one, the scholar said, whose right God has made binding on me and on every believer. The one in whose hand are the keys of the gardens and the landmarks of the kingdom. The one whose two palms have opened with the light of Sinai — he is the cause behind the signs. The one whose knowledge climbs to the highest of all goals. Through him your light is completed. Through him God perfects your affair. At his hand the blessings flow to you.

*That is the giving I was hoping for,* the boy said. *What is he to you, and what are you to him?*

I am his son, the scholar said. I am one of his good works. I seek what pleases him. I shrink from what angers him.

*Then I imagine I am very far from his pleasure and very close to his anger,* the boy said, *because I did not even know he existed. Is there any excuse for me in that?*

The excuse, the scholar told him, is granted *before* exposure to the knowledge of him.

*Then shall I speak, or shall I hold my tongue?*

Say whatever has come to you.

*Then is it not your duty, at exactly this moment, to take me by the hand to the door of his chamber until I enter, and to the house of light until I draw from it? That is part of the truthful service that is binding on you.*

It is, the scholar said. But only with his permission, and only on his decision.

So the scholar took his leave of the boy and went. The boy wanted to press provisions on him before he went and was turned down. The scholar walked the road until he came to his own elder father, and laid out the news of the boy, and described him. The elder recognized the description — knew at once these were the marks of the people of goodness and of the seams from which merit is mined — and he was struck.

*Hurry back with this boy,* he said. *I am hoping he is a door of mercy God has opened for the people of his time.*

The scholar did not wait long. He came back to where the boy was — and found his body in a different state than the one he had left him in.

*What is this I see in you,* the scholar said, *different from how I left you?*

*What have you noticed in me — may my father and my mother be your ransom?*

*Weakness in the body. Yellowness in the face.*

*That is from a loneliness that hit me after you went.*

*What loneliness — what made the body weak?*

*Your absence terrified me,* the boy said. *And the fear of what might happen before we met again wore me down.*

*God has known what is in your heart,* the scholar said. *He has lightened the ordeal on you, and cast mercy for you into the heart of His friend. So pack your travel things. I am going to take you all the way to what you have hoped for. I will bring you to the door of your goal. There is no power except by God.*

The boy gasped — out of joy, out of awe — a gasp that nearly drove his soul out of his body. God held him in place, because God knew him, and gave him back to himself. When he came around, he said: *Praise be to God, who has never withdrawn His mercy from the believers.* He gave thanks again and again to his father; he took up his father's palms and kissed them; he kissed his father's feet. Then he stood up at once, picked up the travel kit, and the two of them went out together, until they reached the greater scholar — the one I have been calling the Shaykh.

They asked for permission to enter, and were granted it, and they went in and gave their greeting and sat where he told them to sit. And when the assembly had settled, the boy turned to his own teacher and said, *Teach me how to speak. Teach me how to ask for what I need.*

The teacher said: *It is not my place to instruct you in anything while you are sitting here — out of respect for the master of this house. You are in front of someone who already knows what you need and knows what you intend.*

The boy held his tongue. And the two of them stayed with the Shaykh for some time, until the boy's affair was completed, his guidance brought to full term, the limit of his hope reached, and he came to know what was for him and what was on him. Then they took their leave and went out together, quietly. They had spoken no words in that assembly except the greetings of peace and welcome — the boy had gone yellow under the weight of awe and could not say a thing. The Shaykh instructed the master of the house where they were staying to honor them and see to whatever they needed. That man took them back to his house, brought them in, greeted them, and showed his pleasure at having them.

He turned to the scholar — and there was old brotherhood and old acquaintance between them — and said, *Brother, is this the boy we used to hear about?*

*Yes,* the scholar said. *This is the one whose name is sweet in the hearts. The believers have been carrying his story on their tongues.*

The boy looked up at that.

*Has the news of me really gone that far among the friends of God,* he said, *that they study my story in their gatherings of remembrance?*

*Yes, my brother,* the scholar said. *That is exactly how it is.*

*Then favors have surrounded me from every direction,* the boy said, *and hands have reached me from every road. I do not know whom to thank. I do not know whom to repay.*

They sat together that night and spoke until morning. When morning came, they asked permission to come in to the Shaykh. He let them in. They greeted him, he gave them leave to sit, and they sat. And then the Shaykh began.

*Praise be to God,* he said, *who has opened the locked-up hearts with His light, who has unlocked the closed-up things that are sought, who has made remembrance easy by His own arrangement, out of kindness to His servants and mercy to those who long for Him. He has not loaded creation with the full weight of what is owed Him; He has not been content to let them disbelieve in His blessings. So He has charged them, by His justice, only with what they will not collapse under. The matter sits balanced between two extremes — and that is what is called justice. He has stretched out His hands with generosity, in benevolence and in surplus. He has not disappointed the one who calls on Him. He has not turned away the one who keeps silent. His blessings come in waves; His favors come in armies. They are not reserved only for the one who has given thanks for them, because His reward is too immense for that. And they are not withheld only from the one who has been ungrateful, because His punishment is too immense for that. He has kept His blessings free of any meanness. He has made His arguments unanswerable against denial. He has spread Himself wide over what exists, so that the evidence against ingratitude is itself unlimited. He is found and not bounded. He is known and not described. Glory be to Him — how vast, and how gentle to His creation. How mighty, and how merciful to His servants. How beyond reach, and how just in His ruling. How exalted, and how close to mankind. How near, and how far past the reach of any guessing. So worship Him and give thanks to Him. To Him you return.*

Then he said: *But after that.* It belongs to the dignity of the mind that it be put to work in seeking. One of the fruits of seeking is finding what truly is. One of the signs of what truly is, is its sweetness. Every freshwater spring is a drink — its surface is plain in front of you and its depths are veiled. The thought that pursues it never grows tired. Creation never reaches the end of it. Its marks are its rights, and the right of a right is binding — and from that binding it is called *truth*. The man who withholds from a truth what is owed to it is acting unjustly. *Listen.* It is the right of thought that it pay an alms to the hearts in the form of good conduct. It is the right of good conduct that it lead on to the seeking of knowledge. It is the right of knowledge that it be acted on. It is the right of action that it be purified by obedience to the holders of the command. It is the right of obedience that it be carried through in both the hard hours and the easy ones, equally in good and in trial — *and none receive this except the patient, and none receive it except a man of immense fortune.*

Then he turned his face toward the boy.

*Young man,* he said. *I have welcomed you as a friend who has come a long way, and I greet you as a visitor who came on purpose. So — what is your name?*

*Ubayd Allah, son of Abd Allah.*

That is your description, the Shaykh said. Your news had already come to us.

*I am Free, son of the Servant of God,* the boy said.

*And who freed you from your master, until you became free?*

*This scholar,* the boy said — and he gestured to the scholar who had brought him in. *This scholar freed me.*

*Tell me,* the Shaykh said, *if this scholar were himself owned and did not own — would his freeing you be valid?*

*It would not.*

*So what is your name?*

The boy bent his head, baffled, with no answer.

*Young man,* the Shaykh said. *How can a thing be known if it has no name?*

*Then I am a newborn to you,* the boy said. *Name me.*

*That waits seven full days.*

*Why must it wait seven days?*

*For the dignity of the newborn.*

*And if the newborn dies before the seven are up?*

*Nothing harms him. He is named after that.*

*And this name you will give me — does it belong to me?*

*Are you to be served like an idol,* the Shaykh said, *just because you have been born?*

*Then how is the thing to be put?*

*The name belongs to you, and you belong to the name,* the Shaykh said. *So do not move outside your boundary. Go back to your appointed time.*

The boy rose at once, in obedience, and did not press back, out of good manners. He went out, his elder father with him, and they returned to where they were staying. The master of the house greeted them.

*How is our brother's matter — his request?*

*A promise has been made,* the scholar said. *The one waiting is thirsty. The term is short.*

So they waited until the seventh day, and then asked permission to come to the Shaykh. When it was granted, the Shaykh told the boy to wash and to put on his cleanest clothes. As the boy was preparing himself, his heart felt — like a premonition — that his need was going to be answered that day, and that what he had been told to do in preparation was itself a sign of the good that was coming on the other side of it. When he had done as he was told, the two of them went out together, and the boy went saying, *A day of fortune, and a master of fortune,* over and over again. Nothing drove him to say it but the surplus of his joy and the intensity of his longing to find out what would bring him nearer to the pleasure of God. When they reached the Shaykh, they found him already at the work-instruments of his hour. He greeted them, and they returned the greeting.

Then he told the boy to come close, and the boy came close, and he brought him closer, and the boy came closer. And then he began to speak — about things on which no fancy has ever descended, that no pen has ever traced, that have never crossed the heart of any human being. They are not mentioned in the schools of the preachers. They are not declared out loud in the great anthologies of books. They are unveiled only to their own people. And when he had discharged what was owed and brought the boy out of the wilderness of bewilderment into the very end of guidance — until the boy's school had clarified and the water he drank had gone sweet, and he had come to know his Lord — the boy stripped off his pilgrim garment by magnifying the sanctities of God, took hold of the strong rope, circled the ancient House, performed his rites in remembrance, and completed his Hajj by the greatest sign.

After that, the boy and his elder father kept coming back to the Shaykh, going over what he had given. The boy was patient at the discipline of it. He never tired of asking, because the noble thing he was asking about was honorable to keep asking. He never had enough of the answer, because the sweetness of the answer left him thirsty. They stayed in that condition for a stretch of time, until the Shaykh saw that the root in the boy had drunk and the branch in him had risen.

Then the boy spoke. *Praise be to God,* he said — addressing it both to God and to the Shaykh — *who has made you fit for this station. Through you He revives a religion. Through you He kills off an ignorance. He has made you a refuge for people and a place of safety. You are the door for the people of the sky and the staircase for the people of the earth. From the light of your honor the lights came out. From the cup of your palm the rivers ran. May you stay like this until the One who is pleased with you is pleased with you.*

*As for you,* the Shaykh answered. *May God bless what He has given you. May He help you, by His success, to guard what He has handed to you. May He put you to work upright in His will, smooth your road with the light of His guidance, open your chest with the keys of wisdom, dignify your affair with the way of the call, complete your portion with the kinds of response, and bring you in obedience to Him to the best of what you have hoped of His pleasure. I am taking my leave of you, my son, and I am giving you permission to go. Your parting is hard on me. But three things press it on us. The first is that we ourselves are travelling — to a country that is not this one. The second is your separation from your family this whole time, on top of your having gone out without your father's permission. There is heavy grief in him and large anger in him; nothing will clear it but your coming back — and I am still hopeful for your father, if you are kind to him. The third is the duty that comes with this blessing that has reached you. You owe it the religion of serving by it and of calling others to it.*

*Your parting is sorrow to me,* the boy said. *Your absence is a loss to me. But your counsel is the more rightly guided, your obedience the more binding, your judgment the soundest. So lay your advice on me, and dismiss me when you wish, and be kind to me by sending my elder father back with me to my country. I want to lean on him, do my work through him, and put all my affairs into his hand.*

*I will do that,* the Shaykh said. *And I charge you with the fear of God who made you. Hold the trust you have hung around your own neck. Take care of the elder father who raised you when you were small and exhausted himself over your case — take care of him with your person and your wealth. Do not dress him in the garments of contempt. Do not load him onto ships of ruin. Do not let his name drop out of your mouth. Do not let yourself be distracted from remembering him. Do not deceive him in your own city. There is room enough for you at the outset.*

*May God reward you,* the boy said. *On behalf of the Master of grace — with the thanks of every grateful one. May He repay you for the length of your shepherding, when no human hand could repay you. I will keep your charge. Do not cut me off from the blessing of your prayer.*

Then they stood. They clasped hands. They embraced. Each of them said his farewell to his friend, unable any longer to hold himself together, unable to speak except by gestures. Then they took separate roads.

The scholar and the boy went on together until they had nearly reached the city where the boy's father lived. There the scholar said: *My son. I have understood the Shaykh's will. You have not yet understood it. The guidance lies inside what he said. This is your city — we have arrived at its outskirts. Sit with me off the road. I want to bring some of my own concerns to your mind, and I want to leave a charge with you about how to act.*

When they had sat down, the scholar said: *My son. I know what your father is like. I know his hostility toward the people of this matter. And on top of that hostility now is your going out with me and your absence from him without his permission and without his security. I want to honor my own elder father's view by hiding myself in some corner of the wilderness here. And you go back to your father — let his soul be soothed, let his anger toward us sit down. I am hoping — if you are kind to him — that he will come closer to what you want than anybody else would. Absence has worn him out. Loneliness has made him afraid. And know, my son, that God has opened a door for you at the cheapest cost of effort — I did not think you would reach it except after a long stretch. God has been good to you. So be good to yourself. And honor the one through whom God has honored you. We have thought very well of you. Our hope in you is large. Guard the trust your elder father has placed in you. Stand firm in your matter. Piety and discretion are the spine of your religion and your work. Thankfulness and patience are added light. Ask of God your provisions, and the goodness of His success for you.*

Then he said: *I commend to you six things. Trust God when you are resolved. Speak with a judgment when you are called on. Do not get angry when you are wronged. Do not give in to weariness when you are slandered. Whoever answers your call toward good — be patient with him. And single yourself out for the very best of what you are calling others to. God will help you, and God will open the way for you.*

*May God reward you,* the boy said, *with three goods. For an imam whose argument you have completed and whose trust you have given counsel for. And for a dead man whose life you have brought back as a kindness. My heart is full, and my chest is heavy with light and joy from the great things you have laid out. I do not know which of your speech is more beautiful — the outward, in the order you arranged it, or the inward, in the meanings you opened up.*

*Praise be to God,* the scholar said. *He has eased the road for you and brought what was far near. And I am taking my leave and going. Is there anything left?*

*The pillars of my needs are three,* the boy said. *A need that you have already settled. A need that I am still hoping for. And a need I am asking your kindness on.*

*I understand your three,* the scholar said, *and I will answer you on what you asked. As for the need already settled — your life-in-knowledge after the death you used to have. May God bless you in that life and bring you to the best of what you intended in it. As for the need you are still hoping for — act on what you have learned and receive what He brings, and God will bring it about for you by His mercy. As for the need you are asking of me — it is as if you want an increase on what you have heard.*

*There is no increase greater than your knowing what is inside me,* the boy said. *So be kind to me with whatever you see fit.*

*As for my knowing what is inside you,* the scholar said, *the unseen belongs only to God — but the believer sees by the light of God. As for the increase: I will summarize it for you. I will stand you on the foundations you are to work from. Look at every one of the things whose outward shape I have laid out for you, and treat them as parables. Whatever in them is praiseworthy is a likeness of the truth and its people — the higher images for the higher of them, and their witnesses point to them. And whatever in the parables was blameworthy, I have spent on the enemies of the truth and its people — the big images for their big ones, the small images for their small ones, and their witnesses point to them too. Those are the foundations of the knowledge on which all the work turns. If you keep them, you will not need anything else. And I charge you with the fear of the One who made you when you were not a thing, with keeping His covenant, and with standing by the right He has imposed on you and on all His creation.*

When he had finished his words and brought the boy to a safe place inside himself and completed the nursing of him, he said: *My son. Look to yourself. I have business other than you. This is the parting between you and me.*

At the word *parting,* the boy let out a gasp that nearly took his spirit out of him, and nearly drove his soul out of his body. Then he bent down and kissed the scholar's feet and wiped the dust of them on his own cheeks. Then he said farewell, and the scholar went.

The boy went back to his house, unable to hold his heart in his chest for grief, unable to push the tears back at the parting from his elder father and from the man who had been the cause of his every blessing — until his family and his neighbors became frightened for him. He spent his day and his night thrashing under sorrow and alarm. And then his own father came into the room — angry.

## 8. Homecoming, the Father, and the Debate with Abu Malik

He came in angry, and he sat down, and he said what fathers say when they are wounded and do not yet know they have been wounded:

"Is this how sons repay their fathers? I taught you the best a child can be taught. You never denied yourself, you never turned away from your own beliefs — not until I grew old, and this stranger came among us. You drew him in upon me. You crossed over with him into his affair without ever consulting your parents. Now hear me. If what I knew of him is true, then you have wronged me in my own person, because you concealed the truth from me. And if what he carries is false, then you have disobeyed me into your own ruin — and whatever harm has reached you has, by that same road, reached me."

I listened to him with my head lowered, and when he was finished I spoke as gently as I knew how.

"Father, I have shown you nothing but mercy. You raised me as your own mercy. You fed me. You chose me over family and over wealth. I am not so unkind that I would let this day pass without offering you, in return, one of two things — whichever you prefer."

"Name them," he said.

"Either," I said, "you let me go on offering you my courtesy and my favor as before — for I am your son, the one you raised, the one whom God has placed under your hand — or you grant me leave to debate openly with you, and give me your word that I am safe from your anger, so I may draw from your mind without fear."

He thought about it for a long moment.

"Outward courtesy while hearts disagree underneath," he said, "is a reproach between us. How long can the surface keep its shape when the underside is at war with it? I do not want that. As for debating you, and your safety in it — you have it. Say what seems best to you."

So I said it. "Consider these forty years I have stood beside you. They cannot be free of one of two things. Either you are a knower, and you have kept your knowledge from me — and then do not blame me if I look elsewhere for my own salvation. Or you are not a knower — and in that you are excused before me — but then you, more than I, needed this stranger when he came. I would have run to him on your behalf before I ran for myself, because of your age and the lateness of your years. I have heard what he said. You heard it too. I have seen what he has shown. So have you."

His heart broke at that. He saw there was no escape from either horn of it, and his eyes filled.

"My son," he said, "the argument you have brought against me is the same argument that lies waiting for you, if you keep your affairs hidden from me now. Tell me. If what you have found is sound, I will accept it from you as a dignity to myself. And if it is hollow, I will turn you back from it as a kindness to you."

So we spoke. We spoke for a long time, and we uncovered things to each other slowly, and by the end of it we were both standing on the same ground — acting on God's religion, holding it up, holding fast to it, calling others into it, helping one another in it. Through the two of us, God revived a great many people. And my father sent me out into the country to carry the news and to invite the scholar back to us. The scholar came. He was the cause of our flourishing. God set blessing on the people of that town through us and at our hands. The command went through, and the slanderers came up empty.

My name is Salih. My father's name is al-Bakhtari. Our family had a long honor among our people, raised up by old favors we had done them and by a name our manners had earned us. So when word of our religion began to travel, it traveled fast — and it eventually reached a group of men who still held to the old creed. My father had been a benefactor to them for many years, and they feared his departure would cut them off from that benefit. They gathered themselves and went together to the scholar of their party — a man called Abu Malik, "the slave of the mighty." Among them he was famous for his discernment, his long patience, and his guarding of their religion; they had nicknamed him *Ka'b al-Ahbar* — the chief of the rabbis — for the breadth of his learning, the firmness of his speech, and the sharpness of his opinions. When they came in to him, they spoke first.

"Abu Malik," they said, "have you not seen what the boy has gone over to?"

"Which boy?"

"Salih ibn al-Bakhtari."

"And what has he gone over to?"

"A stranger came to the town," they said. "A man with a way of speaking, a creed he was calling people into — we do not know what to make of it. The boy went out to him without his father's leave. He was absent for a stretch of time. He came back speaking the stranger's words and calling to what the stranger was calling to. Now we are told his father has answered him and joined him in it. We have stayed silent for three reasons, and that silence is choking us. The first is that al-Bakhtari has been almost a master to us, by the brotherhood in religion his father once taught us — and we fear estrangement after intimacy, and division after brotherhood. The second is that if they are upon the truth, then we have failed to follow them in it and failed to help them. The third is that if they have gone into error, then we have failed to push them out of it and failed to stand for the truth and its people. You are the head of our religion, and one of our standards, and we have come to tell you, and to take counsel from you, and we will not move past what you say."

Abu Malik did not answer for a moment. Then he spoke, and what he said was not what they had come for.

"Easy, men," he said. "Your news has shaken me. You yourselves know that al-Bakhtari and his son were among those we always counted as men of judgment and of piety — and that they hold long rights and old favors over us. You have told me what you have told me, and I trust your honesty in it. But this is news that has come to a people, and an argument has appeared to them, and they have followed it and been satisfied. We cannot rule them in error — that would be unjust to them. Nor can we rule them in guidance and so wrong ourselves by imitation. Nor can we say they have been deceived and dismiss them without knowing — they are nobler with us than that. What we have to do is look at this news with a fair eye, with the light of justice, with a steady mind. It has been said — he who is steady in his affairs keeps his standing intact; he who hurries cannot keep his foot from slipping. A breach in any ordinary thing can be repaired or replaced. A flaw in your food is easy to remedy. But a breach in religion is grave; its damage cannot be repaired and cannot be exchanged for another — it is like a flaw in medicine, which neither releases its taker nor lets him save himself. And it has been told to us that the best of all stored treasure is the treasure of hearts and the acquiring of manners, whose fruits ripen well. One of those fruits is following the way of judgment. Judgment is the crown of the learned; it lifts them by their noble resolve to the highest of glories, and their virtue becomes plain in every place, and they earn honor easily, by an easy demand. We ourselves have stored away a portion of that judgment — and a portion of those manners we acquired in earlier years, in narrow circumstances, on hard ground. We bore it then for the bounty in it. We seek its help against the misfortunes of time. We cannot waste it. This is the hour of our need for it. So there is no sense in leaving these people alone in what they hold without going to them, opening it up to them, and arguing it through with them — with the judgment of the able, with the openness of two seekers, free of any doubt in what we ourselves hold and free of any accusation against them. They are too noble for us to suppose they have abandoned the truth out of forgetfulness. And they are too solid for us to suppose they have walked into misguidance — misguidance is below them. Falsehood has no craft so fine that the clear truth gives way to it.

"But weigh, with me, the matter of these people. It cannot fall outside one of three states. Either the people are still upon what we know as religion, and what has come among them is only an addition in literature and hadiths and poetry and the abundant words of men. Or the people have been given an increase in what they hold of religion, and they have rejoiced in it — and knowledge is the lost property of every believer, and an increase in knowledge is better than a decrease. Or the people have been given guidance other than what they had — by the prophethood of a prophet brought to them, who called them to it, and they followed it on clear evidence. None of that is denied by the command of God. The creation will never reach the end of God's decree in them or the end of His will, even if they strain themselves to the utmost. God is at work every day."

They sat with that. Then one of them spoke.

"Abu Malik — we came in here with hearts so full of anger at these people that we were ready to call them unbelievers and to settle what to do with their blood and their property. We find our hearts humbled now by what you have said, and by your standing up in argument on their behalf."

"This argument," he said, "is not for them. It is for the truth. Do not betray the truth in the absence of those who speak it. If you make the truth's argument depend on whether its spokesman is here, then I am no defender of them — and yet I am defending you, and myself, from telling lies and from stoning the unseen with what we do not know. Otherwise we become like the tyrants of the nations before us, who became enchanted with their own doctrines, rejoiced in their own learning, supposed they had encompassed everything God had commanded and taught and intended for His creation, and said: God will not send messengers after our messengers. They did not think there was anything beyond what they had reached. So they pronounced sentence on God Himself, that He should cut off His own argument against His creation and against His successors in His land. They made God's knowledge in His servants — and what He had sent down with His prophets and messengers — no larger than the span of their own minds. Anything outside that, to them, was error. So they went astray by that opinion, and they led many astray. They made lawful, by their own ruling, what God had made unlawful; and they killed, by their own opinion, the prophets of God. I take refuge in God for you against following the whims of a people who went astray before. The whim of intelligent men can slope into any meaning and be deceived by any road — except the road of religion, which is gentler to those who seek it, and whose virtue is not hidden."

"Abu Malik," they said, "we do not disobey your command, and your counsel is not lost on us. We would not obey you at the start of a matter only to refuse you at the end. You have pointed to opinion and you have laid out the request. If you see fit, take us to these people, that we may have their doctrine disclosed to us and may look at it. If it turns out to be right guidance, we follow it. If it is straying, we leave it."

"That," he said, "is doubt — doubt about what you yourselves hold. And the doubter knows neither his own right nor the falsehood of another."

"Then we stand on our truth," they said, "and we deny whatever else may be — whatever it is."

"That is not how meaning is sought," he said, "and guidance does not come through denial."

"How then is it sought, if we neither deny falsehood nor affirm the truth?"

"If you already know the truth and accept it, and you already know falsehood and refuse it, then you are no longer in the company of seekers — you are in the company of those learned in prophethood, in guidance, and in authority, judging by revelation over all the people of this world."

"We are content," they said, "with what we hold of the truth. We have no doubt about it. We excuse ourselves from the labor of the request."

"Will you not even allow them," he said, "the advantage over you in one respect, lest you give them the advantage over you in many? If you will not stand among the seekers in the posture of need, then you will sit among the ignorant in the posture of inaction."

"How is it that they have any advantage over us at all?"

"Their advantage," he said, "is that they know what you know, and more besides. This is news that has reached people. If you do not seek them, they will come and seek you. If you will not listen to them, they will press their case against you — and then they will outrun you by sheer initiative and effort. To sit out the seeking is itself the whole of ignorance: it is neglect, it is a fault on the learned man until he teaches and on the ignorant man until he learns."

"Abu Malik, you have closed every road on us with your arguing, and every word of yours is right. Open us a door now, in your opinion, that we can walk through."

"To be in love with your own opinion is blindness," he said, "and to neglect the request is error. There is no shame on the seeker for seeking — but the seeker has to know the doors of seeking before he can seek the truth through them. Whoever seeks the truth without knowing how to seek is the readier to deny it, because falsehood announces itself by its appeal — it agrees with everything — and the truth announces itself by hardship and by going against the grain of the soul. Who, then, leaves what his appetite wants and bears patiently the difficulty laid in front of him — except a man with a sound heart?"

"Here is a door, then. Show us — what is that knowledge, and what is a sound heart?"

"The knowledge of seeking," he said, "is this: that you know yourselves to be in need. And the needy man approaches what he needs with the humility of poverty. As for a sound heart — it is a heart that does not insist on rejecting what comes to it, whatever it is, but waits until the truth shows its own face and proves its worth, and until falsehood lays bare its own nakedness. Then the sound heart sees the falsehood by its intellect, and accepts the truth by accepting it."

"But we have been told," they said, "that a man in that posture — neither verifying truth nor refuting falsehood — is ignorant in what he does. He does not know the truth, so he cannot follow it; he does not know falsehood, so he cannot avoid it."

"What you have been told, you have believed," he said. "But has not such a man left the company of liars and joined the company of the ignorant?"

"Yes — he is ignorant with us, not a liar."

"The ignorant man," he said, "has more hope of good in him, because he seeks. He seeks knowledge for himself, and if he does not, then the learned come to seek him out, because ignorance is the original state of creation, and there is no shame on the man who seeks knowledge because of his ignorance. Lying, though — lying is the worst of merchandise. The liar is not guided by his lying, and he is never afterward believed in anything that gets written against him."

"You have shown us the fault of denial. We will deny nothing again until we have learned it. And you have shown us that the door of the request is opened by the humility of poverty before what is sought. Tell us — that humility, that lowering of oneself before the sought thing: should it come after the thing has shown its truth, or before?"

"Before," he said. "Only before. So that you arrive at the showing by way of the humility."

"And if we lower ourselves before it, and it turns out to be misguidance — then what was the humility? Was it wasted?"

"No," he said. "It returns then as humility before God, and submission before His command, and you receive its reward — even if the one before whom you humbled yourself did not deserve it. Because the seeker has fulfilled what was owed to the seeking, even if what was owed to truth was not in fact owed by the one he asked. It is like a man who comes and stands before a king to ask for a gift. He has no way to ask except by humility, soft words, persistence, complaint. If the king gives him something of what he asked, the king has discharged his debt of generosity, and the king is rewarded for what he gave. If the king gives nothing, the asker has still discharged the duty of asking, gently and in the right way, and the stinginess belongs to the one who withheld. So with the seeker of knowledge. He begins with humility, persistence, and patience under the hardship, until he has discharged what was owed to the request. After that, time brings what time brings."

"This is the plain truth. Now give us leave to go out to these people on what you have shown us, and we will not exceed what you have commanded."

"I would not have shown you anything," he said, "and would not have put myself to this trouble, except for the fear of falling short by turning you off the path of God. I would have rather pointed you to it and gone ahead of you alone — for my own dignity, and out of preferring myself in the goodness. But we go together. I will keep company with you, as an honor to you, and out of gratitude for your having included me in this good."

"It is as if you were expecting this matter, before today."

"This precise matter — no. But I have always been expecting some opening from God, from every direction; hoping for good from every road; turning my ear toward every speaker, looking for an increase from his words, whether he speaks rightly or wrongly."

"An increase from the truthful man we understand. But what do you hope for from the words of the false?"

"Truth added to truth," he said, "is light. And recognizing falsehood, for what it is, in the man who carries it, is an increase in certainty. And whenever certainty grows, the light of the heart grows with it. And no one is saved with God except the people of certainty.

"I am setting out for these people. If you are resolved to come with me — then look. Whoever knows a sin in himself, let him repent to God. Whoever has a debt, let him pay it back to its owner. Wash yourselves clean of impurity. Put on, for this request, the cleanest garments of resolve. And bring before what you seek the intention of a needy man. That is what I am doing. We are going out to God. If He knows truthfulness in us, He guides us to His truth. If He knows from us a mockery of the religion, He turns us away from His signs by the smallest stumble — out of jealousy for His friend, that the mockers should not come near him."

"Yes," they said. "We will do what you have commanded — within three days."

"No."

"Two?"

"No. Only this day. If you do it, then. Otherwise, I go on."

"Then we repent to God of every sin," they said, "and we will discharge every debt we owe, and we will do what you have commanded. There is no strength except in God."

And they did. They did it on the same day. They went out together until they came to the gate of al-Bakhtari's house. They greeted my father, and they sat.

"Abu Salih," said Abu Malik to him, "how is the good son? And where is he?"

My father said: "Today my father is good, and I am the son of Salih. And he — he is with his Lord."

Abu Malik thought he meant I had died.

"Has Salih died?"

"Salih has not died," my father said, "and will never die. He remains, as long as time remains."

Abu Malik sat a moment with that. Then he said, slowly:

"Three things, then, that have had their meanings turned around. The child has become the father. The Lord, in the earth, is present — and the Lord has a servant who is brought to Him through him. The one who was made for ending has become unending. A whiff of perfume to perplex the seekers — and beyond this there are stranger things still."

"Abu Malik," my father said, "name your need. And be careful in your asking. You are at the beginning of an ordeal."

"You are right," Abu Malik said. "The first of the truth is always an ordeal. My need is a meeting with the righteous servant."

"There is no way to that."

"Perhaps you mean your son, Salih?"

"Yes."

"That," my father said, "is by his permission."

He rose quickly and came in to me, and he told me of Abu Malik and of those with him, and I was glad of what he said, because I knew the quality of their minds. I said, "O God, open for Your servants the hearing of the heart, and guide them by Your mercy to the signs of what they are seeking, and unfold our chests by the light of Your hidden light." Then I drew my hand over my face and cleared the sitting room and told my father to bring them in.

When they had come in and greeted me and sat — Abu Malik brought close to me by my father's introduction — the first thing I said was, "Abu Malik, you have honored us with your visit and outrun us to goodness with your generosity. It was rather we who should have come to you, to keep faith with your affection and to discharge a debt of long standing."

"When have you ever fallen short of the virtuous, O Abu al-Khair?" he said. "You were patient and gentle among us when you were small, and noble among us when you grew. You have become for us a caller and a warner toward the good. Your root is sound, your branch is high, your work is pure — congratulations to you."

I said, "Have you come to me suspicious, Ka'b al-Ahbar — or imitating? Where is the perfect mind I have always known in you, and the noble forbearance?"

"Your standing is too high for suspicion," he said, "and religion stands too high for imitation."

"How then," I said, "could you call me a warner? The warner is a prophet, and a prophet is God's argument set between Himself and His creation — for them and against them. How can he be an argument for you, when he is not also an argument against you?"

"You are right," he said. "That is the obligation. We are not going to deal in this matter by the ease of words. I began with you in the more handsome way. I came to see that honesty is owed to whoever sets himself up as a veil for religion and a spokesman for the truth, and opens his doors to seekers. We have come to know the worth of the request, so we have sought you out and hoped in you as one to be sought from. So come to the heart of our need."

I said, "Abu Malik, is not the man whose worth you have come to know also one to whom a debt is owed?"

"Yes — owed."

"And now that you have come to know the worth of the request, do you know its right?"

"What is its right?"

"To know its faces," I said, "and then to seek, within each, what is owed to you of the truth."

"What are its faces? And how many?"

"The seeking is of three faces," I said. "There is a seeker who knows. There is a seeker who is learning. And there is a seeker who knows nothing except that he is a seeker. Which of the three are you?"

He paused. Then he said: "I am in need of knowing all three. How can I claim a station I do not know?"

"How then," I said, "can you claim to be a seeker at all, if you do not know the faces of seeking?"

"I did not know them. So show them to me."

"Wealth," I said, "is the treasure of the people of this world. And knowledge is the treasure of the people of the next. The seeking of wealth has three faces: a man who seeks what is already his own, a man who seeks what is owed to him on a term, and a man who seeks what is given in charity. The seeking of religion, the treasure of the next world, is also of three faces. There is the seeker who knows — the divine scholar — who goes out after the dead in their ignorance to revive them by his knowledge. There is the seeker who is learning — the student who has reached some of the degrees of knowledge and is seeking its end. And there is the seeker who knows that he does not know — the ignorant man who knows nothing more than that he is in need, and who looks for the learned in order to learn. These are the three faces, and these are the descriptions of those who seek."

"Yes," he said. "These are the faces of seeking, in religion and in the world. And I am the third — the seeker who knows nothing, who knows only that he is in need. Teach me."

"Did you come to know," I asked him, "or did you come to recognize?"

"What is the difference?"

"Knowledge is report," I said. "Recognition is sight. Knowledge by report is the case of an ignorant man who does not know he is ignorant until he meets a knower. The knower tells him his ignorance, and so he learns by another's word that he is in need. His knowledge of his ignorance has reached him as news. Recognition of his need, of his ignorance, is when a man already knows, by his own mind, that he is asking after something and does not see the face of the answer. His chest is narrowed by the not-knowing, and he hopes for nothing to relieve him except a knowledge of what he does not know. His ignorance has, in him, already become a recognition by his own intellect. Ignorance is need. Need is narrowness. Narrowness is a hard pressure that drives the creature to look for breadth — and breadth is the breadth of knowledge, because knowledge is wide. By this, I see that you are in need."

"I find all of it in myself," he said. "I know I am in need, in dire poverty. Stop up my poverty by your favor."

"I see you hurrying the request," I said, "before you have stood firm."

"Poverty hurried me with its narrowness, so I hurried. And I came to know the worth of breadth, so I sought."

"If poverty hurried you, and you came to know the worth of breadth — what then is your acquaintance with the one you are seeking? How do you know that I am the one with the breadth, that you should ask of me? How do you know that I am the one to be sought, that you should come for me? No knower of prophethood pointed me out to you so that you would know. You have not seen from me the markers of evidence so that you would recognize. Do you not see that you are raising the request to the easy level of imitation? That you wrong wisdom by it, and you wrong yourself? You are like a man who measures with the bottom of the bushel: if he does not press his cause, he wrongs his counterpart; and if he presses it, he wrongs himself. So with the man who takes knowledge in imitation. If he acts on it, he is not thanked; if he leaves it, he is called a denier. You, I think, have a finer mind than that."

"I know it is so," he said. "But I had hoped the truth would prove itself, and that I might excuse you from having to prove it. Disputation breeds rivalry, and rivalry breeds grudges — each man wanting his own advantage over his opponent. How does the truth ever lay hold of a man who is locked into defending falsehood?"

"You have said it well," I said. "The truth does prove itself by its own worth and its own honor, and when dispute grows long, hearts get drawn away from what was more important. But there is no leaving the way the learned have set down. They have said: every sowing whose ground has not first been made glad will not flourish; and every knowledge for whose sake the heart has not first been cleansed by argument will not grow."

"Then bring whatever arguments you wish," he said. "The words of wisdom are like a jewel: turn it over, and its light shines; set it down, and its worth is not hidden."

"You speak truly," I said, "but only where the jewel is not in the company of camouflaged glass made to look like it. When that is so, the jewel is not unmistakable. And when claims multiply, and the tyrants of this world fight over religion until they have dressed it in something not itself and disguised it so that the seekers cannot tell — then the seeker is obliged to have the jewel tested for him, so that its worth shows, and so that the imitation, and the people of the imitation, are exposed."

"You have done justice and discharged what is owed to the truth. As for the imitation of these people, we know it and its faults, and we have rejected it. We have held instead to this opinion we know by, and we have called it the sound jewel of speech. We have stood by it, and you stood with us in it, and we all clung together to its rope. If you have now found something more inward than it, more truly the jewel — well, by my life, jewels do differ in their worth. Show us, then, where the worth of your jewel lies, by an ordeal that brings it out. Our jewel we have known, and you have known it. Say of it whatever appears to you."

"Your jewel," I said, "was held by us as generous, in the rank of a sound jewel — until we presented it to the jewellers and the men of the mines, and they declared it counterfeit. They would not accept it from us as a jewel, and they said: this is camouflaged glass. They showed us its faults, and it fell out of the class of jewels. A jewel rises by its preciousness and by the recognition of those with the eye to know it, and they vie for it, and its price climbs to whatever it climbs. If the jewellers reject it, who else knows it? Who accepts it afterward, except the people of vanity, who neither buy nor sell? So with knowledge. It is not knowledge until it is set before those who know the science of the heavens. If they accept it, it is true knowledge. If they will not accept it, it is empty talk — and empty talk is not allowed in wisdom and not accepted by the wise."

"And who are these who know the science of the heavens," he said, "after the prophets and the messengers? We know of no such men in our own time, and the people have had no such acquaintance since revelation by Books was cut off. The nations now bind themselves only by reports. What do you say to that?"

"Any religion held only by way of report," I said, "is not called a religion. Religion is one, upright for God by His own right, with no difference in it — and you know what difference there is in the reports, and how some of them give the lie to others. The religion of God is too precious for that."

"When they differ in the report," he said, "they go back to the Books. What agrees with them, they accept. What disagrees, they call a lie."

"And what is in the Books only in general terms — who lays it out in detail for them? And what is detailed in them, who joins it back together for them? And who among them is trusted, exactly, with God's lawful and His forbidden? I heard you on the day of the dispute. A distressed man came up to you and said, Abu Malik, I swore an oath of divorce, and I broke it. You said, A group of scholars hold that she is finally separated from you and is no longer lawful for you until she marries another husband. Another group hold that she is still your wife, and that a single pronouncement does not make her forbidden to you. The man said, Which of the two should I act on? You said, Act on whichever of the two you wish. He got up from you and went home not knowing whether what he was going home to was lawful or forbidden. Is that how a judgment is made in the religion of God? Is that how God's lawful and forbidden are settled?"

"By my life," he said, "the Law has been spoiled and emptied out, and what we hold to now is only the name of religion. What is the state of religion with you?"

"And what is religion, Abu Malik?"

"Command and prohibition. Lawful and forbidden. The Sunnahs and the obligations."

"And what is the sacred Law, then — anything other than that?"

"I know nothing in it but what I have just described."

"Then you have folded religion and the sacred Law into one ground."

"Yes."

"Then religion has been emptied by the emptying of the Law, and corrupted by its corruption. What is left to you?"

He cut off there. He sat thinking, his head down, for a long moment. Then he lifted his face.

"I do not see that anything is left to me. What is religion with you?"

"Religion with me," I said, "is what has its veil pulled tight against corrupters and its doors opened for seekers. Its bringer is honored by his precedence, and its seeker has joined the good. Through its worth, deeds are ennobled; through its justice, the codes of law shine. It is a cause connecting heaven to earth — joined, not broken. It is the handhold of those who hold fast and the rope of those who cling, the ark of stillness and the ship of rescue and the light of life. Its content is generous, its sciences are safeguarded, its jewels are marked — and its worth is not hidden from anyone who really looks at it."

"If God has a religion by which He will judge on a day, which pleases Him, by which He rewards and punishes — then by this description, it does exist. The worth of the description is plain now. How then is the One described?"

"Goodness," I said, "comes only from one who is good, Abu Malik. Set your light in the niche of your own mind and understand what is meant for you. The claims of what was decreed for you have closed in on you. The robes of vanity have slipped from you. You are charged with what no one but you can carry. And you have taken upon yourself the affair of these men who follow you in good — they have listened to your words, accepted your guidance, believed your opinion. It is your duty to guard yourself and to guard them from a fire none of you can avoid passing through. Seek the way of escape from it. You have a greater share of God's blessing among them than they have, and a heavier duty of thanks. I know that you have always known the good and hoped for its reward. As for what you ask — that I confirm what I am calling you to — I owe it to you to confirm it, from whichever face you wish."

"All you have said up to here is solid truth," he said. "As for the reports of the common run of people — religion cannot be set right by them, and they are not fit for it. There is nothing left for us but to know what it is you are calling us to."

"I am calling you to the justice of God and to His oneness."

"You have called to a fine thing," he said. "We say this very thing, and we know its excellence above every other doctrine."

"It is not where your mind is heading, Abu Malik. The doctrine you mean is a description of justice without knowing the One described, and a use of God's names without knowing His oneness. What I am calling you to is the knowledge of God's justice and the landmarks of His oneness. If you wish, I will stop you on the corruption of that doctrine of yours at whatever point you like."

"If the doctrine of the people of justice is corrupt, and their oneness is denial of God, then there is no firm truth in the earth — unless there is a meaning outside all the doctrines. Show us, then, where the corruption of justice and oneness comes from."

"Justice and oneness," I said, "are among the most honored of all doctrines. Oneness is too great to be corrupted, because there is no opposite to oneness. Justice is not corruptible, because it is the judgment between God and His creation. But the doctrines are all corrupted by what their carriers have corrupted in the saying of justice and the rest — for they are claims by which no statement is established; they do not encompass the knowledge of what they say, until they come back to God's command. So defend whichever of them you wish, take it on as your own, until I peel it off you with the argument of the truth."

"I am not going to defend a saying I do not myself say. Its author did not ask me to. He did not appoint me as his agent. I am no protector of the men of the doctrines. I will defend, in front of you, only my own saying. And I say: God is one, nothing is like Him; He is just in His ruling, He does not burden His servants beyond their bearing. He commanded them and forbade them — not by compulsion in the command and not by coercion in the prohibition. His command is choice, and His prohibition is warning. The obedient one did not obey under force, and the disobedient one did not disobey under defeat. This is my meaning, and this is what I say."

"Did I not just say to you," I said, "that this doctrine is among the most honored? But these are names and attributes that do nothing for you without the meaning that lies behind them. Like your saying, fire is hot. You speak the truth — but the truth, like that, gives you nothing. Saying fire is hot is a name and an attribute, and naming and attributing do nothing for you when no food is being cooked by it and no darkness is being lit by it. If names and attributes stood in place of meanings, then the mention of fire would burn your tongue, and the mention of food would fill you up."

"You have spoken truly about those things," he said. "Their names and attributes are of no use without the existence of their meaning. Show me, then, that my saying of justice and of oneness is the same — a set of names and attributes that requires the knowledge of a named, described thing behind them."

"Your saying that God is one," I said. "One is four letters. Two of them are joined and two are separate. Which of them is one — or are all of them together one? Your saying Allah — that is four letters. Which of them is Allah, or are all of them together Allah? Or are they signs pointing to Allah? Or do you say otherwise?"

He sat with his head down for a long time. Then he said, "I find no way out of this except to say that they are signs pointing to Allah."

"Which of them points to Allah? Or by what do they point to Allah — by being separated, or by being joined? Or by some being alone while others stay connected — as in your saying Allah, where the alif stands alone and three letters are joined? Which of those is the pointer? That alone, or those connected?"

"I have nothing to say to this. Take us out to another door."

"Then the door of knowing oneness has been shut on you, Abu Malik, by whoever spoke to you in this way."

"Yes."

"Then let me ask you about your acquaintance with God. Where is it from? From whom did you take it? From an attribute that pointed you to the One described?"

"No."

"From a name pointing to a named one?"

"Yes."

"And what is the name that pointed you?"

"Allah."

"Is Allah a name, or the Named?"

"The Named."

"Then what is the name?"

"Allah."

"So Allah is a name for Allah?"

"Yes."

"Then what is the difference between the name and the Named? And which of them do you worship — or both?"

He sat baffled, turning the answer over. Then he said: "I find no doctrine here except that I have seen, in all the crafts, that each made thing has a maker. So I judged that this enormous making has a Maker who resembles nothing of what He has made — just as makers do not resemble the things they make."

"Abu Malik," I said, "you have gone out, with us, from the original meaning. The narrowness of the meaning of oneness has driven you to seek God by analogy, to set up parables for Him — and God is too dear for that. Because each thing in your analogy was made from a thing; and God did not create things from any thing that was before them. The craftsmen — you only know them because you watched some of them with your eyes, how they make things, and so you ruled on a making whose Maker was hidden from you, by what you had seen of an action like it from the craftsmen you watched. By analogy. But when God created — He did not have you witness His making, and there was no other one like Him doing the same kind of making, that you could compare to. You have testified by the present likeness about an absent Likeness — and I have never known you, Abu Malik, to be satisfied with analogy in your religion. How can you be satisfied with finding the knowledge of your Lord by analogy? Your saying, nothing is like Him, implies, in its own words, that there is a like for Him, and that nothing resembles that like. Had you intended oneness, you would have said: There is nothing like Him, and you would not have said: There is nothing like His likeness.

"Abu Malik," I said, "raise your head and say whatever has come to you."

"Speech is gone," he said. "There is no saying. Minds are bewildered before the answer. Imaginations have lost their footing in thought. The instrument is too weak — nothing can be reached by it except asking and request."

"Can speech be heard from Him?"

"No speech is heard except from a speaker, and a speaker is described by his speech, and God is too great for attributes."

"This Vast One, this Mighty One, the High One who holds power — would He be unjust in His creation? Would He burden them with what they cannot carry, command them to what they cannot reach, then punish them when they did not?"

"Then this One, so high above injustice and above aggression — would He be content to leave ignorance as the lifelong companion of His servants?"

"Ignorance," he said, "is a sure denial of thanks, a clinging to unbelief. And God does not love the disbelievers."

"Were they then created already as scholars, knowing what was wanted of them?"

"No. He created them ignorant. They knew nothing."

"Then how do they come to know what He wants of them — so that obedience to Him is set on them as a duty, so that they are warded from His anger — unless you allow that there are causes for that knowledge between Him and His creation? That He sets among them ones who deliver His justice, who stand as witnesses for and against them, whom He has chosen by His own knowledge out of His whole creation and made them messengers to them and arguments against them — so they stand between Him and His servants as the just ones, conveying His command and His prohibition, so that obedience to them is obedience to God and His pleasure, and disobedience to them is His anger and His punishment? Otherwise, by what road would the command reach from God to His creation?"

"This," he said, "is for me a foundation of justice. As soon as creation needed God's justice to teach them what He wanted of them, He set up the just one through whose hand it would come — and each thanks his benefactor, and is rewarded by Him as He wills, or rebuked by Him, or punished by Him, as He wills."

"Is it allowed in His ruling," I said, "to charge one part of His creation with a matter and to point them to it by a just one from among His just ones, and then to charge others with that same matter, but not to point them to it by a just one, the way He did with the first?"

"The ruling can only be one, because justice is one. If the ruling were divided into two faces, one of them could not be free of injustice."

"Then has it not been established for you that this ruling from God is one, with no difference in it, and that the Sunnah is one, not to be exchanged among His messengers? And were it not for the succession of the messengers, and the steadiness of command and prohibition, the truth would have no growth, and creation would have no meaning."

"Yes. That is the truth."

"Then is not their succession from God to His creation in order to demand obedience of them and bind it on them as an obligation? And He made them just witnesses between Himself and His creation — to witness on behalf of whoever obeyed them, that He might accept from him; and to witness against whoever disobeyed them, that He might turn from him."

"It could not have been any other way. Otherwise He would have called His messengers liars and broken His own promise — and God is too generous for that. The matter of the messengers has made it clear that they are of His justice and that the creation is bound to worship Him by obeying them. But tell me about the ranks of these just ones with God. Are they all equal — or is one favored over another?"

"How could they be equal in rank," I said, "when some of them are bound, by obedience, to others? If their ranks were equal, one would not obey another. No — by His justice, He favored some of them over others, so that obedience would pass from the lower to the higher, and from the higher to the one above him, until obedience and worship reach the end God intended."

"And one who obeys the lower among them — is his reward equal to that of one who obeys the higher?"

"Yes. That is the justice of God, by which the reward is owed to every obedient one. He is not asked about the rank of the one he obeyed."

"Can anyone resemble the higher one of them?"

"No — because they come from the Creator with the knowledge of the unseen, in the Books He has sent down to them. No one can come with the like of that except from God."

"Can anyone resemble the lower one of them, if no Book is sent down to him in which the knowledge of the unseen is given?"

"No one can lay claim to that station."

"How is that — when those lower ones do not stand out from the rest of creation by means of unseen knowledge in the way the higher ones make themselves stand out?"

"If the higher one makes himself known from others by the knowledge of the unseen in the revealed text, then the lower one also makes himself known from others by the knowledge of the unseen in its interpretation. Each is from God — both the sending down and the interpretation — and what is with God cannot be reached except through inspiration."

"And the lower one is also inspired?"

"Yes — and for that reason obedience to him became obedience to God."

"If you have set him on a level with the higher one in obedience and in inspiration, why call him lower?"

"Because of his obedience to the higher one, and because of his need for him."

"And what need does he have for a creature, when inspiration comes to him from the Creator?"

"Inspiration comes to him with the interpretation of the sayings and the books and the rest of what the higher ones left behind. As it was inspired to Lot by the hand of Abraham — and the worth of Abraham was made clear by it; and as it was inspired to Ishmael and Isaac, while the bounties and the blessings and the worth were placed in Abraham. The inspiration of God is joined to His just ones in His earth, by the measure of their stations. He does not wish, in His justice, to cut off His command and His prohibition from His servants after granting it as a favor to them — so that worship would be cut off and ignorance left lawful."

"The bearer of the revealed text has shown his truthfulness by the knowledge of the unseen in his book. By what does the bearer of interpretation show that he has the knowledge of the unseen — so that one would bear witness for him with inspiration and bind obedience to him alone, as obedience was bound to the bearer of the text?"

"He comes, in his interpretation, with knowledge from heaven and an unveiling from the highest assembly; and the Book itself witnesses for him."

"Then I bear witness to what the Books have borne witness to. Any interpretation that the sending down testifies to as inspiration from God — to that I yield. Otherwise we would have to deny the Books and the messengers, since their meaning is one. If some of them give the lie to others, all must be reckoned liars. If some of them confirm others, all must be confirmed."

"This is justice. By this the truthfulness is known, and the worth of those who are truthful in it. He has obliged obedience to the messengers of firm resolve, who are His just ones in His creation, and tasked them with delivering His message. Now if obedience is owed to the firm-resolved by prophethood and by the sending down and by the interpretation — what then of these men who sit in their seats, copying them in posture but not in substance? And who then agree, all of them, that after their prophet there is no prophet, and in their own time no warner — that they themselves are the upholders of God's command and His religion, and that what they say leads either to His reward or to His punishment? You say otherwise. How is it that the scholars of this community have failed to look into this stretch of time?"

"They have not failed entirely," I said. "But they have not looked into it the way that would have set it right for them. No one came to them to speak it through, so that they could turn their minds toward it — for they had been made to drink, in their hearts, that they are the foremost in God's command and His religion, and that there is no prophet after their prophet. Just as the misleaders of the nations before them said. Every nation has claimed that there is no prophet after its own prophet."

"God's religion is too dear to God for that," I said. "What gap-time was there after the creed of Abraham, when God sent after him Ishmael and Isaac and Jacob and Joseph and Jonah and Shu'ayb — and through Shu'ayb on to Moses? And inspired them with the doing of good and the calling to the creed of Abraham, and the upholding of the prayer by its Sunnah and the giving of zakat by its obligations — until the ruling of the Torah was sent down on Moses? What gap-time was there after the creed of Moses, between him and the Gospel, when God sent after Moses Joshua son of Nun, and Elijah, and Talut, and David and Solomon, and Zechariah and John — leading on to Jesus? They stood by the ruling of the Torah, holding to its religion and calling to it and reciting its Psalms, until the ruling of the Gospel came down on Jesus. And so with those who passed before them, and those who came after them, and many ages in between whose count only God knows — their messengers came to them with the clear proofs. To each He set parables. Each He called to the truth. Where then is your gap-time? When was the gap-time? God only said:

> عَلَىٰ فَتْرَةٍ مِّنَ الرُّسُلِ
>
> "After a lapse in the messengers."

"He confirmed the messengers and the lapse together, on the appearance of the call after the fear — so they called in secret, in concealment. The earth was never empty of the just one of God for a single blinking of an eye — either present and visible, or fearful and veiled."

"This is irresistible speech, a fixed saying without doubt. The standing of God's argument by justice and by the succession of messengers — that has been settled and made plain. Show me, then, what their hiddenness in our own time means — what is the cause of it?"

"What was the cause of Moses hiddenness," I said, "when he went out of Pharaoh's city and passed beyond it, in fear, watchful, on the road to the land of Shu'ayb?"

"He fled from Pharaoh's people, hidden from them, when he had come to fear for himself and they had agreed to kill him."

"And have you heard, in this religion of yours, of any one of the just — God's just ones, the children of His prophets — being killed, in patient endurance, on the matters of religion?"

"Yes — many of them have been killed."

"Then what just one of God's just ones do you want made to appear, for you and the people of your time? A slain one whom God resurrects and sends to you from death? A fleeing one whom you want to bring back to the killing he fled from? — when he has followed God's Sunnah in His prophets and His messengers, patient under fear, in concealment, until God rules between him and us — and He is the best of those who rule."

"There is no shame on whoever follows the Sunnah of the righteous, in fearing for himself and fleeing from his people. The shame is on the evil communities and the helpers of the wrong-doers, in killing the prophets of God and His just ones, and agreeing together upon that."

"What do you say, then," I said, "about whoever kills God's just one and His proof — where is he going? And the one who helped him?"

"To the Fire. And so too whoever blackened a banner for them, or held an inkpot for them."

"And what do your jurists say about that?"

"They invoke mercy for the slain — and they see no sin in the killer."

"And the one who obeys the killer voluntarily — is he not a partner with him in the blood of the slain?"

"Yes, by God. And he is worse than him."

"His partnership is known, by helping him. From where does he become worse than him?"

"Were it not for the rulings of the jurists, the kingdom of the tyrants could never have been set up over the fools."

"You have spoken the truth, Abu Malik. And are you pleased for yourself, then, that their kingdom is your home, their land your resting place, their armies your neighbors, their executioners your intimates? You are their just one — you rule for them by your knowledge, you settle your own concerns by way of their kingdom. You prayed for them in your asking. You thanked them in your need. You dressed their oppression in the crown of your own justice, and they grew bolder. You clothed their pride in the robes of the sacred Law, and they pressed on further. You lit up their falsehood by letting your own truth slip into obscurity. The weak were deceived by your example, and whatever scholars there were like you. They said: We have a pattern in these — for these are the jurists of the community. They went astray by your means, and you went astray by the means of the tyrants. You imagined that the one to be asked was someone other than you. You forgot the saying of God:

> وَلَا تَرْكَنُوا إِلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا فَتَمَسَّكُمُ النَّارُ
>
> "And do not lean toward those who have wronged, lest the Fire touch you."

"So you cut what God commanded to be joined, and you were watching when you did it. You spoke about the outward forms of justice, while you yourselves were wandering off its path. You went past His saying:

> أَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ وَأُولِي الْأَمْرِ مِنكُمْ
>
> "Obey God, and obey the Messenger, and those in authority among you."

"And you claimed that these tyrants are your people of authority, even when they had disbelieved. And you forgot the saying of God:

> قَاتِلُوا الَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُم مِّنَ الْكُفَّارِ
>
> "Fight those of the disbelievers who are nearest to you."

"So you disobeyed the one whom you had been commanded to obey, and you obeyed the one whom you had been commanded to fight, all the while reckoning yourselves to be doing good. Misguidance ran its full length in you. Your opinion ran its full length in you. Until you said, God will send into our age neither messenger nor warner. Has the beauty of your deeds set you so far above need for the friends of God in the argument? Has the ugliness of your deeds cut you off from hope in God's accepting repentance? Or is it the wisdom of just men, that you rule God by without justice, and that you accept for your Messenger what you would not accept for yourselves in his place? Would any one of you accept that his son should be killed, or that he should be driven from his house and his property, by no right?"

"No one accepts that," he said. "If a child were killed for one of us, or one of us driven out of his property, he would be angry, and his people would be angry on his behalf, and they would not be appeased until they were appeased."

"Then God and His messenger have the better right to your anger on their behalf — when His successor is killed and His friend is driven out of the earth."

"It is hard on us, that the friend of God should be killed among us or driven from his land. But they have deliberately hidden themselves from us, and the tyrants have come out openly over us."

"Had you helped the friends of God, they would have appeared. Had you let go of His enemies, they would not have won. But your behavior, when it comes to the friends of God, has gathered itself into three ranks. There is the killer — the one set loose, who never tires of the hunt, who is not eased even when he catches them. There is the one who is pleased and gloats — biting his fingertips in rage — who never stops urging the killers on against their enemy and never relents toward them, even when they themselves relent. And there is the one who hates what they do, and inwardly holds love for the friends of God, but is one of those who abandon them — without true insight in faith, without anyone able to trust him to keep a secret. For this reason God's friends have hidden themselves from you, by the prudence of concealment — from you, and from this great mass of created beings who have taken their own appetites as their god, and who have hurled themselves on the goods of this world, and who have backed one another up in the killing of prophets. They did not know the fault in what they were doing, so they did not lay upon themselves the wrong of what they had done, and they did not lower themselves to repent of it. Instead, they sent the injustice of their own acts back to God Himself, and attributed it to Him, saying: God will send no messenger after His messenger, and no warner. So they made the killing of God's friends lawful — by no right. They wronged in deed, they deviated in word, they overstepped in denial."

"Yes — this is the description of this community of ours. This is what they say. Where is the rescue from that? Where is the flight from the punishment of God?"

"To God. You take refuge in Him, and He shelters you. You repent to Him, and He accepts your repentance. You ask Him for the road of His friends, and He saves you by your own deed from the deeds of those who have wronged. And so with this whole creation: were they to repent to God, He would accept their repentance — and what would God do with their punishment then? God does not wrong by the weight of an atom, even if they have wronged and lied. He does not hurry to punish them, even if they have hurried to disobey."

"Then I take refuge from God's punishment by repenting to Him and by seeking the way to what pleases Him. Bestow on the one who seeks you, on the one who asks to be guided, your guidance — your Lord will reward you."

"If you do that," I told him, "you will find God, in His forgiveness of you, closer than your own repenting to Him. He loves those who turn back. And you will find me, in guiding you, eager for you."

My eyes filled then, at the mention of repentance, and I broke off speaking — I was overcome by it for a while — and I instructed them to go to my father's house. I went in to my father, the scholar who had set me on this road, and I asked his counsel about the matter of Abu Malik and his companions.

The scholar said: "You know your own people. If you have come to know good in them, then guide them with your guidance. And if you become wary of them, do not let eagerness drive you to harsh treatment of them. Test them by ignoring them for a time without ill will. Have your father be good to them and kind to them for a season. The one who really desires the good is not hidden, and God will not abandon you behind your back. He will open for you, from the light of His success, what He needs to govern the affair of His creation, and from the doors of guidance what will show you the works of those who follow guidance."

So Abu Malik and his companions kept going back and forth in their ordeal until their affair came through to its end, and they knew their own guidance, and the friend of God in their midst inclined toward them in their guidance, and they gave thanks to God for it, and went back to their people as warners — and God guided through them many of His servants to His religion.

And what came in this account in the form of a story was no idle fiction. It was a confirmation of what God has commanded. In it lies the steadfastness of the messengers, and the signs of the successors, and the manners of those who seek.

Praise be to God, first and last. May God send His blessings upon His messenger, our master Muhammad — sent forth from Him to His creation as a bearer of good news and a warner — and upon his successor, the Imam of the God-fearing, the leader of those marked bright on the Day, the beloved of the Lord of the worlds; and upon the Imams from the people of his house, those whom God has blessed and from whom He has removed all defilement and purified them with a full purifying.

> حَسْبُنَا اللَّهُ وَنِعْمَ الْوَكِيلُ، نِعْمَ الْمَوْلَىٰ وَنِعْمَ النَّصِيرُ
>
> "God is enough for us, and the best of guardians; the best of patrons, and the best of helpers."

There is no power and no strength except in God, the Most High, the Most Great.
