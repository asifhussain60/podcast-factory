# The Master and the Disciple

## How to Read a Conversation Made of Doors

Let me tell you how a certain conversation reached us, because everything about the way you read it depends on hearing it the way it was first spoken.

We have been told that some groups among the believers, together with a number of the preachers of religion, came to a Master among them and spoke the way people speak when they have been handed something they cannot quite put a name to. "You have set us free," they said to him, "by making us aware of what you have made us aware of — and in doing that very thing, you have laid an obligation on us that we are bound to fulfill." As they understood it, that obligation came in three parts, and each part was a debt of gratitude: thanks for the summons itself, for that to which he had called them; thanks for the knowledge he had shown them; and thanks for the work he had commanded them to do.

So they asked him to make each of these plain. Show us, they said, from every one of them, what a person who truly wills to render such thanks may actually go and do. Understand — they were not asking out of ignorance about the shape of the thing. They had already grasped, from the limits of religion, what is obligatory upon them and what falls to them to perform: what belongs to the student in the way he frames his question, and what is required of the one who is asked in the way he gives his answer. And they had grasped, too, from the doctrines of the righteous and the manners of the seekers, as much of all this as can be easily put into words. What was still missing was only this: to be told how thanks is turned from a feeling in the chest into something the hands and the days can carry out.

The Master answered them by first setting the whole matter in its proper light. The affair to which I have called you, he said, is the one I hold in honor. May Allah be with His servants, and may He bring it to completion for them, and ennoble those who answer His call. And then he opened the structure of the thing — and it was here, if you are reading closely with me, that the conversation revealed itself to be built entirely of thresholds, each thing giving onto another.

He has made for the first of every thing a last, and for the last of every thing an end, and for every end a further end whose limit is never reached. These, he said, are the stations of those who possess understanding, the houses through which the traveler passes:

> مفاتيحها ذكرى، وأولها بلوى، وأوسطها هدى، وآخرها تقوى

Their keys are remembrance: the first of them is affliction, the middle of them is guidance, and the last of them is piety.

To read the conversation rightly, then — this is what I want you to hold onto — is to read it as a passage through these houses, one after another, in their order. Whoever has set himself to know the structure of things, and has taken his stand upon the very limits of obligation that lie within his reach, has already turned his face toward guidance by the mere features of his request. He has stepped onto the road, and he is walking it in the best of ways. Do not imagine the seeking as one thing and the arrival as another: the shape of a sincere question is itself the first motion toward its answer.

Only after he had established all of this did the Master return to the three thanks and give each of them its practical form — so that none of them would be left to hover as a mere sentiment.

As for thanking the Master, it is to obey him. The gratitude owed to the one who guides you is not something spoken but something enacted, and its whole substance, from beginning to end, is obedience.

As for thanking the knowledge, it is two things at once: to act upon what is in it, and to call others to it. Knowledge that is merely received and then hoarded has not been thanked at all; it is honored only when it is first lived and then handed onward.

And as for thanking the work, it too is two things: to be patient with it, and to call others to it. The labor that has been laid upon you is repaid by your endurance in it and by your summoning of others to share in it, so that the work does not die out with the one who first took it up.

And so each of the three debts — which had seemed at the outset like feelings far too large for any person to discharge — was shown to have a door you can actually walk through: obedience; action and summons; patience and summons. What began as a question about how to give thanks ended as an opening onto the road itself.

## 1. The Persian Who Was Dead and Revived

Let me tell you about the doctrines of the righteous and the manners of those who seek the way, for something has reached me that I want to place in your hands. There was a man among the people of Persia over whom the decree had run, and it had run in an affliction of ignorance. Do not imagine he was empty of gifts. He had a heart of keen retention, an intellect sound and lofty, and a refinement of manners he had labored to earn for himself. And yet ignorance had prevailed over him in this very knowledge I have been describing to you, because it had come to him early and he had grown up inside it, the way a man grows up inside the walls of the house he was born in. So he turned this way and that in the heat of his thirst, taking the glitter of the mirage for the whiteness of water, until at last he reached it — and found it to be nothing at all. He found Allah there before him, and his account was settled. Then his Lord honored the place where he had come to rest and lifted from him the covering that lay over him. He found him astray and guided him; He found him in want and enriched him with knowledge, so that by that one favor the man became one of the scholars of Tur and one of the dwellers of the Bayt al-Ma'mur.

When his guidance was made complete and he had arrived at the very edge of everything he had hoped for, thanks fell due from him toward the One who had made him, and it became his to labor toward his Lord. Now there was a word he had heard from his father, a word that lived inside him the way the soul lives inside the body:

> إن أفضل الحسنات إحياء الأموات
>
> "The best of good deeds is the reviving of the dead."

And so he said to himself: I was dead, and He revived me; I was ignorant, and He taught me. I was not the first man ever to be ignorant, that I should imagine I had outrun all of humanity to knowledge, nor am I the last who will ever be ignorant, that teaching should come to its end here with me. It is a truth binding upon me that I take my place among those who give thanks for this blessing — that I hand this trust on to those who come after me exactly as it was handed to me by those who came before. For this affair belongs first of all to Allah, and it reached me only through a long chain of hands, the first passing it to the second, the second to the third, until, having descended from the highest assembly down to the creatures who are the people of this world, it tore through the veil, and the doors gave way before it one after another, until at last it came to me.

Am I, then, its end? Its final destination? Never. Those who carried it before me and kept faith with it hold the prior claim upon its gains and its merits. And if their gains are not mine to lean upon, then I am not a man who inherits their reward — I am rather a man whom their labor has gained, one of the very souls they were working to win. And if their bounty does not excuse me from labor of my own, then I stand as poor before the seeking of knowledge-through-action as I once stood poor before the seeking of knowledge itself. All this he said within himself, and he understood that what was owed from him was the exact likeness of what had once been owed to him — that the last of his affair, when the reckoning is finally made, is nothing other than its first.

So he went out — out from his family, out from his wealth — walking toward his Lord, answering His call, praying for the good, and calling others to the same obligation he had taken upon himself, so that there might fall due to him, in thanksgiving, the very like of what had fallen due from him. He set out journeying through the lands, passing among the peoples of the non-Arabs and the tribes of the Arabs, searching their faces, laying his questions down before them. Yet he found no one to answer him and met no fellow seeker on the road, until he came to the farthest reach of the island. There he entered one of its cities at an hour when its people were heedless. And as he walked, calling to mind the signs of his Lord and studying the marks they had left, his eye fell upon a company of the people of that land quarreling over religion with no guidance to steady them, plunging headlong into the depths of their own caprice. So he sat down a little apart from them, holding himself aside, listening to their talk and reading their faces to see which among them held to the soundest way.

## 2. A Stranger in the City

Let me tell you what happened as this young man came upon them, and what moved in his heart as he watched.

As he stood watching them dispute, he said within himself: these are the foremost among men, for three qualities show plainly in the very way they argue. The first is that they carry an argument through to its end and do not abandon it half-spoken. The second is that they belong to the people of the Umma. And the third is that they stand nearer to right guidance than most, for they are awake to religion — observing it together, searching after it together — and the one who truly seeks a thing is already on the verge of finding it.

When they had finished the matter they were speaking of, they turned toward him and asked, "Who are you, young man, and from where?"

He answered, "I am a servant of Allah, and one of the dwellers of His sanctuary."

They said, "What is your need, and what is your work?"

He said, "My need has already passed me by, and my work now is to seek it."

They said, "Did anything in what we were saying please you?"

He said, "All of it is strange — to one who marvels."

They said, "But wonder is of two kinds, one praiseworthy and one blameworthy."

He said, "Yes, I have learned that much. And speech, in just the same way, is praiseworthy and blameworthy."

They said, "Then which is the praiseworthy kind, as you see it?"

He said, "That which is correct."

They said, "And what is the correct?"

He said, "That whose origin is from Allah, and which calls back to Allah."

They said, "And what is the blameworthy?"

He said, "That whose origin is caprice, and which calls to something other than guidance."

They said, "You speak truly. Have you nothing of your own for us to hear?"

He said, "I have nothing to say of myself; I am only a follower of the true saying."

They said, "Then let us hear from your words whatever will show us your way. Your state has been sweet to us and your brotherhood pleasant to us, and we are hoping that you may be a door of mercy that Allah has opened for us."

He said, "Yes." Then he composed himself to speak, and the whole company fell silent before him, and he turned his face toward them.

And this is what he said: "He who created the creation by His power set each thing in due measure to what He decreed for it. He it is who brought forth the open highways of the pasture, and made their outward and their inward alike free of caprice — a way that is neither made ambiguous nor worn down into a track that leads nowhere. Were it otherwise, imaginings would be lost in the very search for Him, and minds would fail to reach Him. Yet He does not stand forth in visible being, as though His attributes might be possessed by another, or as though the aim of His path might be turned aside. Rather He is generous and hidden: His knowledge is concealed by its very grace, and His secrets are kept safe for the people who are worthy of them. Its honor is the plumage of guidance, and its most perfect adornment is the garment of piety, marked with the light of lights, laid up as a trust in the breasts of the chosen and the righteous — the keepers of the Word, the noble scribes,

a trust which they faithfully discharge and a bound which they never overstep. It is the law of the Judge and the bounties of the Most Merciful. He ennobled it and set it deep within, He guarded it and drew a veil across it, He gave it its full due and crowned it with His covenants. And so the resolves of the thinkers cannot reach it, and the schemes of the schemers cannot lay hold of it. Its outward is a remembrance and a proof for the one who is guided; its inward is a clear-flowing drink, whose heaven is brought near to those who lift their gaze to it and whose earth is made level for those who search it out, with marked stations set for those who seek. It has watering-places for those who journey toward Him, and signs whose wonders follow one upon another, each pointing by its likeness back to Him. Its course is a setting-forth of excellence, its method a laying-out in careful detail; its rulings are obligations, and its practice is made up of ordained ways. Truthfulness has ennobled its schools, and justice has made wholesome all that is gained from it, so that for every good it is at once a leader and a road, and to its inner sanctum it is both a shield and a guide. Then minds were driven at last, by their own ignorance and their own want, to seek knowledge and to seek sufficiency.

> فَتَبَارَكَ الَّذِي جَعَلَ اللَّيْلَ وَالنَّهَارَ خِلْفَةً لِمَنْ أَرَادَ أَنْ يَذَّكَّرَ أَوْ أَرَادَ شُكُورًا، وَصَلَّى اللَّهُ عَلَى مَنِ اخْتَارَهُ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ وَجَعَلَهُ لِلْعَالَمِينَ نَذِيرًا.

"Blessed, then, is He who has made the night and the day a succession, one following upon the other, for whoever would take heed or would give thanks; and may Allah bless the one He chose from among His servants and made a warner to the worlds."

Then he added: "Have fear of Allah with the fear of one who dreads Him, and dread Him with the dread of one who still hopes in Him. Seek knowledge, and you will come to know; and work, and you will be shown mercy."

## 3. The Boy at the Door — Limits and Conditions

I want to tell you what happened next, because everything that follows in this book turns on it.

His eyes overflowed with tears, and he broke off his speech. He took his leave of the people, rose, and set out upon his journey. They wept at the warmth and the eloquence of his sermon, and they longed to rise and go with him — but out of courtesy they held themselves back, unsure of what such company would ask of them, and so they went back, each to his own house.

One of them, though, could not let him go. A youth followed him — the youngest of the gathering in years, and yet the most thoughtful of them in mind, a boy whose training had brought his intellect to a fine edge and whose reflection had filled his heart. All of this now sat alongside what he had just heard from the Master, everything that had been laid before him and given to him freely. Understand who this boy was: his father was a great sheikh of the Arabs, the noblest of them in lineage and the fiercest of them against the people of religion. Yet the boy kept close to the Master, with his permission, all the way to the end of the journey. And when the Master reached his lodging, he brought the boy inside with him and called for food. When they had eaten and settled into the quiet that comes after a shared meal, the boy finally spoke.

"O wise one," he said, "you have spoken and I have heard; you have preached and I have understood. You dressed the religion in its finest ornament, you made it something a soul would ache for, offering it with the fullest of provision, and you called to it by the noblest of its trusts. You set minds yearning to question you by the very blows your words struck against the heart. Such a thing could come only from a man whose inward knowledge has been made whole and whose outward patience has grown wide — for he is a fountain of wisdom springing from a firm root whose height towers over all the worlds, and from whose branches hang fruit for anyone who reaches. I hope that you are one of those blessed branches, because I see in you a noble judgment, a sound core, a perfect intellect. You told us that minds are driven by their own ignorance to go looking for knowledge and for capacity. Well — my mind is among the neediest of them, and the most destitute. Is there a way, for someone like me, to life?"

The Master said: "The One who created the creatures by His power did not create them for nothing. He did not make them scholars from the first moment, and He does not abandon them to wander forever in ignorance, and He is not content that stupidity should be their whole culture or the rags of ignorance their only clothing. He has appointed a way out of all of that — and He built the way into them, into the very faculties with which He composed their frame and completed their making. He filled their hearing, their eyes, their hearts with lights: an honor from Him to them, and, at the same time, a proof that will one day stand against them. Then He placed the treasures of His wisdom and the marks of His will along the paths that please Him, among His chosen creatures. He laid the charge of religion upon people and called them to it, and He made it their duty to answer that call using exactly those faculties I just named — so that God might sift the corrupt from the pure. In this way the seeker is made able by his very seeking, and the one who stays ignorant is exposed by his falling behind — so that no argument is left standing against Allah once His Messengers have come, and no excuse is left for going astray once the evidence has arrived."

The boy said: "The thought that there is no excuse left has frightened me — and the presence of the evidence has made me long for it. I beg you for a guide, and I will be your servant."

The Master said: "God did not create men already learned. He created them as small children, ignorant, knowing nothing at all. No one is great except after being small; no one is a scholar except after being ignorant. Smallness is the prelude to greatness, and ignorance is a guide pointing toward knowledge. And in just the same way, knowledge cannot be carried until a lesser knowledge has been brought in ahead of it — something quicker to reach the heart and easier for the understanding to hold — so that its first principles can settle in the mind, the limbs can be humbled to its duties, the breast can tremble at its depth, and the intellect can carry its purpose all the way to the end. This is the way of Allah with His servants. Let us not break away from the community by our own private opinion, nor take as our guide anything other than the established way."

The boy said: "Then I am too small for what I want — so raise me. I am ignorant of what I seek — so teach me. I am bound to you by your own generosity — so have mercy on me. You yourself once stood exactly where I stand, and God led His favor to you by the hand of someone whose trial, before you, was heavy, and whose claim on your thanks became real. I know what it is to be the one who suffers the narrowness of ignorance and the need that comes from having so little of your own to draw on."

The Master said: "The state you describe is exactly as you say. And yet I will not do you the false kindness of doing for you what your own effort is meant to do. A claim is only accepted when it is truthful. Knowledge does not profit a man until he acts on it, and action does not profit him until it is accepted. The truthfulness of a word is its justice, and the proof of knowledge lies in the open roads of deeds and in the documented examples that have lasted across the lands, over which day and night have passed, turn after turn. So do not chase after an argument carelessly, do not throw one away carelessly, and do not accept one on mere imitation — because the first doubt that rises against it will tear it right out of your heart. Do not reject a proof once it stands firm, or you make yourself an advocate for falsehood; and do not give up inquiring into it and understanding it, or your own judgment will come out barren. Think well of me, and hold nothing back; work hard at your questioning, and carry no suspicion; seek the religion the way someone like you ought to seek it, and ask God's help in the whole affair. God never lets the seeker's reward go to waste, and He never denies His recompense to anyone He sees reaching for the good."

The boy said: "You have dealt justly with yourself, in a way that is both fitting and effective, and you have set the truth out in the finest words. I ask God's help — and I ask you about this thing you are calling me to: what is it, whose is it, and to whom does it belong?"

The Master said: "It is the religion of Allah, the one He has approved, the one that is from Him — so that nothing but it is accepted from Him. He first handed it to the elite among His Messengers, and then, in general, to the whole of His creation. On the one specially favored with it falls the duty of thanks for it, of acting upon it, and of calling others to it; on the one deprived of it falls the duty of seeking after it until he knows, and of obeying the one who guides him."

The boy said: "This favor that Allah has singled out for His friends — are all such favors equal in their worth, or do some rise above others?"

The Master said: "Did we not already say that every knowledge has a knowledge before it that taught it, and that each bearer carried only his own portion — so that they stand at graded ranks before their Lord, each one held responsible only for what he actually gained of knowledge? Then every possessor of merit is given his merit, in justice from God toward His creation and in mercy toward His servants."

The boy said: "And this thing you are calling me to — is it that very favor, or is it something other than it?"

The Master said: "It is neither the whole of it nor entirely other than it. It is drawn out of it — the way a single cup of water is drawn from the whole breadth of the Euphrates. I am calling you to what Allah approved for His servants and honored on the day He perfected it: precious, guarded, generous, hidden. Through the reward it carries, its honor is dignity for the one who holds it up; and its penalty is disgrace for the one who lets it fall. Its outward form is figures, and its inner reality is knowledge. Its ruling is a light, and its justice is plain for all to see. This is what I am calling you to."

The boy said: "But what makes you more entitled to this matter than anyone else, more deserving of it than the rest — when by your own account it comes from God for the whole of creation, so that every man could just as well lay claim to it against every other man? What makes you more worthy of the name of truth than they are, when this thing reached you from the very place it reached them?"

The Master said: "There is not a scholar alive who speaks a single word of truth without having taken it from the very same roots we ourselves took it from — and in this they agree with us, by an admission from us and from them both. So what divided us from them? They walked away from those roots while we held fast to them; we obeyed where they disobeyed; we protected what we were entrusted with where they let it slip through their fingers; we fulfilled what we were charged with where they neglected it. They agree with us in what they acknowledge, and they part from us in what they do. Their acknowledgment itself is a witness for us against them, and their loss is the very sign of how poor they are before us, how much they need what our hands are holding.

"They are like brothers who inherited a great fortune from their father and split it evenly among themselves. Some of them squandered their shares until they fell into poverty, while the others kept theirs — guarded it, traded with it, and multiplied the wealth in their hands. And then the poor brothers came back to the wealthy ones, to serve them and to beg something of their bounty. It is exactly so with those who lost the way of their Messengers: their own ignorance drove them to come seeking knowledge from the people of the noble roots. And the proof that we are more deserving of the truth than they are is simply this — their need of us, and our sufficiency without them. Our learning has become the answer to their ignorance, our garment covers their nakedness, our abundance fills their hunger. So the truth is binding on the one who would be virtuous, the credit belongs to the one who does the favor, and the thanks are owed to the one who bestows the kindness."

The boy said: "As for your superiority over them — that has become clear to me. And as for the claim of the truth, it stands against every honest seeker who lets it slip past him. But what argument do you have against me — when I already agree with everything you have said, I stand by everything you have described, I deny what you deny, and I know the truth and its worth just as I know falsehood and the people who peddle it?"

The Master said: "My argument against you is the very same as my argument against them — because although you differ from them in your description of things, you agree with them in the substance."

The boy said: "How can that be, when I acknowledge the truth to its rightful owner, and they deny it?"

The Master said: "Even if your acknowledgment sets you apart from them where they deny, you still stand right beside them in holding back where they hold back. What good is acknowledgment if it never turns into acting on the thing acknowledged? And what harm does denial do, once the whole setting has been put right?"

The boy said: "Then bring me to the people of the truth so that I may know them — or to an agent who can establish it on their behalf. Show me which of the two you are able to do."

The Master said: "Do you not see? You have been driven straight to needing the proof of the rightful owner of the truth, or of his agent — exactly as the common people were driven to needing a proof of the truth itself. Deed has separated you from them, and yet ignorance has joined you right back to them."

The boy said: "You have spoken truly, and now the matter is clear to me."

The Master said: "Have I not firmly fixed my argument against you and against the common people alike?"

"Yes," he said.

The Master said: "And can any man establish for himself a truth that belongs to someone else?"

"No one is able to do that," he said.

The Master said: "Then the argument stands against you and against the common people, and nothing is left but submission — and a turning toward the one you cannot do without, the one who has no need of you at all."

The boy said: "As for my poverty — I confess it. And as for the One who is rich while I am the one in need — I ask of Him, and I ask Him for mercy."

The Master said: "Have you ever seen a man driven by his poverty to a man of wealth walk away with anything except by earning it or by being given it?"

The boy said: "Yes. Nothing is ever gotten from such a man except by earning or by gift."

The Master said: "And do the people of gift ever reach, by way of earning, what belongs to the people of earning?"

"They do not," he said.

The Master said: "Then seek this matter by work, and you will attain it as something you have deserved. Do not go seeking it by flattery, or you will attain it only as a handout — and be left deprived."

The boy said: "You are the key of every good and its guide. Show me the more meaningful of the two roads, the one that runs nearer to guidance. The sheer size of my need for you has bound me to you."

The Master said: "My son, there is no distance between us, and there is no blame on you. What you have hoped for from me will reach you. But it comes with limits that are not to be crossed and conditions that are not to be forgotten."

The boy said: "Set whatever limits you love, and lay down whatever conditions you think best. I hope you will find me patient under your testing and grateful for your kindness."

The Master said: "The first of the limits is this: hold to the outward meaning of the Book and its laws, and act on whatever of it you already know. For whoever acts for God according to what he knows, God will guide him to what he does not know."

>
> For whoever acts for God according to what he knows, God will guide him to what he does not know.

The boy said: "But the Books are many, and every one of them is from Allah. For each Book there is a scholar among the creatures who holds fast to it, and all of them are agreed on guarding it and on the religion it carries."

The Master said: "Now we have circled back to what we said before — and now we have to settle a thing that should never have needed settling."

The boy said: "How so?"

The Master said: "By this saying of yours — that the Books are many, that all of them are from Allah, and that each one has a scholar of its own. Do you really imagine that the words of God, His Books, cancel one another out? That the first of them calls the last a liar, and the last calls the first one?"

The boy said: "No, I do not imagine that. But where is the proof?"

The Master said: "If the creatures had acted on what was in the first Book, it would have pointed them onward to the second; and had they acted on the second, it would have pointed them to the third — until the whole work comes to its finish in the last of them. The last has more right to be followed than whatever came before it — even though all of them are from Allah — because the last is nearer to the present age and clearer in its road, since it confirms and supersedes everything that came before it, and nothing came after it to supersede it in turn."

The boy said: "You have spoken truly, and you have made plain both the knowledge of the truth and the mark of the truthful. I will hold to the limits you have commanded me. So — what are your conditions upon me?"

The Master said: "My conditions upon you are five. Do not fail me if I call for you. Do not hide anything from me if I ask you. Do not come seeking me until I have answered you. Do not ask me for anything until I have begun with you. And do not breathe a word of my affair to your father."

The boy said: "I will do everything you have named — and it is a light thing set against the greatness of what I hope for from you. But how am I to manage with my father? He is among the heaviest of trials laid on me, the very sorest thing I carry. Will you allow me to take myself away from him?"

The Master said: "Withdrawing from him would only be a signal to others. No — stay with him, treat him well, keep him content with some measure of outward agreement. Guard your secret from him and hide your affair, for God will be enough for us against his malice and the malice of anyone else."

Now let me tell you how the days went after that. The Master and the boy would meet, and part, and meet again, over a stretch of days — the boy bewildered in his own affair, knowing nothing to stand on except what the law had brought him, unable to find where the Master had settled, and forbidden by the condition he had accepted from going out to look for him. The term stretched long, and all the while the Master was watching his patience, watching how he bore what had been asked of him. Then at last, on a quiet night when all creation had gone still and the watching spies had grown careless, the two of them settled together in their assembly. The boy felt his need drawing close and the chance of being alone with his companion finally within reach — and he fell down before him in prostration. When he lifted his head, the Master spoke.

The Master said to him: "I saw you fall down in thanksgiving. But thanksgiving before the deed only breeds suspicion — and thanksgiving cut short right after the deed makes the deeds themselves come to nothing."

The boy said: "Every word of yours is truth, and every act of yours is mastery. I gave thanks only for the kindness that has already reached me from you — and by that very thanks I was hoping for more of it from you."

The Master said: "Then thanksgiving is fitting, for gratitude is the thing that makes thanksgiving grow. Your right has been fulfilled. So come to me now with your whole heart, bring your understanding fully present, and listen to what is going to be set before you."

The boy said: "I have brought the instrument of understanding and taken up the tool of hearing. Say whatever seems good to you."

The Master said: "The religion has a key — a key that opens it and closes it — just like the thing that tells forbidden union apart from a lawful marriage."

The boy said: "This key you have named must be great in the sight of Allah, since it is the very thing that parts the lawful from the unlawful, and truth from falsehood. What is it?"

The Master said: "It is the covenant of Allah — the covenant that establishes His rights, gathers up all His obligations, and makes the Garden binding for His friends. It is the rope of God stretched across His earth, and the safe harbor set among His servants. I will recite it to you, and bind you by it."

The boy said: "Yes. Take from me whatever you love. I will not refuse your judgment, and I will not step past your command."

And so — let me bring you to it — the Master set about reciting the covenant to the boy, delivering it to him and binding him fast by it, while the boy could scarcely hold himself together for awe, his tears running down from the sheer depth of what he was being taught, until at last the Master reached the covenant's end. Then the boy praised God, and glorified Him, and thanked Him for what he had been given — and he knew, with a certainty past all doubt, that by accepting their pledge he had stepped into the Party of God (حزب الله) and into the company of His friends. Then he fell quiet, composed and still — and the Master began to open the matter up and make it clear.

## 4. How the World Was Made

Let me set down, as faithfully as I can, how my Master opened the matter — the first of the causes of the manifest order, and how its creation began.

God, he told me, is the Creator of things and the One who composes them. He created them and originated them. He is not the first of every created thing in the way that something might take some prior creation as an evidence for what came after it; rather He is the First of all firsts and the Inheritor of every thing. He began the creation of what He created out of light, and from that light three words branched. The first is the will; from the will branched the command; and from the command branched the saying — that He says to what He wills:

> كُنْ فَيَكُونُ
>
> "Be," and it is.

So the first creation is the will, then the command, then the saying.

These are three words. From them, he said, is derived كُنْ, which is two letters, and فَيَكُونُ, which is five, and that is seven letters, and from those seven letters seven things branch. Out of the light He created air, and air is the first of them: singular, without a consort, unique among things, a mixture that surrounds them all but is surrounded by none of them. It is a proof of the One about whom it is said:

> لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ
>
> There is nothing like Him.

Then, out of those three words, He created water, darkness, and light. And then He created smoke out of the water, condensation out of the heat of the darkness, and fire out of the light. These are three things branching from three things that came before them, and each became a consort to its own origin, because it was created from it. The seventh, air, encompasses them all. This, he said, was the beginning of creation and the beginning of things.

When creation had been established upon seven principles, and the path of forms had been laid down according to likeness and analogy, He created out of the smoke seven heavens and their signs, and out of the condensation seven earths and their powers. These seven branch from those seven origins.

Then, my Master went on, we turn to the signs of the heavens and the powers of the earth, and we ask what they are and what bears witness to them. And in this we return again to the three words and their letters, from which the seven were created — namely the will, to command, by saying. That comes to twelve letters. Its signs in heaven are the twelve constellations, and its witnesses are seven kingdoms; its signs upon the earth are twelve islands, and its proofs are seventeen seas. These are the signs of the heavens and the earth, set before us, which we note and pass over.

Then the heavens and the earth were veiled in darkness from the black heat, which is the authority of the earth. And He pierced that darkness with His lights, so that light and darkness — the two distinct realities — were intermingled. He distinguished between them by the night and the day, and made them two signs. He made the night, and made its sign a way of darkness, and apportioned it; and He made the day, and made its sign a way of light, and apportioned it. He adorned the night with its darkness, the moon and the stars its sign, and the day shines with its light, gleaming through the sign of the sun. Then out of the two of them the days were brought forth — states passing between night and day — and they are seven days. Each day of them has twelve hours, and each night of them has twelve hours.

When the creation of things had been joined to these principles, He made them pairs, male and female. And I do not mean male and female in the literal sense, he was careful to say, but in the sense of the active and the receptive. Then the sunna of creation in pairs went forth, and He created out of its essences every living thing: humans, beasts, birds, and crawling creatures. And then this sunna extended even into speech, so that speech too became pairs — adjectives and things described, names and meanings.

> فَسُبْحَانَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ الْأَزْوَاجَ كُلَّهَا
>
> Glory be to Him who created all the pairs.

He made them manifestations of His command, exemplars of His religion, and signs of His power — pointing by things to the One who brings them into being, and by the creation to its Maker.

Then He pierced it with His light, opened it with His command, created it by His knowledge, and sealed it, among His names, with the letters of:

> بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ
>
> In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.

When He had completed all of them by His making, and composed them in pairs by His measure, He chose for Himself — from the choicest of His will and the secrets of His unseen — an abundant knowledge and a clear light, and He made the two of them a religion by which God is served and by which nearness to Him is sought. Then He honored that religion with laws and with books, made it manifest by parables and by the sunna, and set up for it, from the best of His creation, banners and signs and rulers. He made them the houses of His prophethood, the treasuries of His knowledge and His wisdom, the interpreters of His revelation, and He appointed them His successors in His earth as guides upon the path of salvation, in what is apparent and what is hidden. Among them are the imams and the speakers; among them are the arguments and the chiefs; and among them are the summoners to the good, and the learned. In their stations, he said, they are like the stars of heaven, by whom the works are set aright and in whom the parables reach their end. Their imam is like the great sun among the lights, veiled from sight by its own splendor; he is their apparent one and their likeness. The argument, the gate of their imam, is like the shining moon — its appearance and its likeness. And their summoners are like the radiant stars, their appearances and their likenesses.

All the pairs I have described to you, he told me, are apparent and inward, figure and thing figured. The apparent of the seven heavens are parables, and their inner meaning is the seven speakers, the messengers of firm resolve. The seven earths are outwardly parables, and their inner meaning is the imams of guidance. For each speaker among them there are twelve chiefs, and their like from the seven heavens are the twelve constellations. Likewise, each of the imams of guidance has twelve arguments, by which the case of God is pressed upon his people, and their like in the earth are the twelve islands, the dwellings of the chiefs — and on every island stands an argument. And for every argument there are summoners, many and few, for He increases in creation what He wills, so that the argument of God may reach His creation:

> لِئَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلنَّاسِ عَلَى اللَّهِ حُجَّةٌ بَعْدَ الرُّسُلِ
>
> That mankind may have no argument against God after the messengers.

Through these causes — the causes of God that I have described to you — the argument of God reaches His creation, and His justice appears among them, outwardly and inwardly. The inner is the religion of God, by which the friends of God (أولياء الله) are bound; and the outer is the laws of the religion and their likenesses. So the religion became to the laws a soul and a spirit, and the laws became to the religion a body and a guide. There is no standing for the body except by the soul, for the soul is its life; and there is no standing for the soul except by the body, for the body is its instrument. And so it is with the apparent laws and all else besides: they have no standing except by the inner religion, for that is their light and their meaning and the spirit of life within them; and the inner has no standing except by the apparent, for the apparent is its body and its proof. The apparent is the knowledge of this world, which cannot be seen except by it; and the inner is the knowledge of the hereafter, which cannot be seen except by it. There is not a single letter of the letters of the inner, nor a single one of the friends of God, but that its witness in the apparent is many — because of the abundance of the parables and the breadth of the laws.

Speech on this, he said at last, grows long; and the more one answers of it from the words of wisdom, the more its truth is hidden, and its last makes one forget its first — for the preciousness of it and the purity of its essence. For the light of wisdom overwhelms one part of itself with another, just as the light of the sun overwhelms the light of the moon and the stars and dims them. And so too do the words of wisdom adorn the tongue of the wise.

## 5. The World, the Hereafter, and the Speech of Parables

The boy came to me and said: This world, into which so vast a creation has been set down, holds a great multitude, the first of them and the last, and they cannot agree about it. Some have given themselves over to it entirely and press hard in its pursuit, seeing worth in nothing but the gathering of more and more of it. Others reproach it and practice renunciation within it, and still they cannot do without it — even though everything forbidden is a great deal, while only a little of the world itself is forbidden. From you I have heard neither a word about it nor any report. So tell me: what do you say of it?

And I said to him: As for this world, no one will ever truly reproach it — not the learned man, and not the ignorant one. The ignorant man knows its worth precisely through the ignorance that keeps everything beyond it hidden from him, and the learned man knows its worth through the worth of its meaning. No one reproaches the world but an ignorant man. How could the creation ever be permitted to reproach the act of the Creator? What I say, rather, is that the world was set up — for the one with eyes to see — upon seven things, and these branch from seven origins: heaven, earth, darkness, light, vessels, prosperity, and misery. These are seven qualities, and out of each of the seven there branch seven more.

The heavens, to begin with, are seven. The earths, likewise, are seven. The light has seven days that turn upon seven nights: Saturday is for repose and rest; Sunday is for the limits and the sunna; Monday is for the pairings within the great meaning, al-Kabir; Tuesday has three houses — an apparent, an inward, and an inward of the subtle; Wednesday is for the four, which is the sanctuary; Thursday is the fifth of the causes of God; and Friday is the greatest law of God. The darkness is the seven nights, a covering of paradise laid over the seven days, a night for every day.

The vessels, too, are seven: camels, horses, mules, donkeys, elephants, oxen, and ships. By these seven the open reaches of the earth are crossed. Camels are for the sands, horses for the plains, mules for the mountains, donkeys for the near destinations, elephants for the marshes and the wastelands, oxen for the salt-flats and the thickets, and ships for the seas — seven mounts for seven domains. And the blessings of this world rest likewise upon seven: air, water, light, darkness, food, clothing, and marriage. The air is the open space between heaven and earth, in which the winds arise and the breaths pass to and fro, and it is the life of every living thing. Water is the life of the animal. Light is the very substance of sight; darkness is the increase of all that grows; food is the sustenance of bodies; clothing is a shield that veils what is shameful; and marriage is the seed of reproduction and the cause of offspring. Some of these are the keys of the good, and some are the mines of blessings. Which of them, then, is blameworthy — when every one of them is among the gifts of God and His blessings, for which His servants are bound to give thanks? And their opposites in misery are seven as well: heat, cold, hunger, thirst, fear, sickness, and death — seven things that must be borne with patience, once their meaning is known.

Then the boy said: These are the attributes of this world, and I do not doubt that anything within it is other than the making and the composition of the Creator. So what do the scholars intend when they censure the world, and what do the ignorant intend when they praise it? I see that the ignorant have somehow arrived at the meaning, even while ignorant of it — and if that is so, the rightness of their case would seem to lay the error upon the scholars, since the scholars contradict the people of ignorance in every sense.

And I said: The scholars did not err in censuring this world; it is only that the creation does not understand what they mean by it. And the ignorant did not perceive the meaning of what they praised in the world, for they were ignorant of that very meaning. Whoever is ignorant of a thing is merely accustomed to it.

He said: Then show me the truth of what the scholars say, so that I may rightly hold them true. And mark out the speech of the people of ignorance by whatever exposes its falsehood — for the outward appearance points the other way. My breast has grown weary from ignorance of this, and I hope for no relief except through its knowledge.

And I said to him: This world, and all that I have described to you of it, is an apparent standing over an inward. It has no subsistence except through that inward, for the inward is to the world as the soul is to the body. Whoever knows the soul, the body is made wholesome for him; but whoever does not know the soul and acknowledges only the body dwells upon a corpse — and the corpse is forbidden. It is from here that the scholars forbade the world, because it is dead. They forbade the dead thing that God has forbidden, until it be taken from where God wills.

> من حيث يشاء الله. وبان كذب أهل الجهل بمدح الدنيا إذ لم يعرفوا باطنها وما أراد الله بها. وظنوا أن الله خلقها لغير معناها، والله لم يخلق الأشياء عبثاً. ولو كانت الدنيا وما فيها لم يخلق إلا للدنيا، ينشئ فيها خلقاً

From where God wills — and so the lie of the people of ignorance in praising the world stands plain, for they did not know its inward, nor what God intended by it. They imagined that God created it for some meaning other than its own, and yet God did not create things in vain. Had the world and all within it been created only for the world, He would have brought forth within it a creation that fades away, with no consequence and no meaning. But every act without consequence is play, and every speech without meaning is falsehood — and God is far too exalted for that.

The boy said: The truthfulness of the scholars is established now, their merit and their excellence are proven, and the people of ignorance stand apart, their falsehood laid bare by what has appeared of the argument. What remains for us is the knowledge of the inward of the world and of what is in it. And I see now that the distress I complained of to you has had its narrowness lifted clean away.

So I asked him: What is the reward of the one who brought you out from the narrowness of poverty into the breadth of ease?

He said: Thanks and praise.

And I said: And what is the reward of the one who brought you out from the narrowness of ignorance into the breadth of knowledge?

He said: Obedience and action.

Then I said: This world and all things like it are an apparent standing over the hereafter and what is in it, for the hereafter is its soul and its life. Whoever labors in the world for the world, with no knowledge of the hereafter, his labor is misguidance — for it has no meaning and no consequence. But whoever labors in the world for the hereafter, believing in it, his labor has both meaning and consequence, and his labor is met with thanks.

The boy said: You have spoken truly. So make the features of this world plain to me, and everything in it, and set the parables before me against the things they represent, drawn out of their inward.

And I said: God, blessed and exalted, has created nothing among the things of this world — no beast upon the earth, no bird flying on its wings, nothing moist and nothing dry, nothing fixed and inanimate such as mountains, stones, trees, and the minerals of gold, silver, and gem, nor anything at all, great or small — except that it is a parable struck for a meaning.

> وجميع الأشياء ما كبر منها وما صغر، إلا وهو مثل مضروب. ألا ترى الذرّة على صغرها ولطف معناها لها حدود، أمامها وخلفها ويمينها وشمالها وفوقها وتحتها؟ فذلك ستة حدود وهي الحدّ السابع. فكذلك جميع ما خلق الله من الأشياء

Do you not see that the mote, for all its smallness and the subtlety of its meaning, still has limits — before it and behind it, to its right and to its left, above it and below it? Those are six limits, and it is itself the seventh. And so it is with everything God has created, from the mote and the gnat up to what stands above them of the heaven and the earth, and all that lies between them, great and small — every one of them upon this same pattern. Each thing has six limits and is itself the seventh, and this points to the six origins out of which things were created, while the air that encompasses them all is the seventh that holds them. If your breast is wide enough for these questions — for looking into the parables and matching each thing to its counterpart among the inward pairs — then ask me about whatever appears to you.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the heaven, in its height above all things and the breadth of its regions, so that it has gathered every thing within it?

And I said: Its likeness is the speaking Imam, al-Imam al-Natiq, who is raised above all creation by his merit and the loftiness of his degrees, and who has gathered within himself all the rulings — the rulings of the Sharia — by the sheer breadth of his knowledge.

He said: And what is the likeness of the zodiac and the rest of its stars?

I said: The likeness of the twelve chiefs, the nuqaba, the chiefs of the speaking Imam. And its stars are his summoners, the du'at, who call to the good by the leave of their Lord.

He said: What is the likeness of the earth and its breadth, made a place of rest and a bed for this creation?

I said: It is the likeness of the gate, the bab, of the speaking Imam in his lifetime, and of his successor, the wasi, after his death. It is a place of return for the people and a resting-place for the one who seeks guidance, and it is knowing of the parables and of the inward interpretation, the ta'wil.

He said: What is the likeness of the twelve islands in the earth?

I said: They are parables of the twelve arguments, the hujaj — the arguments of the guardians.

He said: And what is the likeness of its rivers and its springs?

I said: The likeness of those who call to the good.

He said: What is the likeness of the seven kingdoms of the heavens and their orbits?

I said: They are the causes between God and the speaker, the natiq, and they are His. These are the seven and the two orbits, and these are the greatest causes of God.

He said: What is the likeness of the seven seas?

I said: They are the causes between God and the imams of the speaker, standing below those first causes.

He said: What is the likeness of the salt water and the sweet, and the difference between them upon the earth?

I said: The salt water is the likeness of the knowledge of the apparent, in which there is no summons and no summoners — just as the salt water yields neither crop nor anything else. And the sweet water is the likeness of the knowledge of the inward, whether as springs within the earth or as water flowing over its face. Knowledge is just so: the inward is the knowledge of the hidden, while there is an apparent by which the summoners run their course, and an inward that is kept concealed.

He said: How did water come to be a likeness of knowledge?

I said: Because water is the life of every living thing, and in the same way knowledge is the life of every knower.

He said: What is the likeness of the air that encompasses all things?

I said: The air is too great to be set as a parable for any thing that might represent it.

He said: Is it greater still, or are the heaven and the earth as great as it — since they are made into parables?

I said: Glory be to God, it is greater than both of them. The breadth of the earth within the atmosphere is as a ring cast down upon an open plain; the breadth of the atmosphere within the heaven is as a ring cast down upon an open plain; and the breadth of the heaven and all its regions within the breadth of the air is as an egg upon an open plain. What represented thing could ever grasp this and serve as an evidence and a likeness for it?

He said: What, then, is its meaning?

I said: It is an evidence pointing to the One who has nothing like Him, glorified and exalted above whatever they associate with Him.

The boy said: But did the Creator not say:

> فَلَا تَضْرِبُوا لِلَّهِ الْأَمْثَالَ

So do not strike parables for God?

And I said: And did He not also say:

> وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى

And to God belongs the highest likeness?

The boy said: Glory be to Him who set riddles within His Book, that it might be approached only through its own door — and you are one of its gates. So make it plain to us: how can both of these be?

And I said: Everything has a meaning, and the meaning of the parables is this — the apparent is an evidence of the hidden. He, blessed and exalted, forbade that He be measured against anything of His creation, so that it might be said, "this is like God," in the way one says:

> هذا مثل الإمام

This is like the Imam, or:

> هذا مثل الداعي

This is like the caller — or, "this is like the argument." Their likenesses became signs pointing to them and marking out their ranks, one above the other. But it is not fitting, in the matter of His greatness, that the Creator be measured against anything of His creation, in word or in deed, so that it is never said, "this is like God." Rather, the parables are to be taken according to their meanings, for their meanings carry signifiers: the lesser points to the greater, and the greater points to the supreme. And it is for this that He said:

> وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى

And to God belongs the highest likeness — meaning the highest evidence, for there is no evidence higher than the evidence that points to God. That is the air, highest above the heaven and the earth, encompassing all things while nothing among them encompasses it. It is the highest likeness, the evidence pointing to the One who has nothing like Him. He did not say that there is nothing like Him — for indeed there is nothing like Him — He meant, rather, that there is no evidence for Him like that evidence. To Him belongs the highest likeness, and no evidence among the evidences of any other, of those of His creation whom we have described, is like His evidence. Blessed be God, the Lord of the Mighty Throne.

The boy said: The meaning has grown vast, and the range has widened. Tell me — was there, before the air, something standing between God and it, given the greatness you have described in it?

And I said: Yes.

He said: What is it?

I said: Time.

He said: And what is time?

I said: It is the time of the will, by which He created what He willed and commanded what He wished.

The boy said: You have plunged me into the deeps of the seas of the highest assembly. Return me now to the sciences of this world and their parables, that I may seek help by them in my affair and be strengthened by them to bear what I am meant to bear. I have begun to fear for myself and to grieve for my own mind, lest it be led astray into what it cannot fathom.

And I said to him: You rose high by your question, and so the answer rose along with you to the loftiest of the apparent meanings — and there your judgment lost its way and your mind was bewildered. How, then, would it be were I to unveil to you the inward of it? You and I would be as Moses and the knowing one.

He said: Is there, then, beyond this inward, an inward yet more inward than it?

I said: By my life, the inward has an inward that is higher in station, wider in power, and more complete in guidance — and it is the very utmost of evidences toward the path of salvation.

The boy said: I see here three layers of knowledge, then — an apparent, an inward, and an inward of the inward. Is there an evidence for this?

And I said: Where would the evidence be, if not for this, and for that to which it points?

He said: Then show me a likeness of these three layers.

I said: I will show it to you from the Book of God Most High, from the argument of the intellect, and from what the great body of the parables points to.

The boy said: I did not think there was any doctrine beyond what I had already reached. Truly the affair of God is greater than the fancies of those who strain after it can ever rise to.

And I said: As for the Book of God Most High, take His word:

> يَا بَنِي آدَمَ قَدْ أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْكُمْ لِبَاسًا يُوَارِي سَوْآتِكُمْ وَرِيشًا وَلِبَاسُ التَّقْوَى ذَلِكَ خَيْرٌ

O children of Adam, We have sent down upon you a garment that veils your shameful parts, and feathers for adornment; and the garment of piety — that is better. These are three: an apparent, an inward, and an inward of the inward.

The boy said: You have spoken truly.

And I said: As for the argument of the intellect: a thing is not known except by a name, an attribute, or a meaning. The meaning is the very reality of the thing; the name is what it is called; and the attribute is what points to it. And so it is with the religion of God — it has an apparent, which is a name, and an inward, which is an evidence pointing to it, and it stands only by the two together. Nothing is pointed to except by a name or an attribute, as in the words of one who says:

> طلع القمر

The moon has risen. The meaning is the disk; "moon" is its name; and "has risen" is its attribute. Were he to say only "the disk" and then fall silent, it would carry no meaning; and were he to say only "moon" and then fall silent, that too would carry no meaning — until, joining the name to the attribute, he says, "the moon has risen," and at once the meaning is made plain and the speech is sound. It is just so if you say "apparent": it calls for an answer, so that one asks, "the apparent of what?" And if you say "inward," it calls for an answer, so that one says to you, "the inward of what?" But when you say "the apparent of the religion and its inward," then the speech is understood, and it becomes a religion having an apparent and an inward — exactly like the thing that has a name and an attribute. And so He said:

> وَذَرُوا ظَاهِرَ الْإِثْمِ وَبَاطِنَهُ

And forsake the apparent of sin and its inward. Thus sin, too, became a thing having an apparent and an inward. And so it is with the religion of God, the Greatest: the laws are its apparent, and the inward is its inward, and it stands by itself. And for the knowing of its meaning there are many parables that point to it — such as the hidden egg, which has an apparent that shields it, an inward that is the white within, and an inward of its inward, the yellow yolk, which is its essence and its meaning.

## 6. Three Layers of Knowledge

He put it to me plainly. Are not this world and the Hereafter a pair, he asked, and the soul and the body a pair, and the knowledge of the apparent and the knowledge of the inward a pair — and so on through many other pairs, all of them of this same kind?

I told him I accepted it. But are they, I asked him, pairs of a single kind?

The pairs, he said, point to the religion of Allah.

I accepted that too, and I opened it out for him. In every pair the outward is a name and the inward is an attribute, and the attribute signifies the knowledge of God and the religion of God, which is the inner of the inner. And in just this way, I said, the creation was set upon three stations. The first is the creation of the angels, the second the creation of the children of Adam, the third the creation of the beasts. The apparent is the knowledge that marks the very limit of the beasts, and whoever knows only this, without the inward, stands no higher than they do. The knowledge of the inward is the knowledge proper to the children of Adam, their own limit, and whoever knows it is a believer and stands at the level of true human beings. The knowledge of the inner of the inner is the knowledge of the angels, and whoever comes to know it becomes spiritual in his knowing while he remains bodily in his frame. Such a one is a prophet, sent by God into His land, by whom He establishes His argument over His creation — the veil of the angels and the interpreter of revelation to the children of Adam. And so, I told him, the people come down to no more than two men. One has learned the very purpose of knowledge, and his soul has been quickened by the spirit of certainty, so that he is a scholar by his knowing and a master by his working. The other is a learner still, walking the path of his own deliverance. As for all the rest, they are savages and rabble who know nothing, followers of every braying caller, led astray by his error and seduced by his ignorance. They imagine they are doing well, but they have been deprived of the true examples that passed away before them, and Allah will not fail in what He has promised — for the deniers there is the like of it.

So this, he said, is how virtue is, and this is how knowledge and its exposition are. Blessed are the friends of God, and blessed are the followers who do good. Then he asked me the question that was really pressing on him: Can I attain this great station by way of the stations that lie beneath it?

Far from it, O my son, I told him. That is a station God has honored above all stations and degrees, and it is reached only by excellent work. If you do its work and strive its striving with truthful certainty and a pure, wakeful heart, I hope you will realize of it what the chosen and excellent friends of God have realized.

And who, he pressed, may even aspire to reach the like of that station to which God has raised His friends?

Their God is your God, I answered, and between God and anyone there is no kinship. Nothing brings the creation near to Him but piety and righteous work. As for the knowledge by which God has raised His friends — you stand at its very beginning, and if you work by it you will reach its end. It is a due upon God that He bring you to the reaches of the righteous among them, and that He deprive you of nothing of what He gave them.

Then can you help me toward that by some meaning, he asked, as your favor was first extended to me?

This, I had to tell him, is a station in which nothing avails but the work of its own people.

But how is that, he said, when you were able to help me before?

I answered him with what I knew to be true. The sower can sweeten the soil, cast the seed, and give the water — but he cannot bring forth the plant and its sheath. A man can sow his seed whenever he wills, yet he cannot fashion from it whatever he pleases. It grieves me, O my son, that you should seek my help in a thing and I be unable to help you in it. May God expand your breast and light your heart with guidance. So keep the fear of God and do what is best, for God does not waste the reward of the one who works well. You are already upon the road of deliverance, the way of guidance, the path of the people of piety. Walk your way, whose beginning you already stand upon, and hold fast to the rope to which you have been called, until you are led by a rope from God to the rope of God — for that is the goal of all who seek.

Is not the rope of Allah, he asked, the Imam to whom you have called me?

He is what is apparent of it, I said. He is your rope and the most trustworthy handhold, your proof, and the gate of your Imam.

Then what is the rope of Allah, he wanted to know, and what is the rope that comes from Allah?

That, I told him, is the end of your guidance and the utmost reach of the worlds.

There is no power and no strength except in Allah, he said. He who has no guide has drowned in the depths of ignorance, just as he who has no ship has drowned in the seas of this world.

I caught his own words and turned them back to him. And what is this strength you have just named, which cannot be had except by Allah?

We say, he answered, that I have no device by which to draw a benefit to myself, and no strength by which to ward off from myself a harm I dislike, except by Allah.

Then why, I asked him, do you say *There is no strength except in Allah*, and not *There is no device except in Allah*?

He gave me the learning he had received. We have it narrated in our sciences that the *hawl* is the year, by the words of God:

> حَوْلَيْنِ كَامِلَيْنِ

two complete years, meaning two years. And we have had it narrated, as the common people also relate, that *quwwa*, strength, means mules, by the words of God:

> وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْكُنُوزِ مَا إِنَّ مَفَاتِحَهُ لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ

And We gave him of the treasures such that their keys would weigh down a band of men possessed of strength. They said the band was forty men and the strength was mules, and that the keys of his treasures were the load of forty mules.

So it is as though, I said back to him, by your words *There is no strength and no power except in Allah*, you meant *There is no year and no mule except by Allah*. Is this, with you, the interpretation of your saying and its meaning?

He answered me honestly. I have no other word than this, and it is a feeble one that resembles nothing of the speech of wisdom. I reckon the meaning within it is something other than this — something stronger in speech and firmer in the mind. Have the two words an inward interpretation beyond this?

The creation, I told him, has not uttered a single word of truth but that it has a root in the inward, nor a word of falsehood but that it once had a meaning, for speech was turned away from its meaning until it became absurd. And what is absurd has simply been moved from its place, just as truth, when it is altered, becomes falsehood. This is a speech by which what follows is grasped by analogy and pattern, as what came before it was grasped by likeness and parable.

You have spoken truly, he said. So what is the meaning of the two words and their like?

As for *al-hawl*, I told him, he is the speaking Imam of God, His truthful successor, the master of the twelve deputies — just as the *hawl*, which is the year, is completed only in twelve months. The Imam was named *hawl* because he turns the speech of the Creator, in the fineness of its essence, into the speech of the children of Adam, until it is understood and becomes plain speech. So the speech of wisdom takes on for him a body and a frame, while the speech of the Creator carries within it the spirit of life and the light of deliverance. The excellence of the speech of wisdom over all other speech is the excellence of the speech of the Creator that lies within it. The Imam was named *al-hawl* by this very turning of speech, and he does not turn it except by the leave of God. And *quwwa*, strength, is his proof and his gate, whom God has strengthened to bear the heavy burden of utterance. He takes the speech from the Imam in sum and distributes it among the summoners in detail, each according to his station — and so the proof was named strength, and he is strengthened only by God. It is for this reason that the speaker says *There is no strength and no power except in Allah*, meaning there is no Imam and no proof except by Allah, and from Allah come their light and their sustenance. As for the band, those possessed of strength — they are the ten proofs. So too did Joseph (ع) say to his father:

> يَا أَبَتِ إِنِّي رَأَيْتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ رَأَيْتُهُمْ لِي سَاجِدِينَ

O my father, I saw eleven stars and the sun and the moon; I saw them prostrating to me — meaning by the sun the Imam, by the moon his proof and his gate, and by the stars the completion of the twelve deputies. And likewise the brothers of Joseph said:

> لَيُوسُفُ وَأَخُوهُ أَحَبُّ إِلَىٰ أَبِينَا مِنَّا وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ

Truly Joseph and his brother are dearer to our father than we are, though we are a band — meaning the twelve deputies — that he should choose over us one man from among us. So there entered upon them a failure of judgment in the matter of Joseph (ع), and the risings of envy toward him appeared in them when God favored him. Yet the command of God was fulfilled in Joseph, and the end of their affair was remorse and turning back to him.

Praise be to God, he said, who cleansed them of defilement and returned them to the ground of repentance and the ways of turning back. Then he asked me another: What is the meaning of the king's saying:

> إِنِّي أَرَىٰ سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ

I see seven fat cows being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others dry. What are they — and what is the vision seen in sleep?

As for the vision, I told him, it is like the outward work a man does, which is as a mirage: when he comes to it he finds it nothing. So too is the work of the outward without the inward — nothing of it is accepted, however much there be. And likewise the vision in sleep: nothing is gained from what is seen, however often it be seen. The king said *I see*, in the account of the common people, seven things turning upon seven. As for the seven fat cows, they are the causes God has set between Himself and His creation. As for the seven lean ones, they are the seven appointed speakers, driven by their need to what comes to them from God at the hands of His seven causes. The seven green ears are al-Khidr, a cause between God and the guardians. The seven dry ones are the guardians, driven by their need to what comes to them from God at the hands of these same seven causes. So blessed be God, Lord of the worlds.

And what is the likeness of this among us? he asked.

That, I said, is in the foundation of Islam and its offspring, like a single grain that put forth seven ears — meaning that seven Imams came forth from his loins.

How then, he said, did God forbid this world when He said:

> فَلَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا

So let not the life of this world deceive you?

God has spoken truly, I told him. He did not say *Let not the world deceive you*. He said *Let not the life of this world deceive you*. And life has four meanings: a life apparent in this world, whose end is passing away, and the life of the Hereafter, whose end is abiding. He said: do not work for the perishing life, but work for the abiding life. That is His saying:

> يَا لَيْتَنِي قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِي

Oh, would that I had sent forward something for my life — meaning, would that in the perishing life he had sent forward something for the abiding life. And the two lives have a further meaning still: a life by the knowledge of the outward and a life by the knowledge of the inward. The knowledge of the outward is the life of this world, and it is the lower knowledge; the knowledge of the inward is the life of the Hereafter. It is for this that He said *Let not the life of this world deceive you* — meaning, let not the outward of knowledge, and the working by it alone, deceive you. For He will accept from you only the inward established together with the outward, and He does not accept the outward without the inward.

What then, he asked, of one who knows the inward knowledge but does not know the outward and does not stand by it? What is his station among the people of religion?

It is an ill station, I told him, and his inward will neither stand nor be made sound, once he has abandoned a thing he was commanded to do and made of it a shield for his inward. He is like the outward of fruits: when their rind is peeled from them before their harvest they are spoiled and cannot be set right afterward. He is like the body, whose soul is not sustained in it a single hour once its limbs are cut away. So it is with the outward of religion — when its obligations are neglected, its inward will not stand upright for its owner. And his abandoning of the outward, without leave from the one who laid it upon him, cannot escape one of two traits. If he abandoned it out of incapacity, then toward the inward he is more incapable still; and if he abandoned it deliberately, out of ability, then he is a rebel against the one who commanded him. The rebel is a transgressor, and the transgressor is no neighbor to the friends of God. Rather he is their enemy and they are his — because he has severed what God commanded to be joined.

And one who knows the outward knowledge and stands by it, he asked, but does not know the inward — what is his station among the people of religion?

An evil station, I said, for he is like a body that has grown but into which no soul has been breathed. He is counted among the dead, and death is the abode of the deniers. As one who denies the signs of God, he is an enemy to the religion and its people.

I see now, he said, that the outward is not made sound except by the inward, and the inward does not stand except by the outward; and that this world is lawful only to the one who knows the Hereafter, which is its true life and its inward. So too is religion: it is not made complete for its people until they establish both its outward and its inward.

Yes, I told him. This is the meaning, and upon this the work must be. For to establish all that God has commanded is to deserve His good pleasure, and to abandon any part of what God commanded is to expose oneself to His wrath.

Then is not the one, he asked, who knows the outward together with the inward and establishes them both — a believer?

Yes, I said. He is a believer in truth.

And is it not so, he went on, that whoever knows and then works for the Hereafter is granted this world made wholesome for him, its good things made lawful, and the turning about in its gains?

Yes, I told him. This world is made good only for such as these. They are the most deserving of it and the foremost to it.

Then why is it, he asked, that I see the people of this quality — who are the very scholars in this affair — having cast off the world and all that is in it, until the ignorant among them suppose it forbidden to them? They turn away from this world and look upon it only askance, and their souls are heavy toward it. I see their colors gone yellow with no illness, their bodies weak, their skin clinging to the bone, as though they do not eat their food, though indeed they eat it.

When he mentioned my brethren and described them so, I choked with feeling, and my eyes overflowed with tears, and for a long moment I could not speak from the depth of what moved in me. The boy wept at my weeping, until I began to fear that some of our enemies might come and overpower us. So I took hold of myself, and my heart grew calm, and then I turned back to him.

O my son, I told him, the people you have described in this way looked to themselves by the light of life and busied themselves in their affair with the certainty of deliverance, and they did not deal harshly with their own souls out of any vanity. O my son, this world is seen by wealth and children, and the Hereafter is seen by knowledge and certainty. O my son, the faith of these people in the unseen is sound, so they hold the promise and the warning as sure. Their souls yearn toward what they were promised, and they seek it. The dread of the threat alarms them, and they strive. They fear the agony of death, and they hasten to good deeds. Their bodies have grown weak from the toil of fasting, and their colors have gone yellow from the length of their vigils. They do not sleep with those who sleep, out of fear; they do not eat of their sustenance except as a grudging morsel. The longings of reflection have turned them from the pleasure of ease, and the sweetness of remembrance has kept them from the respite of sleep. Their souls have turned away from the appetites, so the world has not been sweet to them; they remember death often, so life has not grown pleasant to them; they have beheld the bounties of the Hereafter with certainty, so they have looked upon this world only askance. Thus they refused this world, being turned away from it — without ever holding it forbidden.

This is the true report, he said, and in this sense the scholars held this world hateful, while the people were heedless of them, supposing they had forbidden to themselves what God had made lawful for them. You have described to me the delusion of the people of the world in their state, and their heedlessness of the Hereafter.

If the people who cling to this world would only reflect, I told him, upon their own making and the turning about of their states — from weakness to strength and from strength to weakness, from ease to ruin and from life to death — and would then look at those who passed before them out of the vanished ages, among whom the good and the wicked alike went down into a single ground, the good receiving no reward and the wicked no punishment in this world, they would know that they were created for something other than this world. But labor lay heavy upon them, and they would not carry the burden of the seeking. So they took this paltry, nearer portion, and were content with the ease of this world in place of the Hereafter. This is their way and this is their meaning. They have no argument before God and no honor before His friends — and there has surely come to them of the tidings that which holds a warning.

## 7. The Five Shares and the Long Road to the Shaykh

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has guided us to His religion, and we could not have been guided except by His favor. Tell me now about the beginning of this creation and its composition. Is it a single creation or many different ones? For I see so great a difference among people in their opinions and their passions."

The scholar said: "As for the creation of bodies, it is various, as you see in the difference of tongues and colors. That variety is itself a proof of God's power, made plain through His signs, so that the created may know one another: the child knows his father and the father his son, the man knows his wife and no other, and the woman knows her husband and no other. So God fashioned them in His justice. But as for the creation of the instrument, which is to say the senses, both what is apparent of them and what is hidden, that is a single creation, and what lies hidden within will tell you the same as what shows without. Have you ever seen a learned man with three eyes, that you might call it an increase in him? Or an ignorant man with a single eye, that you might call it a deficiency in him, so that one could say the learned man saw by virtue of his surplus and the ignorant failed to see by virtue of his lack? Have you seen any increase at all in the instrument of one man over another? You see their creation as a single, level thing in the outward instrument, and the meaning of their composition is one and the same. From this we have come to know that the inner senses follow the very same pattern. This is justice, and God would not be just in what is apparent and unjust in what is hidden. He did not set two hearts in one breast for the learned man, nor strip the ignorant man of anything. The instrument is one, and the composition belongs to the One who gathered it. By this very instrument God has raised His argument against them, an instrument He gave them which they did not use. The Almighty said:

> لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ أَعْيُنٌ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ آذَانٌ لَا يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا

They have hearts wherewith they do not understand, and they have eyes wherewith they do not see, and they have ears wherewith they do not hear.

Do you suppose that God, in His bounty and His generosity, has laid upon them a burden they are unable to bear? Never. He is fairer than that."

The boy said: "I do not see it otherwise, nor do I claim otherwise. But I ask you about the difference of passions. From where did it come, when the instrument is whole and the origin of the composition is one?"

The scholar said: "As for the origin, it is one. All of them are from Adam, and Adam is from dust, whom his Lord created and into whom He breathed of His Spirit. But they departed from the way in their unions, they were begotten upon a course that was not of the faith, and they grew up in something other than guidance. I do not doubt that among them are children of illicit birth who claim fathers not their own. When the roots were corrupted, the branches were corrupted and grew strange, and their return to the truth became hard, all the more so with the arrogance that entered them against obedience, the sloth that entered them against worship, and the envy they bore toward the friends of God. So they did not keep themselves within the bounds of the lawful, apart from the forbidden."

The boy said: "If the origin is one and the instrument is one, from where then does the difference of passions arise, when their meaning in seeking is one, for every man seeks the good for himself?"

The scholar said: "By my life, their meaning in seeking is indeed one, but they differ in striking the mark, like archers aiming at a single target. They are of one mind in loosing their arrows at it with resolve and with the instrument, yet for all that they differ in whether they hit it. So too the hearts within the breasts and the minds are gathered as one, and the minds are gathered upon the safeguarding of the body and the securing of its dignity. But when the call of truth appeared, they parted ways. Some held that to answer the Messengers and to humble themselves before them was part of their own dignity and a guarding of the self against folly. Others held that to deny the Messengers and to raise the self above obedience to them was part of their own dignity and a guarding of the self against the narrowness of worship. The root was one in both: the seeking of ease for the body and of honor for the soul. So one company chose what the Messengers had promised them of the reward of God, though in this world it was rare and hard to come by, choosing it for its lastingness. The others chose the bliss of this world, though it perishes, choosing it for its nearness, the ease of its asking, and the swiftness of its reward. The very hearts by which the first sought the bliss of the Hereafter were the hearts by which the others sought the bliss of this world. Each looked to himself, and each supposed himself his own good counselor. So they came together in the seeking and differed in the striking. This is of God's justice, His generosity, and the breadth of His giving. To every seeker among them is given what he sought. Whoever desires the reward of this world, We give him from it, and whoever desires the reward of the Hereafter, We give him from it."

The boy said: "But the people of this world are ranked in classes within their world. Some are rich beyond need of labor, kings by the abundance of their wealth, and some are poor and in want of money, serving the rich to receive from their hands. Then the bliss of this world gathers them all upon one footing. Are they all alike in the evil of their end, or is there for some of them a portion apart from the rest?"

The scholar said: "It is of God's justice that He joins the one to the other and does not part them from their companions and those they held dear. So they are equal in His punishment, just as they were joined together in disobeying Him."

The boy said: "And this religion which God has honored and perfected, which He has accepted as a religion for Himself and laid as a duty upon His servants, will He accept it from some men without others, so that they too fall into classes like the people of this world? Or will He accept it from none but the man who brings it whole?"

The scholar said: "If the people of this world are ranked in classes within their world, then the people of religion are ranked in degrees within their religion. Justice makes it binding that the Creator be worshipped to the full measure of His will, the greatness of His sovereignty, and the greatness of their need of Him. But His mercy widened toward them. He knew that among them are the weak, so He accepted from them whatever sincere striving in work was within their reach and whatever effort in obedience they could give, whether little or much. Then He charged each part of His creation according to its capacity and its work, and gave to every man of merit his due of merit, so that they stand at degrees before their Lord. Then, of His justice, He made the end of their obedience to be that He joins them to the people of complete religion and excellent work among His servants. The Almighty said:

> وَمَن يُطِعِ اللَّهَ وَالرَّسُولَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ مَعَ الَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ النَّبِيِّينَ وَالصِّدِّيقِينَ وَالشُّهَدَاءِ وَالصَّالِحِينَ وَحَسُنَ أُولَٰئِكَ رَفِيقاً ذَلِكَ الْفَضْلُ مِنَ اللَّهِ

And whoever obeys God and the Messenger, those are with the ones upon whom God has bestowed favor: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous. What excellent companions they are. That is the bounty from God.

This is by their exertion of effort in obedience, not by the merit of their deeds, for their deeds are not the equal of the deeds of the righteous. It is rather a mercy from God to them, though they themselves are weak and their works are slight."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has done this for them. Then is it to be feared that any man should be punished for something other than disobedience, even when his work is little?"

The scholar said: "God is more just than that."

The boy said: "Is it to be hoped, then, that any man should enter the Garden without works at all, like the mad and like little children?"

The scholar said: "That falls under the grace of God, His generosity, and the breadth of His mercy."

The boy said: "Then what is the measure of effort in obedience and of sincerity in work, such that God is bound to accept a man's excuse in all that lies beyond it?"

The scholar said: "The full reach of his knowledge and the utmost of his strength."

The boy said: "Then describe to me the duty of knowledge and the duty of action, and the goal of each, that I may test myself by them. If I am able to reach the goal in seeking my Lord's pleasure, I shall press on. And if not, I shall halt at the limit of my strength, and God is the first to excuse me for what I could not do. I trust in the justice of God and feel safe from any wrong on His part, yet I fear the evil within my own soul."

The scholar said: "Do not feel fear where you have come to feel safe, and do not feel safe where you ought still to fear. Strive for God in your work with your own person and with your wealth. For God does not accept what He hates, nor does He hate what pleases Him. He has hated disbelief for His creation and been pleased with thankfulness from His servants and with justice in His judgment, and He accepts nothing else. Truthfulness and faithfulness are the two gardens of His Paradise, and none is saved with Him but the people of truthfulness and faithfulness."

The boy said: "Then make plain to me the goal of the work in my own person, and what God has made binding upon me in it."

The scholar said: "Obedience to God ennobles its people, even when their roots are humble, and good work is the crown of those who do it. As for what is binding upon you in your own person, it is to fulfill every obligation and to shun every forbidden thing, for that is the very sustenance of the religion and the purity of the body. And the whole of what wins you God's pleasure and His acceptance is this: that you extend to another what you would love to have extended to yourself, and that you withhold from him what you would love to have withheld from you. In that is justice, and in justice is God's pleasure."

The boy said: "Yes, this is the binding truth, and whoever does not act by it is in nothing with God. But is there any room for ease in it when necessity compels?"

The scholar said: "God has indeed made that broad for His servants, out of His compassion and His mercy. He has said:

> فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ

Whoever is compelled, neither desiring it nor transgressing, there is no sin upon him."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has laid no hardship upon His servants in religion. I am resolved to hold fast to what He has commanded, and I seek His help in doing as He has ordered me. But what is the duty regarding my wealth, which you joined to my own person when you said, work with your person and your wealth?"

The scholar said: "As for your wealth, it is whatever God has granted you of every kind of gain. Take it only from what is lawful, and spend it only where it is due."

The boy said: "And what is the lawful source from which it is taken, and what is the due place in which it is spent?"

The scholar said: "Its lawful source is the good of earnings, that in which you have wronged no one and which you have not tainted with falsehood. As for its due, that is its purifying alms. Look upon all that has come to your hand, whether little or much, and set aside a fifth of it, from the best of it and the part most beloved to you, and pay it to the one whose claim upon you is binding. In that way your wealth is purified by the alms, just as your body is purified by work. Work is a shield, and the alms are a cleansing, and he who purifies himself has surely prospered."

The boy said: "I shall do so, and I turn to God in repentance for what has passed. Then what am I to do with the remainder of my wealth?"

The scholar said: "You shall spend upon yourself and your household in what is fitting. And if your surplus grows beyond that, share it with the needy among your brethren and give your kindness freely to everyone who turns to you in want, for the man who has no surplus of his own stands in need of the surplus of another. So be swift to every good and foremost in every bounty. This is the goal of the work, if you can be patient in it."

The boy said: "Yes, I shall be patient, and then patient again. And how should a man in great want fail to be patient toward one who has no need of his labor? Every needy man is one who bears patiently, and every man given a favor is one who owes thanks."

The scholar, gladdened by the boy's words, said: "Praise be to God, who has guided you to speak rightly. I ask Him to help you carry the work through to its completion, for that is the perfection of merit."

The boy said: "I had long been seeking from another's hand the credit of a favor I ought to have done for my own soul. And now that the credit has fallen to me from my own hand, shall I deny it to my soul? I can find no one who would cheat it more than I would. So here is the whole of my wealth before you, in five shares. One share is its purifying alms. One share is expiation for what I have squandered. One share is thanksgiving to God for what I have come to know. One share is for the poor among my brethren. And one share you are to accept by your grace, that it may be a provision for you. Take all of it to yourself, and God will restore its like."

The narrator said: The scholar's heart was moved for the boy when he saw how the jewel within him had come clear, how the trial had drawn out from him a praiseworthy result, how his merit stood plain and his work was purified.

The scholar said, while his tears ran down: "O my son, for the like of you let the scholars journey the long road. For the like of you let the earth be upheld and the heaven stand firm. For the like of you let the bonds of kinship shine forth. Upon the like of you let the rain come down from the openings of the clouds. So rejoice, my son, in what God has given you and in the breast He has opened to Islam. Take the glad tidings of a light from your Lord and of a near opening, and receive God's gift to you and the gains of His favors upon you, with abundant work and noble thanks. As for my own share of this wealth, I have no need of it. I have come out from wealth and fled from its temptation, and it would not be right, my son, that you should turn me back to what I have loathed. As for your brothers share, you shall hold it for them. When they ask you for something, you will pass it to them by your own hand, so that they may know your kindness and give thanks for your benevolence. As for the share of expiation, keep it by you until you come to know your fault. As for the share of thanksgiving, it is a trust held for God until you reach your maturity and rise to the highest of what you have come to know. As for the share of the alms, it too is a trust held for the one to whom the alms are due. So take all of it to yourself until you meet Him, for He is the Ruler over you and on your behalf."

The boy said: "And who is this generous one before you, whose judgment you do not overreach and whose command you do not transgress?"

The scholar said: "He is the one whose right God has made binding upon me and upon every believer, in whose hand are the keys of the gardens and the landmarks of the kingdom, whose palms are opened

with the light of Sinai. He is the cause behind the signs, and his knowledge calls to the loftiest of ends. Through him your light is completed, through him God perfects your affair, and at his hand the blessings flow down to you."

The boy said: "Such kindness as this, to whom does it belong, and who are you yourself?"

The scholar said: "I am his son and one of his servants in good works, seeking his pleasure and shrinking from his displeasure."

The boy said: "I fear I am far from his good pleasure and near to his displeasure, for I did not know him. Have I any excuse in that?"

The scholar said: "An excuse is granted only before one is brought within reach of that knowledge."

The boy said: "Shall I speak, or shall I hold back?"

The scholar said: "Say what seems best to you."

The boy said: "Then is it not your duty, at this very moment, to take me by the hand to the door of his chamber until I enter, and to the house of light until I draw from it? This is part of the full measure of the trust that is binding upon you to discharge."

The scholar said: "This is indeed your right, but only with his permission and by his decision."

Then the scholar took his leave and departed. The boy wished to press provisions upon him for the road, yet thought better of forcing them, and the scholar would not accept. He went on until he reached his own elder father and set before him the news of the boy, describing his qualities. The father recognized in them the marks of the people of goodness and the mines of merit, and he marveled.

He said: "Bring this boy to me quickly, for I hope he will be a door of mercy that God has opened for the people of his age."

So the scholar lingered only a little and then returned to the boy, and found him changed in body from the state in which he had left him.

The scholar said: "What is this I see in you, other than the state I left you in?"

The boy said: "And what have you noticed in me, may my father and mother be your ransom?"

The scholar said: "Weakness of body and a yellowness in your color."

The boy said: "That is from a desolation that came over me after you left."

The scholar said: "What desolation is this, and what is the cause of the weakness?"

The boy said: "Your absence made me desolate, and the dread of misfortune striking before I could meet you again drained my strength."

The scholar said: "God has known what is in your heart, and so He has made the trial light for you and cast into the heart of the one who has charge of you a mercy toward you. So take up your provisions for the journey, for I mean to carry you to your hope and bring you to the door of your goal. And there is no power except by God."

At this the boy was so overcome with joy and awe that his soul nearly parted from his body, until God, knowing his condition, took hold of it and returned it to him. When he revived, he said: "Praise be to God, who has never withdrawn His compassion from the believers."

He gave abundant thanks to his father and pressed his lips to his palms in service, and to his feet. Then he rose at once, took up the traveling kit, and the two set out until they reached the greater scholar. They sought permission and were admitted; they entered, gave their greetings, and sat down at his leave. When the gathering had settled around them, the boy said to his teacher: "Teach me how I should speak, and how I should ask for what I need."

The teacher said to him: "It is not lawful for me to instruct you in anything while you stand in this station, out of reverence for its master. You are before one who knows your need and knows what you intend."

So the boy held his peace. They stayed with the scholar for a time, until the boy's affair was completed, his guidance perfected, and he had reached the utmost of his hope and come to know what was his and what was owed of him. Then they took their leave and went out together, keeping themselves hidden. They had spoken no word in the gathering save the greeting of peace and words of welcome, for the boy had turned yellow from the intensity of his awe and could not bring himself to speak. The shaykh who was master of that house ordered that the two be honored, that their dignity be upheld, and that whatever they required be provided. He led them to his own dwelling, and when he had brought them in, he greeted them and showed his gladness in them.

He said to the scholar, for between them was brotherhood and an old companionship in learning: "My brother, is this the boy we used to hear of?"

He said: "Yes. This is the one whose remembrance grew sweet in hearts and rose upon the tongues of the believers."

The boy said: "Has the report of me truly reached the friends of God, until they recount my mention in their gatherings?"

The scholar said: "Yes, my brother. So it is with you."

The boy said: "Then favors have surrounded me from every side, and open hands have overtaken me from every road. I do not know whom to thank, nor whom to repay."

Then they all sat and talked through the night. When morning came, they sought leave to come before the Shaykh. When they were admitted and had finished their greetings, the Shaykh gave them leave to sit, and they sat. Then the Shaykh began to speak.

He said: "Praise be to God, who has parted the closed hearts by His light and opened by His justice what was locked away of every sought-after thing, and who by His making has made remembrance easy, a gentleness toward His servants and a mercy from Him to those who long for Him. He has not burdened creation with the full measure of what is due to Him, nor has He been content that they deny His blessings. Rather He charged them, out of His justice, with only what they are not too weak to bear, so that the affair was set balanced between two extremes, and it was named justice. He has spread out His hands with generosity in kindness and grace, disappointing no one who calls upon Him and denying no one who keeps silent. His blessings are overflowing and His favors abundant, neither reserved for those who give thanks, on account of the vastness of His reward, nor withheld from those who deny them, on account of the greatness of His punishment. He has kept His blessings too noble to be stinting, made His proofs too firm to be denied, encompassed all that exists so that He is beyond being contained, and set Himself, in the evidences, beyond denial. So He is present without limit, and known without being described. Glory be to Him: how great, yet how gentle to His creation; how mighty, yet how merciful to His servants; how powerful, yet how just in His ruling; how exalted, yet how near to His people; how near, yet how far beyond every imagining. So worship Him and give thanks to Him. To Him you shall return."

Then he said: "As for what follows: it belongs to the dignity of minds that they be put to work in seeking, and among the fruits of seeking is true finding, and among the marks of what is found is its sweetness. Every sweet freshwater spring is a drink whose surface is plain but whose depths are veiled; the one who seeks it never wearies of the thought it demands, and creation never reaches the end of its purpose. Its landmarks are a right, and the right of the truth is an obligation, and from that it is called a right, in confirmation of its being due. Whoever withholds from the truth what is owed to it has done wrong. Now among the rights of thought is that the hearts pay their zakat through good manners; and among the rights of good manners is the seeking of knowledge; and among the rights of knowledge is that it be acted upon; and among the rights of acting upon it is that it be kept pure through obedience to those charged with authority; and among the rights of obedience is that it be perfected in hardship and in ease alike, in good fortune and in trial the same."

And here he named the word of God:

> وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ

"And none is granted it except those who are patient, and none is granted it except one of great fortune."

Then he turned his face toward the boy and said: "O youth, you have been honored as an estranged friend arriving, and greeted as a visitor who came with purpose. So what is your name?"

He said: "Ubayd Allah, son of Abd Allah."

The Shaykh said: "That is your description, and your news has already come to us."

The boy said: "I am free, son of Abd Allah."

The Shaykh said: "And who freed you from a master, that you should become free?"

The boy said: "This world possessed me, and I have handed it back to the One who summoned it," and he gestured with his hand toward the scholar who had called him.

The Shaykh said: "Consider: if this one were himself owned and owned nothing, could his freeing of you be valid?"

The boy said: "It would not be valid."

The Shaykh said: "Then what is your name?"

At this the boy fell silent, at a loss for an answer.

The Shaykh said: "O youth, how can a thing be known when it has no name?"

The boy said: "Then I am newly born to you. Name me."

The Shaykh said: "That waits upon the completion of seven days."

The boy said: "And why is it deferred to the completion of seven days?"

The Shaykh said: "For the dignity of the newborn."

The boy said: "And if the newborn dies before the seven days are complete?"

The Shaykh said: "Nothing harms him. He is named after that."

The boy said: "And this name by which you would name me, is it truly mine?"

The Shaykh said: "Would you then become an object of worship, though you have only just been born?"

The boy said: "How is such a thing to be said of it?"

The Shaykh said: "The name belongs to you, and you belong to the name. So it does not appear except within your limit, and it runs its course with your term."

So the boy rose and hurried away in fair obedience, not returning the word out of good manners. He went out, and his father went out with him, until the two came back to their lodging, where the master of the house greeted them.

He said: "How stands our brother in the matter of his need?"

The scholar said to him: "A promise has been given. The one who awaits it is thirsting, and the appointed term is near."

So they remained until the seventh day. Then they sought leave of the Shaykh, and when he had granted it, he ordered the boy to wash and to put on his cleanest garments. As the boy took up this preparation, his heart sensed that his need would be granted that very day, and that this preparation he had been commanded pointed to a good that was coming after it. When he had done what was commanded and they had all gone out together, the boy went out saying, "A day of good fortune, and a patron of good fortune," repeating it again and again. Nothing drove him to this saying but the abundance of his joy and the intensity of his longing to know what would draw him nearer to the pleasure of God. When they came before the Shaykh, they found that he had taken up the implements of his work. He greeted them with peace, and they returned the greeting.

Then he ordered the boy to draw near, and he drew near; and he brought him close, and he came close. He began to speak of matters that no imagining had ever encompassed, that no pen had ever traced, that had never crossed the heart of any mortal, matters not to be uttered in the assemblies of preachers nor set out openly in the collected books, on account of their surpassing worth, and unveiled only to their own people. When he had discharged what was due and brought the boy out of the wastes of bewilderment, in which he had wandered lost, to the very end of guidance, his way became clear and his draught sweet, and he came to know his Lord. So he released his pilgrim's consecration by magnifying the sanctities of God, held fast to the sure rope, circled the ancient House, spent his rites in remembrance, and completed his pilgrimage by the greatest sign.

Then he and his father kept returning to the Shaykh, studying what he had been given, the boy patient in his perseverance, never wearying of the question for its nobility, never sated with the answer for its sweetness. They remained in that state for a season of the age, until the Shaykh knew that the root had been watered and the branch raised high.

Then the boy said to the Shaykh, in thanks to God and to him: "Praise be to God, who has made you worthy of this station, so that through you He revives a religion and puts an ignorance to death, and has made you a stronghold for the people and a place of safety. You are the gate of the people of heaven and the ascent of the people of earth. From the light of your might the lights appeared, and from the pouring of your palm the rivers ran. May you never cease to be so, until the One who is pleased with you makes you pleased."

The Shaykh said: "As for you, may God bless you in what He has given you, and aid you by His enabling grace to keep what has come to you, and set you upright in the doing of His will, and smooth your path with the light of His guidance, and open your breast with the keys of wisdom, and ennoble your affair with the way of the call, and complete His grace upon you with the truthful proofs of response, and bring you, in obedience to Him, to the best of your hope in His good pleasure. And now I bid you farewell, my son, and give you leave to depart. Heavy upon me is our parting, yet three things have driven us to it. The first is that we are setting out to a country other than this. The second is your long separation from your family through all this time, together with the hardship you took upon yourself in leaving without your father's permission, so that there is grave grief in him and great anger which nothing will clear away but your return, though I hope, if you are gentle with him, for good in your father's case. And the third is the duty of rendering the right of this grace that has reached you, for it is binding upon you to worship by it and to call others to it."

The boy said: "Your parting is a grief to me, and your loss a diminishment. But your counsel is more rightly guiding, your command more binding, your judgment more sound. So charge me with what you see fit, and dismiss me when you will, and be kind to me by sending my father along with me to my country, that I may lean upon him in my work and entrust all my affairs to him."

The Shaykh said: "That I shall do. And I enjoin upon you the fear of God who created you, the keeping of the trust for which you have struggled, and the safeguarding of your father, who raised you when you were small and toiled long in your affairs, with his own self and with his wealth. Do not clothe him in the garments of disdain, do not set him upon the mounts of ruin, do not leave his name unspoken, do not let yourself be turned away from his remembrance, and do not deal falsely with him in your city, for at the very beginning there is room enough for you."

The boy said: "May God reward you, on behalf of the Master of every grace, with the good that every grateful one could give, and may the Lord of bounty repay you for your shepherding, when hands fall short of repaying you. I will keep your charge. So do not cut off my share from the blessing of your prayer."

Then the two rose, clasped hands, and embraced, each bidding the other farewell, unable to hold back his tears and unable to speak except by a sign. Then they parted. The scholar and the boy set out and traveled on until they drew near the boy's own city, where his father was.

The scholar said to the boy: "My son, you have heard the charge of the Shaykh, and there is no right guidance to be found except in his words. This is your city, and we have reached its edge. Sit with me here, apart from the road, for I wish to remind you of something of my own affair and to counsel you in what you should act upon."

When they had sat down, the scholar said: "My son, you know your father's state and his enmity toward the people of this way, and now your going out with me and your absence from him without his leave or his safe-conduct have come one upon another to weigh on him. I mean to carry out my own father's counsel and keep hidden somewhere in this open country, while you go back to your father, so that his soul may be set at ease and his anger toward us may subside. And I hope, if you are gentle with him, that he will be nearer than any other to what you desire, for the absence has alarmed him and the loneliness has distressed him. Know, my son, that God has opened for you, by the lightest of your striving, what He has opened for none before you.

Then they stood and clasped hands and embraced, and each gave his friend farewell, unable to master himself against the weight of the parting, able to speak only by a gesture of the hand. Then they went their separate ways.

So the scholar and the boy set out together, and when they had come near to the boy's own city, the city where his father lived, the scholar said to him: "My son, I have grasped the counsel of the Shaykh, and you have not yet grasped it. There is no right guidance except in his words. This is your city, and we have reached its edge. Sit with me here, off the road, for I wish to remind you of certain of my concerns, and to charge you with what you should do."

When they had sat down, the scholar said: "My son, I know your father, and I know his enmity toward the people of this affair. To that has now been added your going out with me and your long absence from him, without his leave and without his trust. I mean to honor your father's mind by keeping some of these matters veiled, so that you may return to him, and his soul may be quieted, and he may remain at peace among us. And I hope, if you are gentle with him, that he will be nearer than any other to what you desire, for the absence has worn him down and his solitude has become a fright to him. So you must guard the trust your father laid upon you, and stand firm in your affair. Piety and God-wary discretion are the twin guardians of your religion and your work, and thanksgiving and patience are an increase to your light. May Allah unlock your provision and grant you the goodness of His guidance."

Then the scholar said: "I charge you with six qualities. Rely upon Allah once you are resolved. Speak with a considered ruling when you are called upon. Do not fall into anger when you are wronged, and do not grow weary when you are slandered. Whoever answers your call to the good, be patient with him. And single out your own self for the best of all that you summon others to, for Allah will help you and open the way before you."

The boy said: "May Allah reward you with three rewards: an imam whose plea you have upheld and whose trust you have counseled well, and a dead man whose life you kindled by your grace. My heart is filled and my breast is swelling with light and gladness at the greatness of what you have set out. I cannot tell which of your words is the finer: the outward, which you ordered in its due sequence, or the inward, which you unfolded in its meanings."

The scholar said: "Praise belongs to Allah, who made His way easy for you, and drew its far reaches near, and helped you carry the weight of it. Now I bid you farewell and am drawn away from you. Is there yet any need in which one might help you carry a part of your burden?"

The boy said: "My needs rest upon three: a need I have already had fulfilled, a need for which I hope, and a need I ask you to grant me out of your kindness."

The scholar said: "I have understood your three needs, and I answer you in what you have asked. As for the need already past, it is your life in knowledge after death. May Allah bless you with such a life, and bring you in it to the best of what you intended. As for the need for which you pray, it is to act upon what you know and to accept what He sets before you, and Allah will bring it about for you by His mercy. As for the need you ask of me, it is as though you wished for some increase upon what you have heard."

The boy said: "There is no greater increase than your knowing what lies within me. So grant me what you see fit."

The scholar said: "As for my knowing what is within you, none knows the unseen but Allah. Yet the believer sees by the light of Allah.

> وَلَكِنَّ الْمُؤْمِنَ يَنْظُرُ بِنُورِ اللَّهِ
>
> But the believer sees by the light of Allah.

And as for the increase, I will draw its matter together for you, and set you upon the foundations by which you are to work. Look upon all these things whose outward face I have named to you, and turn them into parables. Whatever among them is praiseworthy is a likeness of the truth and its people, its finer part for their finer part, and its tokens pointing toward them. And whatever among the parables is blameworthy, turn it upon the enemies of the truth and its people, their great ones for the great among them and their little ones for the small, its tokens pointing toward them. These are the foundations of the knowledge upon which the work turns. If you keep them, you will have no need of any besides them. And I charge you with the fear of the One who created you when you were nothing, and with keeping His covenant, and with rendering the right He has laid upon you and upon all His creation."

When he had finished his discourse, and had brought him to his place of safety, and had completed his nurture, he said: "My son, take care of your own soul, for I have concerns other than you, and this is the parting between me and you."

At the mention of parting the boy gave such a gasp that his spirit almost took flight and his soul almost left his body. Then he bent and kissed the scholar's feet and wiped their dust across his cheeks, and bade him farewell, and left. The boy returned to his house with his heart torn from him in grief, unable to hold back a single word of his sorrow at parting from his elder father, the father who had been the cause of his grace, until his own family and his neighbors grew afraid for him. He passed his day and his night restless with sorrow and alarm. Then his father came in upon him, angry.

## 8. Homecoming, the Father, and the Debate with Abu Malik

Let me tell you how it ended between the boy and his father, for I was there in spirit as the teller of it, and I want you to feel what I felt.

His father stood over him and said: "O my son, is this how sons repay their fathers? I have taught you nothing but the best a child can be taught, and never once did you deny your own self or renounce your beliefs until the time I grew old and this stranger came among us. You drew him in upon me, and you crossed over into his affair without ever consulting your parents. If what I held was true, then you have wronged me in my very self, for you concealed the truth from me. And if it is false, then you have disobeyed me by ruining your own soul, and whatever reaches you reaches me."

The boy answered, and I want you to hear the tenderness under his words: "O my father, I have shown you nothing but mercy and tenderness. You raised me out of your own mercy and fed me, choosing me over family and wealth. I have no wish to be unkind to you in a day like this. Grant me, of your bounty, one of two things."

"Name the two," said the Shaykh, "what are they?"

The boy said: "Either you leave me to my courtesies and my kindness toward you, for I am your son whom you raised from a child, and our affair rests with Allah; or you grant me leave to debate with you, and you give me your assurance of safety from your anger, and you bring your mind to bear on what I say."

The Shaykh said: "As for courtesy on the outside while the hearts differ within, that is a reproach standing between me and you. How long can outward agreement hold when what lies beneath it is at odds? I do not want that. But your debate and your candor, those you have. So say whatever seems best to you."

The boy said: "Consider my standing beside you these forty years. Can it be anything but one of two cases? Either you are a man of knowledge, and you withheld that knowledge from me, and then you must not blame me if I seek my own deliverance through another; or you are ignorant, and in that you are excused before me, yet you needed a man who was a stranger to me more than I did. Had I gone before you to him, it would only be because you are the elder and were born long before me, for I have heard his words just as you have heard them, and I have seen what you have seen."

The Shaykh's heart was rent by his son's words. He saw that there was no escape for him from either argument, and his eyes overflowed with tears. He said: "O my son, the argument that has fallen upon me from you falls upon you from me in the same measure, if you keep your affair hidden from me. So set it before me. If it is sound, I will accept it from you and count it an honor to myself; and if it is false, I will turn you back from it, honoring your own soul and out of compassion for it."

So they debated at intervals and disclosed things to one another at length, until their affair came to rest upon guidance and right direction: acting upon the religion of Allah, safeguarding it, holding fast to it, calling others to it, and helping one another in it. Through the two of them Allah revived many of His creation. The boy was sent out to bear the good news, with his father's blessing, and to invite others to come. He returned to them and became a cause of their guidance, and Allah poured His blessings upon the people of that country through them and at their hands. The command of Allah was fulfilled, and the slanderer was disappointed.

Now let me name them for you, so you can hold them close as I do. The boy's name was Salih, and his father's name was al-Bakhtari. The two of them held an honored rank among their people for the good they had done, and a high standing for the excellence of the manners they had gained. News of them spread through the land, along with news of the religion they now followed, until it reached a people who still clung to the old creed. Al-Bakhtari had long held influence among them, and they feared his leaving their religion would cut that influence off from them. So they went out and gathered before a scholar of theirs named Abu Malik, the mighty servant, whom they called, for his piety, the abundance of his forbearance, and his defense of their religion, "Ka'b al-Ahbar" (كعب الأحبار). Among them he was a man of standing through his knowledge, his grasp of speech, and his discernment of opinions.

When they came in to him they said: "O Abu Malik, do you not see what the boy has gone over to?"

"Which boy?" he asked.

"Salih ibn al-Bakhtari," they said.

"And what has he gone over to?"

They said: "We will tell you. A stranger has come to the country with a fine manner of speech and a creed he summons people to, and we do not know what it is. The boy inclined to him and went out with him without his father's leave, and was gone for a time. Then he returned speaking the stranger's words and calling to what the stranger called to. We have heard that his father has answered him and taken his side in it. What the two of them have done has grieved us, and we have held back from them for three reasons. First, the standing kindness al-Bakhtari showed us, on his father's account, together with the brotherhood in religion we shared; we dread estrangement after intimacy and hate division after brotherhood. Second, if these people are upon the truth, then we have fallen short in following them and helping them in it. Third, if they have strayed into error, then we have fallen short in turning them from it and in supporting the truth and its people. You are the head of our religion and one of our standards, and we have come to inform you and to consult you, and we will not go beyond your opinion."

Abu Malik said: "Restrain yourselves, for what you say has alarmed me. You know that al-Bakhtari and his son were among those we counted people of forbearance and piety, and that they have rights over us and old favors to their credit. You have said of them what you have said, and you are, in my sight, people who do not stray from the truth, so Allah has spared you from that. This is news that has come upon a people; an argument was made plain to them, and they followed it and were satisfied by it. We cannot pronounce them in error and so wrong them, nor pronounce them guided and so wrong ourselves by imitating them, nor say they have been deceived and so refute them without knowledge, for they are nobler in our eyes than that. Rather, we must look into this news with a fair eye, by the light of justice, and with the steadfastness of the mind. It has been said:

> مَن تثبّت في الأمور لم يزل سلطانه محروزاً، ومن عجّل في أمره لم يأمن زلة قدمه

He who is deliberate in his affairs keeps his authority secure, and he who is hasty cannot keep his foot from slipping. A tear in anything else admits of mending, and what is spoiled in it may be replaced, as with a flaw in food, whose harm is easy to remedy and whose cure is near at hand. But a tear in religion is gravely harmful and heavy in loss; its flaw cannot be mended nor exchanged for another, as with a flaw in the very medicine, which neither spares the one who takes it nor leaves him to himself. And we have been told concerning this:

> أفضل الذخائر كنوز الألباب واكتساب الآداب

The best of provisions is the treasure of sound minds and the acquiring of good manners, for the fruit of their outcomes is praiseworthy. Among their fruits is following the way of forbearance, and forbearance is the crown of the learned; by it noble resolve raises them to the highest glories, and their excellence is made plain in every place, along with the honor they win in the ease of what they seek. We have laid up for ourselves some measure of that forbearance we borrowed, and some of the manners we gained in the past of our days amid the narrowness of our circumstances and the hardship of the pursuit. We bore that patiently for its worth, and we seek help by it against the calamities of affairs and the accidents of the times. We must not squander it. This is the very hour of our need for it, and of putting it to use in what has come upon us in the matter of our brothers. There is no sense in leaving them and what they hold without laying it open and debating with them in it, with the forbearance of the capable and the earnest inquiry of seekers, free of any doubt in what we hold and free of accusing these people. They may have left the truth in forgetfulness, and they are nobler in our sight than that, or they may have gone out into error, and they are above such error too. Falsehood has no brilliance for which the plain truth should be abandoned. But consider the case of these people, for it cannot be free of one of three states. Either they are still upon the religion we know, and what has happened among them is only an addition of literature, sayings, poetry, and the abundant talk of people. Or they have been given an increase in the religion they already hold, and they have rejoiced in it, for knowledge is the lost property of every believer, and an increase in knowledge is better than a lack of it. Or they have been given a guidance other than what they held, through the prophethood of a prophet brought to them and calling them to it, and they have followed it upon clear proof. That is not to be denied of the command of Allah, nor will the creation ever reach the end of Allah's decree in them and His will for them, however hard they strive:

> وإن الله كل يوم هو في شأن

Indeed, every day He is engaged in some matter."

They said: "O Abu Malik, we came to you with hearts full of rage against these people, ready to brand them unbelievers, to refute their opinion, and to reckon their blood and their wealth lawful to us. But now we find our hearts humbled by your words, and by the way you have argued on their behalf and spoken in their defense."

Abu Malik said: "This argument of mine is not for them; it is for the truth, that I should not betray it in its absence. I have upheld the argument of the truth on its behalf when the one who should argue for it is away. Nor is this a defense of them; rather I have defended myself and you against uttering falsehood and against hurling accusations into the unseen concerning what we do not encompass in knowledge. For then we would be like the tyrants of the nations gone before, who were pleased with their own doctrines in their reckoning and rejoiced in the knowledge they possessed, until they supposed they had encompassed all that Allah had commanded, all He knew, and all He willed of His creation. So they said Allah would send no messengers after their messengers, and they never imagined that beyond the knowledge they had reached there could be any doctrine. They ruled that Allah should cut off His proof from His creation and His deputies in His earth. They made Allah's knowledge in His servants, and what He sent down upon His prophets and messengers, no more than the reach of their own minds, so that whatever passed beyond it was, to them, error. By that opinion they went astray and led many astray. By their own ruling they made lawful what Allah had forbidden, and by their own opinion they killed the prophets of Allah. I take refuge for you in Allah that you should follow the whims of a people who went astray before you. Men of intellect may have their passions bent by every meaning and be deceived down every road, save only the road of religion, for in seeking it there is safety, and its excellence is not hidden."

They said: "O Abu Malik, we will not disobey your command, and your counsel is not lost on us. We would not obey you at the beginning of our affair only to oppose you at its end. You have pointed us to an opinion and hinted at a course of seeking. If you see fit, take us to these people, that we may have their doctrine laid open to us and may look into it. If it is right guidance we will follow it, and if it is error we will avoid it."

Abu Malik said: "This is doubt on your part about what you hold, and one who doubts neither knows his own right nor knows the wrong of another."

They said: "Then we will stand firm upon our truth and give the lie to whatever else there is, be it what it may."

Abu Malik said: "That is not how meaning is sought, nor is guidance found through calling things lies."

They said: "How, then, is the seeking to be done, if we neither deny falsehood nor affirm the truth?"

Abu Malik said: "If you already know the truth and accept it, and know falsehood and reject it, then you are not among the seekers at all. You are among those learned in prophethood and guidance, the ones who judge by revelation over the people of this world."

They said: "Then we are content with the truth we hold and have no doubt in it, and we would be excused from the labor of seeking."

Abu Malik said: "Would you not be content that they have merit over you in a single respect, only to hand them merit over you in many? Would you refuse to stand in poverty in the dwellings of the seekers, only to stand in ignorance in the dwellings of those who sit back?"

They said: "How has this come to be among us, and in what have they been given merit over us?"

Abu Malik said: "Their merit over you is in knowing what you know, and more of what you do not know. This is a matter that has come upon a people. If you do not seek them out, they will seek you out to it; and if you will not listen to them, they will strive against you in it, and so they will gain merit over you both by being first to seek and by their striving. As for sitting back from seeking, that is nothing but ignorance, neglect, and a fault: on the one who knows, until he teaches; and on the one who is ignorant, until he learns."

They said: "O Abu Malik, you have hemmed us in by argument from every side, and your every word is right. Now open for us a door from your opinion by which we may act."

Abu Malik said: "To marvel at one's own opinion is blindness, and to neglect the seeking is error. There is no shame upon seekers in seeking, but the seeker must know the doors of seeking so that he seeks the truth by knowing how to seek. Whoever seeks the truth without knowing how is the quicker to deny it, for falsehood shows its first signs in display and in agreeing with received opinions, while the truth shows its first signs in trial and in going against the whims. None forgoes agreement with his own passion, and bears patiently through the onset of trial, save one of sound heart."

They said: "Here a door has opened for us, in which you point toward the knowledge of seeking. Show us what that knowledge is, and what the sound heart is."

Abu Malik said: "As for the knowledge of seeking, it is that you know yourselves to be in need. The one in need seeks what he needs with the humility of one who is poor before what he seeks. And as for the sound heart, it is the heart that does not persist in denying whatever comes to it, be it what it may, until the truth is the very thing that makes itself plain and shows its own worth, and falsehood is the very thing that lays bare its own shame. Then the heart tells falsehood apart by its intellect and accepts the truth by acceptance."

They said: "But we have been told that whoever is in this position, who neither verifies the truth nor refutes falsehood, is ignorant in what he does. If he does not know the truth he cannot follow it, and he cannot know falsehood so as to shun it."

Abu Malik answered them: "What you have related, you have believed. But has not this man passed beyond the rank of the liars and become, as it were, two ignorant men gathered in one?"

They said: "Yes. With us he is ignorant, not a liar."

"Then know," Abu Malik said, "that the ignorant man is nearer to hope, for he seeks the good and pursues it. He seeks knowledge for himself, and if he does not, the learned seek it out for him, because ignorance is the root condition of every creature, and there is no shame in it for one who seeks knowledge on account of his ignorance. Lying, however, is the worst of all wares. The liar is never guided by his lying, and he is never believed in what stands recorded against him."

They said: "You have made this plain to us. We have come to know the defect of lying, and so we will never deny a thing until we know it for what it is. You have also opened for us the door of the request through reverence toward what is sought. But tell us: is this reverence and humility toward the sought thing to be shown after it has shown its right, or before the statement is given?"

"Before the statement," he said, "so that by your reverence you may arrive at the statement."

They said: "And if we revere what is sought until its content becomes clear, and then its statement proves misleading, what becomes of our reverence? Has it not been thrown away?"

"No," Abu Malik said. "It was reverence to God and humility before His command, and its reward is yours even if the one before whom you humbled yourself did not deserve it. For the seeker has discharged the duty of the request, though he has not attained the duty of the truth. He is like a man who stands before a king to ask for some gift, and in the asking has nothing open to him but reverence, flattery, and the pressing of his complaint. If the king gives him something of what he asked, the king has discharged the duty of his own grace and earns the reward of what he gave. If he gives nothing, the petitioner has still discharged the duty of asking gently, and it is niggardliness that has bound the other to withhold. So it is with the seeker of knowledge. He must begin with humility, persistence, and patience under trial until he has fulfilled the duty of the request, and after that time will bring him what it brings."

They said: "This is the clear and manifest truth. Will you permit us, then, to go out to the people as you have counseled us? We will not count what you have commanded a burden."

Abu Malik said: "I would never have counseled you a thing and then held myself back from it. Were it not for the fear of falling short by turning you from the path of Allah, I would have sent you onward and gone ahead of you to the good, keeping that dignity for myself and preferring my own soul above every other. But we will go together. I give you my own company as an honor to you, and in thanks for your having singled me out for this good."

They said: "It is as though you had been expecting this matter before today."

He said: "This precise matter, no. But I have never ceased to expect deliverance from Allah on every side, hoping for good by every road, inclining my ear toward every speaker and asking an increase from his words, whether he speaks truly or falsely."

They said: "An increase from the words of the truthful we understand. But what do you hope to gain from the words of the false?"

"An increase of truth upon truth is light," Abu Malik said, "and to recognize falsehood for what it is increases certainty, and the greater the certainty, the greater the light of the heart. None are saved with Allah but the people of certainty. I am now rising to go to the people. If you are resolved to rise with me, then let each of you look to himself. Whoever knows a sin in himself, let him repent to Allah; whoever owes a debt, let him repay it to its owner. Wash yourselves clean of every impurity, put on for the request the purest garments of resolve, and bring to what is sought the intention of the needy, for this is what I am doing. Our going out is a going out to Allah. If He knows truthfulness in us, He will grant us success to His truth; and if He knows in us any mockery of the faith, He will turn us from His signs by the slightest stumble, keeping His friend unsullied by the mockers."

They said: "Yes. We will do what you have commanded, within three days."

"No," he said.

"Two days, then?"

"No, only this very day," he said. "If you will do it, do it; otherwise I go on alone."

They said:

> لا قوة إلا بالله

> There is no power except in Allah.

"We repent to Allah from every sin, we set aside every debt, and we do what you have commanded."

So they did so that same day, and they went out all together until they came to the gate of al-Bakhtari. When they had greeted him and sat down, Abu Malik said to al-Bakhtari: "O Abu Salih, how is your good son, and where is he?"

Al-Bakhtari said: "Today my father is Salih, and I am the son of Salih; and Salih is with his Lord."

Abu Malik supposed from these words that Salih had died, and he said:

> إنا لله وإنا إليه راجعون

> To Allah we belong, and to Him we return.

"Has Salih died?"

Al-Bakhtari said: "Salih has not died, and he will never die. This is no turn of phrase. He abides as long as time abides."

Abu Malik said: "Here are three matters whose meanings have been turned about. The child has become the father; the Lord is found upon the earth, and this Lord has a servant to whom one comes through him; and the one created for perishing has become everlasting. A whiff of fragrance for the perplexed seeker, and behind this lie wonders still greater."

Al-Bakhtari said: "O Abu Malik, name your need, and be deliberate in your asking, for you stand at the very threshold of the ordeal."

"You have spoken truly," Abu Malik said. "The first of the truth is an ordeal. My need is a meeting with the righteous servant."

"There is no way to that," said al-Bakhtari. "Yet perhaps you mean my son, Salih?"

"Yes," he said.

"That is by his own leave," said al-Bakhtari.

So the old man rose in haste until he came in upon his son Salih, and told him of Abu Malik and his companions, and of what he knew of the excellence of their minds. And Salih rejoiced at it, and said: "O Allah, open the hearing of the hearts of Your servants, and guide them by Your mercy to the signs of what they seek, and expand our breasts with the light of Your hidden light."

Then he passed his hand over his face, cleared the assembly for them, and bade his father bring them forward.

When they had entered upon him, greeted him, and taken their seats, with Abu Malik and those below him being seated each by introduction, the first thing Salih said was: "O Abu Malik, you have honored us with your visit and outrun us to the good by your generosity, though you had the greater right that we should visit you, to guard your affection and to discharge the duty long owed to you."

Abu Malik said: "And when were you ever wanting in virtue, O Abu al-Khair? You were forbearing toward us when we were small and generous to us when we were grown. You became for us a summoner and a warner to the good. Your root is sound, your branch is high, your work is pure. Good fortune to you."

Salih said to him: "So you come to me accusing, O Ka'b al-Ahbar, or merely imitating? Where is that perfect mind and that noble forbearance I have always known in you?"

Abu Malik said: "Your rank is too high for accusation, and religion stands too high for imitation."

Salih said: "Then how do you name me a warner, when the warner is a prophet, and the prophet is a proof from Allah between Him and His creation, for them and against them? How can he be a proof for you when he is not a proof against you?"

Abu Malik said: "You speak truly, and that is the obligation. We do not act here upon the ease of words. We began with you by the fairest way. I saw that truthfulness has the greater claim upon one who has set himself as a veil for religion and a champion for the truth, and has opened its doors to seekers. We have come to know the excellence of asking, and so we sought you out and hoped in you as the one sought. Turn now to our need."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, is not the one whose excellence you have come to know also one to whom a right becomes due from you?"

"Yes," he said, "it becomes due."

"And when you came to know the excellence of asking, did you also come to know its right?"

"And what is its right?" he asked.

Salih said to him: "To know its faces, and then to seek in each the truth that is due from you."

"And what are its faces," said Abu Malik, "and how many are they?"

Salih said: "Seeking is of three faces. There is a seeker who knows, a seeker who does not know, and a seeker who knows that he does not know. Which of the three are you?"

Abu Malik said: "I have more need to know all three than to claim any. How can I lay claim to a station I do not know?"

"Then how did you claim to be a seeker at all," said Salih, "when you do not know the faces of seeking?"

"I did not know them," said Abu Malik. "Explain them to me."

Salih said: "Wealth is the treasure of the people of this world, and knowledge is the treasure of the people of the Hereafter. The seeking of wealth is of three kinds: a seeker who asks for what is his own, a seeker who asks for what is owed him at an appointed term, and a seeker who asks for the gift of alms. So too is the seeking of the faith and the treasures of the Hereafter of three faces. The first is the seeker who is himself a knower, the divine scholar, who seeks out the dead in their ignorance to revive them by his knowledge. The second is the seeker who knows, the learned man who has attained some of the degrees of knowledge and now seeks its farthest end and limit. The third is the seeker who knows himself known by his own ignorance, the ignorant man who knows nothing beyond knowing that he is in need, and so he seeks out the scholars in order to learn. These are the three faces, and the descriptions of the seekers."

Abu Malik said: "Yes, these are the faces of seeking, in religion and in the world. As for me, I am the seeker who knows nothing except that he is in need. So teach me."

"Did you come to know," said Salih, "or did you come to know by sight?"

"And what is the difference between the two?" asked Abu Malik.

Salih said: "Knowledge by report is tidings; knowledge by direct sight is witnessing."

"This too," said Abu Malik, "is something one needs to know."

Salih said: "As for the way of report, it is the case of an ignorant man who does not know that he is ignorant until he meets a scholar, and the scholar informs him of his ignorance; then he knows himself ignorant and in need of knowledge by the scholar's word, and his knowledge of his own ignorance has come to him as tidings. As for knowledge of need and ignorance by direct sight, it is the man who already knows within his own mind that he is asking about a thing but cannot see the face of its answer. His breast is straitened by his ignorance, and he hopes for no relief except by the knowledge of what he does not know, so that his very ignorance has become, through his mind, a kind of knowing. Ignorance is want, and want is narrowness, and narrowness is a severe need that compels the creature to seek breadth. And the breadth is the breadth of knowledge, for knowledge is wide and spacious. By this I know that you are in need."

Abu Malik said: "I find within myself everything you have described. I have come to know that I am in dire need and severe want. So relieve my want by your bounty."

Salih said: "I see you hastening the request before you have made it firm."

"Want hastened me by its narrowness, and so I hastened," said Abu Malik, "and I came to know the excellence of breadth, and so I sought."

Salih said to him: "If want has hastened you and you have come to know the excellence of breadth, then how do you come by the knowledge of the one you seek?"

"Your excellence became sound to me, and so I made for you," said Abu Malik. "I hinted at my meaning, and so I sought you out."

Salih said to him: "And from where did it become sound to you that I, and not you, am known for this breadth, so that you sought me? From where did you learn that I am the one sought, so that you set out for me? No scholar of the prophethood pointed you to me, that you should know; you have witnessed from me no confirming proofs, that you should recognize. Do you not see that you are raising your search to the ease of imitation, and so wronging both wisdom and yourself? You are like the man who measures from the bottom of the measuring vessel: if he does not fill it, he wrongs the one owed; and if he presses it down full, he wrongs himself. So it is with one who takes knowledge by mere tradition. If he acts upon it, he is given no thanks; and if he leaves it, he is called a denier. And you are, to my mind, wiser than that."

Abu Malik said: "I know well that it is as you say. Yet I hoped that the truth would be the thing that establishes itself, and that I might spare you the labor of the proof. For disputation breeds contention, and contention breeds grudges, since each of two adversaries seeks his own victory over the other. And when will the truth ever take hold of one who plunges into the defense of falsehood?"

Salih said to him: "Well have you spoken. The truth does indeed establish itself by its own worth and honor, and when quarrel grows great it occupies the hearts and turns them from what is worthier. Yet there is no abandoning what the scholars have laid down as their way. They have said: every seed for which the ground is not first made sweet will not thrive, and every knowledge for which the hearts are not first purified by proof will not grow."

Abu Malik said: "Then bring forward whatever proofs you wish. The words of wisdom are like the jewel. Turn it over, and its light shines forth; set it aside, and its worth is not hidden."

Salih said to him: "You speak truly, but only so long as there is no counterfeit of coated glass set beside the jewel; then indeed the jewel is not concealed. But when claims grow many, and the tyrants of the world contend over the faith until they have dressed it in what is not its own and disguised it before the seekers with a false likeness, then it becomes the seeker's duty to put the jewel to the test, until its worth is made plain and the counterfeit and its people are exposed."

Abu Malik said: "You have done justice and discharged the due of the truth. As for the disguising practiced by these people, we have come to know it and its defects, and so we rejected it and held fast to this understanding by which we came to know you, and we called it the sound jewel of speech. We took our stand upon it, and you agreed with us in it, and together we clung to its rope. If you have found a meaning more precious in essence than this, then, upon my life, jewels do surpass one another, each above the next. So show me where the worth of your jewel lies, by a test that makes its worth known. As for our jewel, we have known it and made it known. Say of it, then, whatever appears to you."

Salih answered him: "As for your essence, we were generous with it, holding it a sound jewel, until we brought it before the jewelers and the people of metals, and they tested it in the forge. They did not accept it from us as a jewel. They said it was glass in disguise, and they showed us its flaws, and so it fell outside the bounds of jewelry. For a true jewel rises by its own preciousness, and by the recognition of the people of insight, who prize it and compete for it, until its price climbs to every sum. Those jewelers who know it, and any who come to know it afterward, do not turn it away. None accept a false stone but the people of delusion, who neither buy nor sell. So it is with knowledge: it is not knowledge until it is set before those who know the science of heaven. If they accept it, it is true knowledge; and if they do not, it is idle noise. And idle noise is not permitted in wisdom, nor accepted by the wise."

Abu Malik said: "And who are these who know the science of the heavens, after the prophets and the messengers? We know of no such man in our age, nor have the people held any covenant with such men since revelation was cut off with the books. The nations are bound now only by the reports handed down. What do you say to that?"

Salih said: "Any religion held only by way of narration is not called religion. For religion is one, upright, kept by God in its truth, admitting no difference within it. But you know well the disagreement among the narrations, how one gives the lie to another, and the religion of God is too dear for that."

Abu Malik said: "When they differ in what is narrated, they return to the books. Whatever agrees with the books they accept, and whatever contradicts them they reject as false."

Salih said to him: "But what of that which the books give only in the general, unspecified? Who then makes it specific for them? And what they give in detail, who joins it back into the whole for them? Who among them is trusted over what God has made lawful and forbidden? I heard you on the day of the dispute, when a distressed man came to you and said, O Abu Malik, I swore an oath of divorce and then broke it. You said to him, Some hold that she is severed from you and not lawful to you until she marries another husband, and some hold that she is still your wife and is not forbidden to you by a single pronouncement of divorce. So the man said to you, Then which of the two shall I act upon? And you told him, Act upon whichever of the two you wish. He rose from your presence not knowing whether the home he returned to was lawful or forbidden. Is this how judgment is rendered in the religion of God? Is this how God's lawful and forbidden are settled?"

Abu Malik said: "By my life, the Sharia of my age has been ruined and suspended, and we hold on to nothing but the name of religion. What, then, is the state of religion with you?"

Salih said to him: "And what is religion, O Abu Malik?"

He said: "Command and prohibition, the lawful and the forbidden, the practices and the obligations."

Salih said to him: "And is the Sharia, in your eyes, anything other than this?"

Abu Malik said: "I know of nothing in it beyond what I have described to you."

Salih said to him: "Then you have gathered religion and Sharia into a single ground?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said to him: "Then religion has been suspended by the suspension of the Sharia, and ruined by its ruin. What remains with you?"

Abu Malik fell silent from any answer for a time, his head bowed, turning over in his mind the matter of his religion. Then he raised his head and said: "I do not see that anything remains with me. So what is religion in your eyes?"

Salih said to him: "Religion, with me, is that whose veil is made firm against the corrupters and whose doors are opened to the seekers, so that its bearer is honored by coming first, and its seeker attains the good. By its worth deeds are ennobled, and by its justice the laws bloom. It is a bond stretched from heaven to earth, joined and never severed, the handhold of those who grasp firmly and the rope of those who hold fast, the ark of tranquility and the ship of deliverance and the light of life. Its content is generous, its sciences are guarded, its jewels are marked, and their worth is hidden from no one who looks upon them."

Abu Malik said: "If God has a religion by which men will one day be judged, with which He is pleased, for which He rewards and by which He punishes, then it must be after this description. And now that the excellence of the description has grown clear, how then is the One described?"

Salih said to him: "The good comes only from what is good, O Abu Malik. Set your light in the lamp-niche of your reason, and grasp what is intended for you. The claims of destiny have pulled you in every direction, and the robes of delusion have dazzled you, and you have taken upon yourself in your own affair what none but you can carry, along with what you have shouldered of the affairs of these people who follow you in good faith. They have listened to your word, accepted your guidance, and trusted your judgment. So it is your duty to shield yourself and them from a fire you have no escape from entering. Seek, then, the way to be saved from it. You are the most favored of them in the grace of God and the most bound of them to thank Him, for I have known you to recognize the good and to hope for its reward. As for what you asked, that I confirm what I have called you to, it is my duty to establish it for you by whichever of the ways you wish."

Abu Malik said: "All that you have said before is proven and true. As for the narrations of the common people, religion is not set right by them, nor are they fit for it. Nothing remains for us but to know what you call us to."

Salih said to him: "I call you to the justice of God and His oneness."

Abu Malik said: "How excellent is that to which you call. We ourselves profess this saying, and we know its excellence above all other doctrines."

Salih said to him: "That is not where your mind has gone, O Abu Malik. That doctrine you intend is a description of justice without knowledge of the One described, and a swerving in the names of God without knowledge of His oneness. What I call you to is knowledge of the justice of God and the landmarks of His unity. If you wish, I will halt you before the corruption of that saying, wherever you please."

Abu Malik said: "If the saying of the people of justice is corrupt, and their profession of oneness a swerving into denial, then there is no firm truth left on the earth, unless it be some meaning apart from all the doctrines. So make plain to us the corruption of justice and of oneness, and from where it comes."

Salih said to him: "Justice and oneness are among the noblest of all doctrines. Oneness is too great to be corrupted, for it has no opposite; and justice cannot be corrupted, for it is the ruling that stands between God and His creation. But all these doctrines are spoiled by the corruption men have worked in the saying of justice and the rest of it. It is a mere claim by which no statement is proved, and they do not encompass in knowledge what they have said, until they surrender it back to the command of God. So defend whichever of the two you wish, and take it for your own, until I strip it from you by the argument of truth."

Abu Malik said: "I will not defend a saying I do not hold, nor one whose author was questioned rather than I. I do not set myself up as any man's agent, nor am I the author of these doctrines, nor a guardian over the man who makes such claims. I will defend before you nothing but my own saying. I say that God is One, that nothing is like Him, that He is just in His ruling, that He does not burden His servants with what they have no power to carry. He commanded them and forbade them, not by binding decree nor by compulsion, but His command is a giving of choice and His prohibition a warning. So the obedient did not obey under His trickery, and the disobedient did not disobey overpowered by Him. This is my meaning, and this is what I hold."

Salih said to him: "Did I not tell you that this doctrine is among the noblest? Yet these are names and attributes of no use unless a meaning lies behind them. It is as when you say, fire is hot. You have spoken truly, but a truth that does not profit you, because your saying fire is hot is a name and an attribute whose mention brings you nothing, if by it no food is cooked and no darkness is lit, and men are left in the dark without the meaning of fire being present. Were names and attributes to stand in things in place of their meanings, then the mere mention of fire would burn your tongue, and the mention of food would fill you."

Abu Malik said: "As for the saying about these things, you have spoken truly, for their names and attributes are of no use unless their meaning is present. So show me that my saying about justice and oneness is names and attributes that call for the knowledge of a named and described One."

Salih said to him: "Your saying that God is One: now One is four letters, and we see two of them joined and two apart. Which of them is one, or are all of them together one? And your saying Allah is four letters: is the alif alone the God, or are all the letters together Allah, or are they signs pointing to God? Or do you say otherwise?"

Abu Malik was struck silent for a time. Then he said: "I find no way out of this except that they are signs pointing to God."

Salih said to him: "Which of them points to God? Or by what do they point to God, by their being apart or by their being joined? Or by some standing alone and some joined together, as in your word Allah, where the alif stands alone and the three letters are linked. Which of them points to God? This one by standing apart, or these by their joining?"

Abu Malik said: "I do not know what to say to you in this. Take us on to some other door."

Salih said to him: "Then the door to the knowledge of oneness has been shut before you, O Abu Malik, by whoever spoke to you in this fashion."

He said: "Yes."

Salih said to him: "Then let me ask you about your knowledge of God. From where is it, and from whom did you take it? Is it from an attribute that pointed you to One described?"

He said: "No."

Salih said: "Then from a name pointing to One named?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said: "And what is the name that pointed you?"

He said: "Allah."

Salih said: "Is Allah a name, or the named?"

He said: "The named."

Salih said: "Then what is the name?"

He said: "Allah."

Salih said: "So Allah is a name for Allah."

He said: "Yes."

Salih said: "Then what is the difference between the name and the named? And which of the two do you worship, or is it both?"

Abu Malik was bewildered for a time, turning over his answer. Then he said: "I find no way in this except that I have seen all the crafts, and every one of them has a maker, and so I judged that this vast creation has a Creator who resembles nothing of His creation, just as the craftsmen do not resemble the things they have made."

Salih said to him: "O Abu Malik, you have carried us away from the first meaning, and the strait place in the meaning of oneness has driven you to seek Him by analogy and to set up likenesses for Him. But God is too dear for that, for everything made is made from some thing, while God did not create things from any thing that was before them. As for these craftsmen, you know them only because you have watched some of them with your own eyes, how they work. So you passed judgment upon the making of what you did not witness, judging a maker you never saw by what you observed of an action like the actions of the craftsmen you have seen and known, drawing it out by comparison. But when God created, He did not make you a witness to the creation of what He made, nor did He set up any likeness that works as He works. You have testified by the likeness present before you to a likeness that is absent. I have never known you, O Abu Malik, to be content with analogy in your religion, so how are you content to seek the knowledge of your Lord by measurement?

"And your saying, nothing is like Him, proves, by your own words, that He has a likeness, and that nothing is like that likeness. Had you meant true oneness, you would have said, there is nothing as He is, and you would not have said,

> لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ

there is nothing like His likeness. Glory be to the One whom no reaching of thought can grasp and whom no soaring of the pointing hand can attain in comprehension. He is exalted, far above the description of those who are charged with duty. O Abu Malik, had you turned this quest toward one of His servants, or a king from among His angels, it would have left you helpless to comprehend what is said about how he was made. How then with his Creator? The lights of the people of the earth fall short of grasping the light of the sun, and the sun is but one of the lights of this world. So how shall the one who falls short of grasping a servant of His ever grasp the Creator? Say now what appears to you."

Abu Malik bowed his head to the ground in his bewilderment, and could find no path to speech.

Salih said to him: "O Abu Malik, raise your head and say what is present with you to say."

Abu Malik said: "Speech is gone, and there is no speech. Minds are bewildered before the answer, imaginations are struck dumb before thought, and the instrument is too weak to grasp anything beyond seeking and asking."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, the One who is of such greatness, do the eyes perceive Him?"

He said: "No. He is higher than that."

Salih said: "Is speech heard from Him?"

He said: "There is no speech but from a speaker, and the speaker is described by speech, and God is too great for the attributes."

Salih said to him: "This Vast One of greatness, this High One of power, could He be unjust in His creation, so that He charges them with what they cannot bear, and commands them with what they cannot grasp, and then punishes them if they do not?"

Abu Malik said: "Whoever charges the obedient with what he cannot bear has charged him with disobedience, and to punish him is wrongdoing. And God is too dear for that."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, the One who is thus far above injustice and above all wrongdoing, would He be content that ignorance should be the companion of His servants?"

Abu Malik said: "They learned this from God."

Salih said: "If He is higher than any sight can reach, wider than any perception can grasp, and greater than the limits of speech, then how does the one whom He forbids and commands come to know His command, so as to obey rather than disobey, and to thank rather than deny, unless you grant that there are causes for this knowledge set between Him and His creation? Must there not be those who deliver His justice and bear witness to His witnessing, for them and against them, chosen by His knowledge and selected out of all His creation, made messengers to them and proofs against them, so that they stand as the just ones between Him and His servants, conveying His command and His prohibition, so that obedience to them is obedience to God and the winning of His pleasure, and disobedience to them is His wrath and His punishment? Otherwise, how could the command reach God's creation at all?"

Abu Malik said: "This, to me, belongs to the very foundations of justice. Once creation stands in need of God's justice to teach them what He wills from them, by the hand of the righteous one He has appointed for it, then each will thank his benefactor, be rewarded by Him as He wills, be held to account by Him, and be punished by Him if He wills."

Salih said to him: "Is it permissible to rule with justice in any way other than this?"

He said: "It is not permissible for anyone but God, and God is the wisest of judges."

Salih said: "Would it then be permissible for Him to delegate His command to some of His people, setting them up justly against their own wrongdoing, and then to assign others to the very same charge, yet without guiding these later ones by a just proof as He guided the first?"

Abu Malik said: "The judgment can only be one, because justice is one. Were the ruling split into two meanings, one of the two would inevitably fall into wrongdoing."

Salih said to him: "Has it not then been established for you that the judgment of God is one, with no difference within it, and that His way is one, unchanged in His messengers? For were it not for the succession of the messengers and the standing of command and prohibition, the truth would have no growth and creation no meaning."

He said: "Yes, this is the truth."

Salih said: "And is not that succession sent from God to His creation so that He may impose obedience upon them and make the messengers an authority between Himself and His creatures, to bear witness for those who obey them, so that their obedience is accepted, and to bear witness against those who disobey them, so that they are turned away?"

Abu Malik said: "The judgment could not have been otherwise. Were it so, He would have falsified His messengers and broken His promise, and God is too generous for that. The affair of the messengers has made plain that they are of His justice, and that people are made to worship Him by obeying them. But tell me of the ranks of these just ones before God. Are they equal, or have some of them precedence over others?"

Salih said to him: "How could they stand level in rank when they are bound in obedience one to another? Were they equal in station, none of them would owe obedience to another. Rather, by His justice He has given some precedence over others, so that obedience may pass from the lesser to the greater, and from the greater to the one above him, until obedience and worship reach through them the very limit that God intends."

Abu Malik said: "Then the one who obeys the superior among them, and the superior one himself, are they equal in reward?"

He said: "Yes. This is the justice of God, which secures to every obedient one his reward. One is not to ask whether the measure of a superior or of a lesser applies."

Abu Malik said: "And is each of them the like of his fellow?"

He said: "No. For they come from the Creator bearing knowledge of the unseen, the Books He has sent down upon them, and no one can bring the like of them except from God."

He said: "Then can anyone imitate the lesser among them, when no book is sent down to him carrying revealed knowledge of the unseen?"

He said: "No one can lay claim to that station."

He said: "And how is that, when the lesser one does not display the hidden things among creation as the superior one displayed them of himself?"

Salih said to him: "If the superior one set himself apart from others by knowledge of the unseen in the revelation sent down, the lesser one likewise sets himself apart from others by knowledge of the unseen in the interpretation of that revelation. Both the sending down and the interpreting are from God, and what is with God is not attained except by revelation."

Abu Malik said: "And the lesser one too is inspired?"

He said: "Yes, and for that reason obedience to him became obedience to God."

Abu Malik said: "But if you set him level with the superior one in obedience and in revelation, why did you name him the lesser?"

Salih said: "By his obedience to the superior one and his need of him."

Abu Malik said: "And what need has he of a creature, when the revelation reaches him from the Creator?"

Salih said to him: "Even though revelation comes to him, it comes to him with the interpretation of the reports and the Books, and of what remains of what the superior ones left behind. So it was revealed to Lot (ع) by the hand of Abraham (ع), and Abraham's precedence was made plain; and so it was revealed to Ishmael and Isaac (عليهم السلام), while the goodness, the blessings, and the merit were set upon Abraham. Thus revelation from God is joined to His just ones in His earth, each according to his rank. He does not, in His justice, will to cut off His command and His prohibition and His worship from His servants after He has favoured them with it, so that worship should be severed and ignorance made lawful."

Abu Malik said: "The bearer of the revelation has shown his truthfulness by the knowledge of the unseen within his Book. By what does the bearer of the interpretation prove that knowledge of the unseen is with him, so that the revelation bears witness for him and makes obedience to him alone binding, as it did for the bearer of the revelation?"

Salih said to him: "He brings, in his interpretation, knowledge from heaven and a declaration from the highest assembly, and the Book itself bears witness to it."

Abu Malik said: "Then I bear witness to what the Books have borne witness to: that every interpretation which the revelation attests is itself revelation from God. Otherwise all the Books and the messengers would have to be rejected, since their meaning is one."

Then he said to him: "If some of them give the lie to others, then all must be reckoned liars; and if some confirm others, then all must be confirmed."

Abu Malik said to him: "Such is justice, and by this the truthfulness of the one who confirms it, and his merit, are known. Obedience is made binding to the messengers of firm resolve who are His just ones in His creation, whom He charged with delivering His message. Yet if obedience is binding by prophethood, by the sending down, and by the interpretation, then what of those who seat themselves in the councils of the prophets, resembling them though they are not of them? They have all agreed that after their own prophet there is no prophet and no warner in their time, that they themselves are the ones who uphold the command of God and His religion, and that what they say leads to God's reward or His punishment. Yet you say otherwise. How then did the scholars of this community fail to look into this age?"

Salih said to him: "It is not that they left it wholly unexamined, but they did not look into it with a scrutiny that could be sound for them, and no one spoke of it to them so that they might incline to it with their minds. For it had been made to sink into their hearts that they are the more entitled to the command of God and His religion, and that there is no prophet after their prophet, just as those before them among the seducers of the vanished nations said. So every nation claimed that there is no prophet after its own prophet."

Abu Malik said: "And what was the beginning of that, and what did they intend by it?"

Salih said to him: "The originators of it were three in every age: a rebellious devil, a stubborn tyrant, and a hypocritical jurist. By this saying they sought to sever the traces of the prophets from the marks of prophethood and to make it a possession passed among themselves. They could not accomplish it until they had made this saying sink into the hearts of the nations, addressed to every nation: that God sent none better than your prophet, that there is no prophet after him and no warner. So they whispered to them from where they did not perceive it, and drew them away from where they did not know it. Every nation clung to its own prophet by holding all who came after him to be liars, supposing that this drew them nearer to their prophet, and every nation held that every prophet after its own was a liar. Thus, by this saying, the remembrance of prophethood and the marks of the prophets were cut off from all the nations together. They did not seek what they had lost, and would not have believed had they sought it. So the seducing devils gained power over those they seduced and led them astray; the tyrants gained the means to kill the prophets and killed them; and the doubt of the evil jurists worked its way in, so that they made the falsehood fair and were destroyed and destroyed others. All of this so that the hearts of creation would not incline toward the prophets when they appeared, nor seek them out when they were lost, until despair of the prophets carried them so far that when the prophets called them openly to the truth they were called liars, and the prophets were killed among them and they felt no anger.

"This is the nation of the Magi, clinging to its rebellion, worshipping its idols, devoted to its fires, saying in their claim that God will send no messenger after their messenger. And this is the nation of the Torah, clinging to its Scripture, devoted to its calf, standing by the bounds of its prophets, humble before its Judge, saying in their claim that God will raise no messenger after Moses (ع). And this is the nation of the Gospel, speaking by its likening, claiming that its prophet is its God, drawing near by its crosses, magnifying its rabbis, saying that God will send no messenger after Jesus (ع). And this is your community, which has walked the paths of those before it, so that whoever led others astray led it astray too. It inherited the Book from other than its rightful people, walked with the religion outside its true way, followed its leaders into error, obeyed its masters in misguidance, and its jurists bowed before the tyrants, so that they raised up for them a religion apart from the established ways, performing the prayer outside its time and paying the alms other than in its right. They are agreed to endure them, constant in their need of them, certain that they can do without any other, saying that God will send no messenger and no warner after their Messenger. So you and those like you followed the saying of the great mass, awed by the abundance of consensus and the power of the sultans. You accepted it from them, adorned it with the fairest of your doctrines, favoured it with the finest of your words, and raised upon it an argument of justice where no argument could be raised against you. So you followed the tyrants above you out of dread, and those below you followed you out of desire, and they supposed it to be the truth.

"Now if this community must be believed in what it has decreed against God, that His proofs have been cut off from His creation by a single strand of consensus, then the earlier nations are more entitled to be believed, for they hold three qualities by which their argument against you is binding, and in which they are the more truthful, not you."

Abu Malik said: "And what are these qualities, in which they are given precedence over us, such that we must believe them if their word is true, or hold them liars if their word is astray?"

Salih said to him: "Their saying is like your saying, and your saying is of the same kind as theirs. Your hearts resemble one another, and yet they hold the precedence over you in the argument. The first quality: they say they are earlier in the religion of God than you, so they have the merit of those who went before. The second: there is consensus among them, and between them and you, in confirming what they hold, so they have the merit of the truthful. The third: your own testimony for them that their prophets are truthful and that they are messengers of God, while none of them has borne witness for your prophet, neither to prophethood nor to truthfulness. So they have precedence over you by your agreement with them and their standing apart from you, and they say that the knowledge you possess is copied from their knowledge, which is the origin of your knowledge. This is their argument against you, clear in speech and plain in justice. If the ruling of creation is to be imposed upon the Creator, then the ruling of these people is the more binding. But if the Creator is the One who rules over His creation, abrogating what He wills and confirming what He wills, with none to overturn His judgment, then by my life the saying of them all falls away. The Creator is foremost over the creation and over the command, and every day He is engaged in an affair, and His act is not to be denied, even were He to send a warner every day."

وَإِنَّهُ كُلَّ يَوْمٍ هُوَ فِي شَأْنٍ

"And truly, every day He is engaged in some affair."

Abu Malik said: "This community has strayed into a far error."

Salih said to him: "Most of the earlier ones strayed before them. For they cast out the friends of God from their right, set the foolish above them in the command, and made lawful what God had forbidden, deliberately, so that none might resemble prophethood, so that people would forget their longing for the prophets and would not be moved to kill them on that account. Instead they diverted them with kingship and largesse and led them along in the following of their whims. In this they were helped by men known outwardly for truthfulness and piety, who made a show of it before the people, until the one who used to command the community to good turned it away from the good, and the one who used to forbid it evil guided it toward evil, and the faithful to the friends of God grew few. So truth and falsehood came to alternate like light and darkness in their turning. Were it not for the remnants of God's friends and His just ones in the earth, and the revelation that comes to them, the little truth would never have prevailed over the much falsehood. For by what else could God distinguish the foul from the pure except by His just servants? And how could God's proof reach all creation except through them?"

Abu Malik said: "There is no way and no escape for the justice of God and His command, amid the multitude of His opponents, except by the just servants of God. By them the proof of God to His creation is made sound."

Salih said to him: "Then, since it is established that the company of the prophets of God are the just ones, is not each single one of them called just?"

He said: "Yes, he is a just one, as it is said:"

شَهِدَ فُلَانٌ وَهُوَ عَدْلٌ مِنَ الْعُدُولِ

"So-and-so bore witness, and he was a just one among the just."

Salih said to him: "It has then been established for you that the just one is the friend of God, and so the matter has passed beyond your saying that it is a mere attribute, and has become one who bears the attribute?"

He said: "Yes, this is sound in speech."

Salih said to him: "And whoever does not attain the one described, does the description profit him?"

He said: "The description does not profit him except by attaining the one described."

Salih pressed the point further. "Then whoever does not know the just one of Allah and His guardian, will his speech itself be just?"

And here, let me tell you, Abu Malik smiled. "You have brought me out of my own chapter on justice and monotheism," he said, "and nothing has brought me out of it but the truth itself, in the clearest of arguments and the gentlest of speech. After this, what position is left to be proved?"

"From here, O Abu Malik," Salih answered, "the camouflaged glass falls away from those who trade in the colors of jewels. They could not recognize its worth; but when it is set before one who knows, the pretenders are exposed, and those deceived by them are exposed along with them."

Abu Malik conceded the ground. "I have believed, and I act upon it. Yet how are we to be safe from the misleaders of the nations, and to know the causes of Allah, or even one cause among them, when we do not reach them in their own time? For in our age there are neither prophets nor messengers, as there were in the nations before us."

"Glory be to Allah, O Abu Malik," Salih replied. "You have kept speaking of the justice of Allah throughout your life; yet the moment we prove it to you in your own presence, where it becomes binding upon you, you turn back, claim you would be deceived about Allah, and attribute injustice to Him."

"God forbid," Abu Malik said. "Whoever says such a thing has transgressed and lied against Allah. From where have you bound me to that?"

"From your own words," said Salih, "that the age of the prophets has passed and that in our time there is neither prophet nor messenger. Tell me: was the sending of those earlier ranks a just act of His toward His servants, or was it in vain?"

"God forbid," he answered. "Only what is just issues from Him."

"Then what has excluded us from that justice?" Salih pressed. "Are we not His servants as they were? They were created as we are created, and we are bound in service as they were bound. Has the obligation been lifted from us after it was made binding upon them? Or has His giving run dry in our age, so that we lean only upon what was granted to them? Or has the way of Allah changed in our case after it was firmly fixed for those before us, so that we must rule that God Himself has changed? Or is justice a duty upon us while its very instrument is judged lawful for us to neglect, though it was forbidden to those before us, when there is no substitute for the words of Allah and none to overturn His judgment? Who deserves the name of the just except the guardians of Allah? There is no just one among all that God created except the one whom God has chosen, who became just by His command. That is why a judge is named just, for the sake of the one who appointed him, and a true word is named truthful, because of the truthfulness of the one who utters it."

Abu Malik said, "It is indeed so, and we are not permitted to say anything of God but the truth. Yet when we followed our own opinion in speech and judgment, in the absence of the rightly guided among us, we abandoned His judgment. We supposed this was part of Allah's ruling and His way with us, as it had been with those before us in the interregna between one messenger and the next: the stretch between Abraham and the time of Moses and the Torah, and the stretch between the Torah and the time of Jesus and the Gospel. We stood on that same footing, working through the interregnum, holding fast to the traditions and the way."

"The religion of Allah is far too precious to Him for that," Salih answered. "Was there ever truly an interregnum after the religion of Abraham, when Allah sent after him Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Jonah, and Shu'ayb, leading up to Moses? He inspired them with righteous deeds, the call to the religion of Abraham, the establishment of prayer by its way, and the giving of zakat by its obligation, until the ruling of the Torah was sent down upon Moses. And what interregnum stood between Moses and the Gospel, when Allah raised up after Moses Joshua son of Nun, and Elias, Talut, David, Solomon, Zechariah, and John, leading up to Jesus? They upheld the ruling of the Torah, holding to its religion, calling to it, and reciting its Psalms, until the ruling of the Gospel came down upon Jesus. So it was with those who passed before them and those who came after, and with many generations between them whom none but Allah can number. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs; for each He struck parables, and to each He called the way to the truth. Where, then, is this interregnum, and when was it? Allah said only:

عَلَىٰ فَتْرَةٍ مِّنَ الرُّسُلِ

"After a lull in the succession of messengers,"

and by that very word He affirmed the messengers. The lull was in the open rising of the call, after fear had come; so they called in secret and concealment. Why then would they be cut off from the earth? Not for the blink of an eye has the earth been emptied of the justice of Allah: either present and manifest, or fearful and veiled."

"This is a statement that cannot be refuted," said Abu Malik, "a proven word in which there is no doubt. The proof of Allah's argument through justice and the succession of messengers has been established and made plain. Show me, then, the meaning of their absence in our own time. What is its cause?"

"What was the cause of Moses absence," Salih replied, "when he went out of Pharaoh's city and passed on in fear, watchful, until he reached the land of Shu'ayb?"

"He fled from Pharaoh's people," Abu Malik said, "hiding from them, because he feared for himself when they had agreed to kill him."

"And have you heard, in this law of yours, of any of the just, the just ones of Allah and the sons of His prophets, who was killed while patiently bearing the burdens of the religion?"

"Yes," Abu Malik said. "Many of them have been killed."

"Then what justice of this age do you wish to appear to you and to the people of your time?" said Salih. "One who was slain, whom Allah raises up and sends to you after death? Or one who fled from among you, whom you would drive back to the very killing he escaped, when he has followed the way of Allah in His prophets and messengers, patient under fear and concealment, until Allah judges between him and us, and He is the best of judges?"

"There is no blame," Abu Malik said, "upon one who follows the way of the righteous by fearing for himself and fleeing from his people. The blame is upon the wicked nations and the helpers of the powers of darkness, for killing the prophets of Allah and His just ones, and for their agreement, all together, upon that deed."

"And what do you say of one who kills the just of Allah and His proof," Salih asked, "where does his end lie? And what of the one who helped him?"

"Both are in the Fire," Abu Malik said, "and so too anyone who raised a banner for them or carried an inkwell in their cause."

"And what do your jurists say of this?"

"They ask mercy for the slain," he said, "yet see no sin in the killer."

"And whoever obeys the killer of his own free will," said Salih, "is he not a partner with him in the blood of the slain?"

"Yes, by Allah," Abu Malik said. "He is his partner, and even worse than he."

"His partnership is plain enough in his aiding him," Salih said. "But how is he worse than the killer himself?"

"Were it not for the narrations of the jurists," Abu Malik answered, "the rule of the tyrants could never have stood through the folly of the foolish."

"You have spoken the truth, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "And have you been content, for yourself, to take their kingdom as your home and their land as your dwelling, their armies as your neighbors and their enforcers as your intimate friends? You are their just one: you judge for them by your knowledge and settle your needs through their kingship. You have prayed for them in your petitions and thanked them for what you sought from them. You have dressed their tyranny in the crown of your justice, and they grew proud; you have clothed their vainglory in the garment of the sacred law, and they overreached; you have lit up their falsehood with the dimming of your own truth. So the weak were led astray by your conduct, together with every scholar who was like you, and they said, These are the jurists of the community; in them we have our example. You went astray by your own reasoning, and led others astray by the reasoning of the tyrants; you supposed the one held to account was someone other than yourselves, and you forgot the word of Allah, Glorified is He:

وَلَا تَرْكَنُوا إِلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا فَتَمَسَّكُمُ النَّارُ

"And do not incline toward those who have done wrong, lest the Fire touch you.

"So you severed what Allah commanded to be joined, and did it with open eyes; you spoke of the outward forms of justice while wandering lost from its path. You came upon His saying:

أَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ وَأُولِي الْأَمْرِ مِنكُمْ

"Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you,

"and you made these powers of darkness your rulers, even in their disbelief. And you forgot the saying of Allah, Exalted is He:

قَاتِلُوا الَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُم مِّنَ الْكُفَّارِ

"Fight those of the disbelievers who are near to you.

"So you disobeyed the ones you were commanded to obey, and obeyed the ones you were commanded to fight, all the while supposing you did well. Misguidance ran its course with you, and mere opinion carried you so far that you said Allah will never raise a messenger or a warner in your age. Is it that the goodness of your deeds has set you beyond need of the guardians of Allah for the proof? Or that the ugliness of your deeds has cut you off from hope in Allah's acceptance of repentance? Or is it justice that you should judge God without justice, and be content, for your Messenger, with what you would never accept for yourselves in his place? Would any one of you be content that his son be killed, or that his house and his wealth be seized from him without right?"

"No one is content with that," Abu Malik said. "If a child of ours is killed, or a man is driven from his property, he is enraged, and his people are enraged for him, and they are not satisfied until he is satisfied."

"Then Allah and His Messenger have a greater right that you be angry for their sake," Salih said, "when His vicegerent is killed and His guardian is driven from the land."

"It grieves us," Abu Malik said, "that the guardian of Allah should be killed among us or driven from his land. But they have withdrawn from us of their own accord, and the tyrants have risen over us in their place."

"Had you supported the friends of Allah, they would have appeared," Salih answered. "Had you forsaken His enemies, those enemies would not have prevailed. But your standing toward the guardians of Allah has settled into three ranks. There is the one who strikes and is set over them, who never wearies of the hunt and is not sated even when he has his victory. There is the one who is pleased and gloating, biting his fingertips in spite, sparing no effort to incite others against them, never turning his gaze from them even when they are heedless. And there is the one who loathes what is done, who professes love for the guardians of Allah yet forsakes them, having no clear sight in faith and no trust to be kept in confidence. For this reason the guardians of Allah have withdrawn from you in caution, for your sake and for the sake of this vast multitude who have taken their own desires as their god, who have fallen upon the goods of this world and joined hands in a deed on a par with the killing of the prophets. They were blind to the fault of their conduct, so they neither held themselves to account for the wrong they did nor humbled themselves to repent of it. Instead they turned back and ascribed the injustice of their own deeds to Allah, and they said, Allah will send no messenger after their messenger, and no warner. So they made lawful the killing of the guardians of Allah without right. They wronged by their deeds, swerved by their words, and transgressed by their denial."

"Yes," Abu Malik said. "This is the very portrait of this nation of ours, and this is exactly what they say. Where, then, is deliverance from it, and where is the escape from the punishment of Allah?"

"To Allah," Salih said. "Take refuge in Him, and He gives you refuge. Repent to Him, and He accepts your repentance. Ask Him for the way of His guardians, and He delivers you, through your own deeds, from the doing of those who have wronged. So too with this multitude: if they repent to Allah, He accepts their repentance, and what would Allah then want with punishing them? Allah does not wrong them by the weight of a single atom, even when they wrong and lie; and He does not hasten them to punishment, even when they hasten to disobey."

"Then I seek refuge from the punishment of Allah," Abu Malik said, "by repenting to Him and seeking the means to what pleases Him. Grant something to the one who seeks you and to the one who asks to be guided, and your Lord will reward you."

"If you do that," said Salih, "you will find Allah nearer to pardoning you than you are to repenting to Him, for He loves those who turn back to Him; and you will find me eager to guide you, watchful over you."

Then, at the mention of repentance, Salih's eyes filled with tears, and he broke off his speech. He heartened them, and bade them go to his father's house. And he went in to his father, the scholar who had first sent him out, and consulted him about the affair of Abu Malik and his companions.

The scholar said: "You know your own companions best. If you see good in them, guide them with your guidance; and when you warn them, do not let eagerness carry you to inflict punishment upon them. Test them by withdrawing your attention from them, yet without harshness, and charge your elders to show them kindness and to be good to them in due season. The one who longs for good cannot stay hidden, and Allah will not abandon you at your back. He will open for you, from the light of His enabling grace, that by which the affair of His creation is set in order, and from the gates of guidance that by which He shows you the deeds of those who find their way."

So Abu Malik and his companions went on wavering in their trial until their affair was made complete, and they knew their own guidance, and the guardian of Allah among them turned toward that guidance with kindness. They gave thanks to Allah for it, and returned to their people as warners; and through them Allah guided a host of His servants to His religion.

And now let me speak to you plainly, in my own voice. Whatever in this account has taken the shape of a narrative was no idle telling, but a confirmation of what Allah has commanded; for in it lies the steadfastness of the messengers, the signs of the guardians, and the manners of the seekers.

Praise be to Allah, first and last. May Allah bless His Messenger, our master Muhammad, sent forth from Him to His creation as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner; and bless his guardian, the Imam of the God-fearing, the radiant leader of the bright-marked, the beloved of the Lord of the Worlds; and the Imams of the people of his house, whom Allah has favored and from whom He has removed every defilement, purifying them completely. Allah is sufficient for us, and how excellent a Trustee, how excellent a Master, and how excellent a Helper. There is no power and no strength except by Allah, the Most High, the Most Great.
