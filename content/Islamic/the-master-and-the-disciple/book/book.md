# The Master and the Disciple

## 1. The Persian Who Was Dead and Revived

As for the doctrines of the righteous and the manners of the seekers, we have heard of a man from the people of Persia (أهل فارس) who was afflicted by the calamity of ignorance, though he possessed a heart of keen retention, a sound intellect, and a cultivation he had won for himself. Yet ignorance held sway over him in the very knowledge we have described, for he had been born into it and raised in that condition. And so he strayed in the heat of his thirst, taking the shimmering mirage for the whiteness of water, until, when he reached it, he found it to be nothing at all — and there he found Allah before him, who settled his account. Then Allah honored his resting-place and lifted the veil from him. He found him astray and guided him, found him in want and enriched him with knowledge. By that favor he became one of the scholars of Tur (الطور) and one of the dwellers of the Bayt al-Ma'mur (البيت المعمور).

When his guidance was complete and he had attained the utmost of his hope, it fell upon him to give thanks to his Maker and to labor his way toward his Lord. Now he had heard a saying from his father, one that was to him as the soul is to the body: "The best of good deeds is to revive the dead" (إن أفضل الحسنات إحياء الأموات). So he said to himself: "I was dead and He revived me, ignorant and He taught me. I was not the first to be ignorant, that I should have outrun all people to knowledge, nor am I the last who will be ignorant, that teaching should end with me. It is a true obligation upon me to be among those who give thanks for this blessing, passing this trust on to those who come after me just as it was passed to me by those who came before. For this matter is, in its origin, from Allah, and it reached me only through a long chain of intermediaries, the first handing it to the second, and the second to the third, until it descended from the highest assembly down to the creatures who are the people of this world. Then it broke through the veil, and door after door thrust it forward until it reached me. Am I, then, to be its stopping-place and its end? Never. Those who transmitted it and kept faith with the trust of it have the prior claim to the reward and the merit; and if their earnings are not mine to rely upon, then I am rather the gain their labor has won. And if their bounty does not excuse me from labor of my own, then I remain as poor in my need to seek knowledge through action as I was poor in my need to seek the knowledge itself." He said this to himself, and he knew that what was required of him was the very like of what had been granted to him, and that the end of his affair, in the final reckoning, was one with its beginning.

So he left his family and his wealth behind, going out toward his Lord in answer to His call and summoning others toward the good, that a share of thanksgiving might fall due to him such as the share that had fallen due from him. He set out to travel through the lands, passing among the peoples of the non-Arabs and the tribes of the Arabs, studying faces and putting his questions to them, yet finding none who answered and meeting none who sought — until at last he reached the far edges of the island (الجزيرة). He entered one of its cities at an hour when its people were heedless. And as he was calling the signs of Allah to mind and searching through the crowds, his eye fell upon a company of the people of that land quarreling over religion without guidance and plunging into the depths of their own caprice. So he sat down a little apart from them, listening to their talk and reading their faces to see which of them held to the soundest way.

He said to himself: "These are the ones most fit to be revived, for three reasons. The first is that the proof has already reached them. The second is that they are a people of religion. And the third is that they are the nearer to right guidance, given their wakefulness toward religion, their shared attention to it, and their shared search after it; and one who seeks a thing is close to finding it."

When they had finished the discussion they were engaged in, they turned to him and said: "Who are you, and where are you from, young man?"

He said: "I am a servant of Allah (عبد الله), and one of the dwellers of His sanctuary (حرم)."

They said: "Then what is your need, and what is your work?"

## 2. A Stranger in the City

He answered them: "My need has slipped past me, and I am hard at work in search of it."

They said: "Did anything of what we told you please you?"

He said: "All of it is strange—to anyone who stops to wonder."

They said: "But wonder is of two kinds, the one praiseworthy and the other blameworthy."

He said: "Yes, I have come to know that. And words too are of two kinds, the praiseworthy and the blameworthy."

They said: "Which kind, in your view, is the praiseworthy one?"

He said: "The kind that is sound."

They said: "And what makes words sound?"

He said: "That their source is from Allah, and that they call to Allah."

They said: "And what makes them blameworthy?"

He said: "That their source is caprice, and that they call to something other than guidance."

They said: "You have spoken the truth. Have you nothing of your own to say, that we might hear it?"

He said: "I have nothing of my own to offer. I only follow after what is rightly said."

They said: "Then let us hear from you what might show us your way. Your manner has grown sweet to us and your fellowship dear, and we hope that you may be a door of mercy Allah has opened for us."

He said: "So be it." Then he composed himself to speak, and the people fell silent before him, and he turned his face toward them.

He said: "He who created all things by His power and set them in due proportion, and who decreed by His generosity and so gave guidance, is the One who opened up the broad pastures of the way—making their surface like scattered chaff and their depths dark and abundant, their paths never tangled and their tracks never worn away. Were it not so, the imagination would lose itself in the search for Him and minds would fall short of reaching Him. He is not so disclosed in His being that any but His own could lay hold of Him, nor so that the goal of His path could ever be turned aside. Rather He is generous and yet veiled: His knowledge is held back for the sake of its worth, and His secrets are guarded for those who are worthy of them. Their honor is the very plumage of guidance, and their fullest ornament is the garment of piety. They are stamped with the light of lights—a trust laid up in the breasts of the chosen and the righteous, the keepers of the Word, the honored scribes,

a trust that they carry out and a limit they never overstep. It is the law of the Judge and the bounty of the Most Merciful. He honored it and set it deep within, He sheltered it and kept it hidden, He gave it its full due and sealed it with His covenants. So the ambitions of the thinkers cannot reach it, and the contrivances of the schemers cannot lay hold of it. Its outward face is a remembrance and a proof for those who are guided, and its inner face is a sure and plentiful drink, whose sky is brought near to those who gaze up and whose ground is made level for those who search, and whose shooting stars have their appointed marks for the seekers. It has watering places for those who journey toward it, and signs whose marvels follow hard upon one another, pointing through their likenesses to Him. Its course is a bestowing of favor and its way an unfolding in full detail. Its rulings are binding duties and its practice an ordered body of law. Truthfulness has ennobled its teachings and justice has made its yields wholesome, so that He has made it for every good a guide and a road, and made it, for its own inner meaning, both a shield and a guide. Then He drove the minds of men—through their ignorance and their need—to seek out knowledge and sufficiency. Blessed is He who made the night and the day to follow one upon the other, for whoever would remember or would give thanks. And may Allah send His blessings upon the one He chose from among His servants and made a warner to the worlds."

Then he said: "Fear Allah with the fear of one who dreads Him, and dread Him with the dread of one who hopes in Him. Seek knowledge, and you will come to know; and act, and you will be shown mercy."

Then his eyes brimmed over with tears, and he broke off his speech and, weeping, took his leave of the people; he rose and turned back toward his mount. Weeping came over the people too, at the warmth of his words and the depth of his counsel, and they longed to rise and go along with him. But their sense of good manners held them back from pressing in upon him all at once, for they did not know whether that would be welcome to him, and so they turned back to their own dwellings. Yet one man did follow after him out of the company—a youth, the youngest of them in years and the most even-tempered of them all. Discipline had brought his mind to its full ripeness and reflection had set his heart alight, and there weighed upon him, together with all that, everything he had just heard.

## 3. The Boy at the Door — Limits and Conditions

His eyes brimmed over with tears, and he broke off his speech. He took his leave of the people, rose, and set out upon his journey. The people wept at the warmth and the eloquence of his sermon, and they longed to rise and go with him; yet out of courtesy they held back, keeping only his memory with them through all of it. They did not truly grasp what they had assented to, and so they turned away to their houses.

The youth kept the Master's company, with his leave, until the Master reached the end of his journey. The boy had a father who was a great sheikh among the Arabs, the most honorable of them in lineage and the most influential of them over the people of religion. When they arrived, the Master went in and brought the boy in with him. He called for food, and once they had finished and had settled into their talk, the boy spoke.

"O wise one," he said, "you have spoken and I have heard; you have preached and I have taken heed. You adorned the religion with the finest ornament, you made it desirable with the best of longing and the fullest of provision, and you called to it by the noblest of its trusts. Minds yearned toward your call, drawn through the openings your words had made in their hearts. Such excellence could come only from one whose inward reality, the batin, has been perfected as a teacher, and whose outward bearing, the zahir, has broadened his forbearance — for he is a fountain of wisdom from a firm source, whose blessings rise over the worlds, and the branches of whose branches bear fruit for the seekers. I hope that you are one of those blessed branches, for I see in you a noble mind, a sound core, and a perfect intellect. You have said that minds are driven by ignorance to seek knowledge and capacity, and my mind is among the neediest and most destitute of them. Is there, then, a way to life for me?"

The Master said: "He who created the creatures by His power did not create them in vain, nor fashion them scholars from the outset, nor abandon them in the wastes of ignorance, nor is He content that stupidity should be their culture or the garments of ignorance their dress. He has appointed for them, as their way out of all this, the very faculties by which He composed their frame and completed their creation. He made their hearing, their eyes, and their hearts filled with lights, as a mark of His honoring them and as an argument standing against them. Then He placed the treasures of His wisdom and the tokens of His will within doctrines that please Him, doctrines borne by His elect from among His creation. He charged them to hold to that religion and to call others to it, and He laid upon all the duty of answering His call through the very faculties we have named, so that God might set apart the corrupt from the good. So the seeker, by his seeking, comes to see, and the one heedless of his own backwardness is exposed by it. No argument remains for anyone against God after His Messengers, and no excuse for going astray once the evidence has been given."

The boy said: "The absence of any excuse has frightened me, and the presence of the evidence has made me long for it. I beg you for a proof, and I will be a lowly servant to you."

The Master said: "God did not create men as scholars. He created them as small children, ignorant, knowing nothing. There is no greatness save after smallness, and no scholar save after ignorance. Smallness is the prelude to greatness, and ignorance a guide to knowledge. In the same way, knowledge can be borne only if a lesser knowledge is brought in before it — one quicker than it into the hearts and smoother into the understanding — so that its beginning may take hold in the imagination, the limbs may be humbled to its obligations, the ribs may shiver at its depth, and the minds may bear its aim. This is the Sunna of God with His servants. It does not befit us to break from the community by our own opinion, nor to follow, under our own guidance, anything other than the Sunna."

The boy said: "I am too small for what I desire, so raise me. I am ignorant of what I seek, so teach me. I am bound by your favor, so have mercy on me. You yourself were once in this very state, and God drove the blessing to you at the hands of one whose trial with you was great and whose thanks fell due upon you. I know well the plight of the man who suffers the narrowness of ignorance and the need of one whose resourcefulness has failed him."

The scholar said: "The state you describe is indeed as you say, and I am jealous for you to be free of it. But my mercy toward you does not lie in bringing your hope nearer while your labor lags behind. Every claim is accepted only when it is truthful; knowledge gives no benefit until it is acted upon, and action gives no benefit until it is accepted. The truthfulness of speech is its justice, and the proof of knowledge lies in the open roads of deeds and in the documented likenesses — in all that the ends of the earth have held and over which night and day have differed. Do not pursue an argument idly, do not cast one aside idly, and do not accept one by mere imitation, lest the first doubt that rises against it tear it out of your heart. Do not deny an argument once its proof stands, lest you become a partisan of falsehood; and do not abandon inquiry into it and understanding of it, lest your opinion be left barren. Think well of me without reserve, strive in your questioning without suspicion, seek the religion as one like you should seek it, and seek God's help in your affair. God does not let the reward of the seeker go to waste, nor deprive of recompense anyone He sees seeking the good."

The boy said: "You have dealt justly with yourself, in fitting and effective deed, and you have pointed to the truth in the finest of words. I ask God's help, and I ask you concerning this matter to which you call: what is it, whose is it, and to whom does it belong?"

The scholar said: "It is the religion of God, which He has accepted, and which can be accepted from Him alone and from no other. He delivered it first to the elect among His Messengers, and then in general to all His creation. Upon the one specially favored fall thanks for it, action by it, and the call to it. Upon the one deprived of it falls the seeking after it until he comes to know, and obedience to the one who guides him."

The boy said: "This favor that God has singled out for His friends — are all such favors equal in worth, so that men are equal in their excellence, or do some surpass others?"

The scholar said: "Have we not already said that every knowledge has before it a lesser knowledge that taught it, and that every favor has its bearers, so that they stand at graded ranks before their Lord, each charged according to what he has gained of knowledge? Then to every possessor of merit is given his merit, in justice from God toward His creation and in mercy to His servants."

The boy said: "This to which you call me — is it that very favor, or is it something other than it?"

The scholar said: "It is not identical with it in its full reality, nor wholly other than it. Rather it is from it, as a single scoop of water from the breadth of the Euphrates. I call you to what God has accepted for His servants and honored on the day He completed it — mighty and guarded, generous and hidden. He ennobled it by His reward, an honor for the one who upholds it, and He made its retribution a debasement for the one who wastes it. Its outward form is figures, and its inward reality is knowledge. Its wisdom is a light, and its justice is plain. This is what I call you to."

The boy said: "What has made you more entitled to this matter than others, and more deserving of it than the rest, when you claim it is from God for all creation alike, so that everyone lays claim to it and denies it to his neighbor? What has made you more worthy of the name of truth than they, when this matter came to you only by the same road it came to them?"

The scholar said: "There is no scholar who speaks a word of truth but that he has drawn it from the very origins from which we ourselves have drawn, and in this they meet us, acknowledging it from us and among themselves. What set us apart from them, then, was their abandoning of those principles while we held fast to them: we obeyed where they disobeyed, we preserved what we were entrusted with where they squandered it, we upheld what we were charged with where they neglected it. So they agree with us in acknowledgment and differ from us in deed. Their acknowledgment is a witness for us against them, and their squandering is the mark of their poverty toward us and their need of what is in our hands. They are like brothers who inherited great wealth from their father and divided it equally among them. Some squandered their portion until they fell into poverty, while others kept theirs, guarded it, and traded with it, so that the wealth in their hands multiplied. Then the poor among the brothers turned to the wealthy, to serve them and to seek their bounty. So too are these who wasted the way of their Messengers: their ignorance drove them to seek knowledge from the people of the noble origins. The proof that we are more deserving of the truth than they is their need of us and our sufficiency without them. So our scholar has become the one who answers their ignorant, our clothed one covers their naked, and our rich one feeds their hungry. Thus the truth is bound to the man of merit, the credit belongs to every possessor of favor, and thanks are owed to every doer of kindness."

The boy said: "As for your superiority over them, that has become clear; and as for the argument of truth, it has been established against every one of the common people who squanders. But what is your argument against me, when I already acknowledge what you have said, stand by what you have described, deny what you have denied, and know the truth and its worth, and know falsehood and its people?"

The scholar said: "My argument against you is the same as my argument against them, since you have parted from them in outward description but agreed with them in the inner meaning."

The boy said: "How can that be, when I acknowledge the truth to its rightful owner while they deny it?"

The scholar said: "If you have parted from them by acknowledgment where they denied, you have agreed with them in withholding where they withheld. For what does acknowledgment profit without a turning back to act, and what harm does denial do when the reality is present?"

The boy said: "Then I turn back to the people of truth by their right, if you make me know them, or to an agent who can establish it for them. Show me which of the two you are able to do."

The scholar said: "Do you not see that you have been driven to need the establishing of the rightful owner of the truth, or of his agent, just as the common people were driven to need the establishing of the truth itself? Deeds have set you apart from them, and ignorance has joined you to them."

The boy said: "You speak truly. Make it clear to me."

The scholar said: "Have you not acknowledged that my argument stands against you and against the common people?"

He said: "Yes."

The scholar said: "Then can anyone establish for himself a truth that belongs to another?"

He said: "No one is able to do that."

The scholar said: "Then the argument has been established against you and against the common people. Nothing remains but submission, and turning to the one whom you cannot do without and who can do without you."

The boy said: "As for my poverty, I acknowledge it. As for the One who is rich while I am in need, I seek Him, so have mercy on me."

The scholar said: "Have you ever seen anyone whom poverty drove to a man of bounty attain anything of that bounty except by earning or by favor?"

The boy said: "Yes. None of it is attained except by earning or by favor."

The scholar said: "And do the people of favor gain any share of what belongs to the people of earning?"

He said: "No."

The scholar said: "Then seek this matter by work, and you will attain it as your desert. Do not seek it by flattery, for then you would attain it by favor, and so be left deprived."

The boy said: "You are the key to goodness and its guide. Show me the more meaningful of the two paths and the nearer of them to guidance. The greatness of my need has bound my captivity fast to you."

The scholar said: "O my son, there is nothing beyond your reach, and no reproach against you. What you have hoped from me shall come to you. But it has limits not to be transgressed, and conditions not to be forgotten."

The boy said: "Set whatever limit you please, and lay down whatever condition you see fit. I hope you will find me patient under your testing and grateful for your kindness."

The scholar said: "The first of the limits is to uphold the outward meaning of the Book and its laws, and to act upon what you know of it. For whoever acts for God by what he knows, God guides him to what he does not know."

The boy said: "But the Books are many, and all of them are from God. For every Book there is a scholar among creation who cleaves to it, and they are all agreed upon guarding it and upon the religion within it."

The scholar said: "We have come back to our first words, and we are made to need the settling of what should have needed no settling."

The boy said: "How is that?"

The scholar said: "From your saying that the Books are many, that all are from God, and that each has its own scholar. Do you then imagine that the words of God and His Books cancel one another, that the first of them gives the lie to the last, and the last to the first?"

The boy said: "I do not imagine that. But what is the proof of it?"

The scholar said: "Had creation been able to do all that is in the first Book, it would have led them to the second; and had they done what is in the second, it would have led them to the third, until the work reaches its end in the last of them, since the last is worthier than what came before it. For though all of them are from God, the later is nearer to the present age and clearer in its way, abrogating what preceded it, with nothing coming after it to abrogate it."

The boy said: "You speak truly, and you have shown me the knowledge of truth and the sign of truthfulness. I hold to the limits you have ordained upon me. So what are your conditions for me?"

The scholar said: "My conditions upon you are five: do not fail me if I entrust you; do not conceal anything from me if I ask you; do not seek me out until I answer you; do not ask me for anything until I begin with you; and do not mention my affair to your father."

The boy said: "I will do all that you have named, and it is easy for me set against the greatness of what I hope from you. But how am I to manage my father? He is one of the heaviest trials upon me and the hardest I have to bear. Will you permit me to remove myself from him?"

The scholar said: "To withdraw from him would be a signpost pointing others toward you. Rather stay with him, be dutiful to him, satisfy him with some measure of outward agreement, keep your secret from him, and veil your affair. God will suffice us against his harm and the harm of others."

The narrator said: Then the scholar and the boy took leave of one another, and they remained apart for a long stretch of their days. The boy stayed perplexed in his affair. He knew nothing but the law at which he had been made to halt, he did not know where the scholar dwelt, and he could not seek him out, bound by the condition laid upon him, even as the absence lengthened. All the while the scholar was watching his patience and his endurance of what had been laid upon him. At last, on a quiet night when creation was still and the spies had grown heedless, when they had settled together in their assembly, the boy sensed that his need was drawing near and that the chance to be alone with his companion had come. He fell down before him in prostration, and when he raised his head, the scholar spoke.

"I saw you prostrate in thanksgiving," said the scholar. "Thanksgiving before the deed breeds suspicion, and thanksgiving cut short after the deeds makes the deeds themselves vain."

The boy said: "All your words are truthful, and all your works are mastery. My thanks are only for the favor that has already come from you, and by thanking I hoped for an increase from you."

The scholar said: "Thanksgiving deserves that it should bring increase to the thankful. Your right is now due, so turn to me with your heart, bring your understanding present, and listen to what is laid before you."

The boy said: "I have brought the instrument of understanding present and taken up the equipment of hearing. Say what seems good to you."

The scholar said: "The religion has a key that unlocks it and locks it, like that which distinguishes unlawful union from lawful marriage."

The boy said: "This key you have named is great in the sight of God, since it distinguishes the lawful from the unlawful, and truth from falsehood. So what is it?"

The scholar said: "It is the covenant of God, which affirms His rights, gathers His obligations, and makes Paradise binding for His friends — the rope of God upon His earth and the safe haven among His servants. I will recite it to you, and I will bind you by it."

The boy said: "Yes, take from me whatever you please. I do not spurn your judgment, and I do not overstep your way."

The narrator said: Then the scholar set about reciting the covenant to the boy, delivering it to him slowly and binding him fast by it, while the boy could not master himself for awe, and his tears ran down from the intensity of the lesson, until he reached the end of the covenant. Then he praised God and glorified Him, and gave thanks for what had come to him, and he knew with certainty that, by accepting their pledge, he had entered the Party of God (Hizb Allah) and the company of His friends. Then he fell silent from his praising, and the scholar began to expound and explain.

## 4. How the World Was Made

The scholar began with the first of the causes of the manifest order and the origin of its creation. God, he said, is the Creator of all things and the One who composes them; He created them and brought them into being. He is not merely the first of every created thing, as though He had taken some earlier creation as evidence for what came after — rather, He is the First of all firsts and the Inheritor of everything. He began the creation of what He created out of light, and from that light three words branched forth. The first is the will; from the will branched the command, and from the command branched the saying — that He says to whatever He wills "Be," and it is. So the first creation is a will, then a command, then a saying.

These are three words, and from them "كن" is derived, which is two letters, and "فيكون," which is five letters, so that together they are seven letters; and from these seven letters seven things branch forth. From the light He created air, the first of them: singular, without consort, unique among things, mingling with them and surrounding them, yet surrounded by none. It is therefore the proof of the One concerning whom it is said "ليس كمثله شيء" — there is nothing like Him. Then, out of those three words, He created water, darkness, and light — the light of the heavens and the earth. Then He created smoke out of the water, He created the condensing of heat out of the darkness, and He created fire out of the light. These are three things branching from three things before them, and each became a consort to its origin, since it was created from it. The seventh, the air, holds sway over them all. This was the beginning of creation and the beginning of things.

When creation had been established upon seven principles, and the order of forms had been laid down according to likeness and analogy, He created seven heavens and their signs out of the smoke, and seven earths and their sustenances out of the condensing of heat. These are the seven branches drawn from those seven origins.

We then consider the signs of the heaven and the sustenances of the earth, asking what they are and what bears witness to them. Here we return in speech to the three words and their letters, from which the seven were created — namely, "a will to command by saying." That is twelve letters. Its signs in the heaven are the twelve constellations, and its witnesses are seven kingdoms. Its signs upon the earth are twelve islands, and its witnesses are seventeen seas. These are the signs of the heavens and the earth, which people pass by even as they turn away from them.

Then the heavens and the earth were closed up in darkness by the black heat, which belongs to the dominion of the earth. Then He pierced them through with His lights, and light and darkness — the two opposed realities — were intermingled. He distinguished between them by night and day, as two signs. He made the night and its sign a course of darkness and apportioned it, and He made the day and its sign a course of light and apportioned it. So the night grew dim with its darkness, and the moon and the stars are its sign, while the day shone with its light, and the seeing rays of the sun are its sign. Then out of the two of them the days were brought forth, passing in turn between night and day, and they are seven days. Their signs are that each day has twelve hours, and each night twelve hours.

When the creation of things had been completed upon these principles, He made them in pairs, male and female. I do not mean male and female in the bodily sense, but in the sense of the superior and the subordinate. Then this practice went forth in the creation of pairs, and from their substances He created every living thing — humankind, beasts, birds, and creeping things. Then this same practice extended into speech, and speech too became paired: qualities and things qualified, names and meanings. So glory be to Him who created all the pairs and made them outward signs of His command, likenesses of His religion, and proofs of His power — pointing by means of things to their Originator, and by means of the creation to its Maker.

Then He opened it with His light, began it with His command, created it by His knowledge, and sealed it — from among His names — with the letters of "بسم الله الرحمن الرحيم." When He had perfected the whole of it by His making and composed it in pairs by His measuring, He chose for Himself, out of the choicest of His will and the depths of His unseen, a great knowledge and a clear light, and made the two of them a religion by which God is served and through which one draws near to Him. Then He honored it with the sacred laws and the books, and pointed to it through the parables and the customs. Then He set up for it, from among the best of His creation, banners and proofs and rulers, and made them the houses of His prophethood, the treasuries of His knowledge and wisdom, the interpreters of His revelation, and His successors upon His earth — guides upon the path of salvation in what is outward and what is inward. Among them are the imams and the speakers; among them are the proofs and the chiefs; among them are the summoners to the good and the learned. In their stations they are like the stars of heaven, for by them the works are raised up and in them the likenesses reach their end. Their imam is like the great sun among the lights, veiled by its own coverings from sight, and that sun is his outward form and his likeness. The proof of their imam, and his gate, is like the shining moon, which is his outward form and his likeness. Their summoners are like the radiant stars, which are their outward forms and their likenesses.

All the pairs I have described to you are outward and inward, likeness and thing represented. The outward of the seven heavens are likenesses, and their inward are the seven speakers, the messengers endowed with firm resolve. The seven earths are outwardly likenesses, and their inward are the imams of guidance. For each speaker among them there are twelve chiefs, and their likeness from the seven heavens is the twelve constellations. Likewise, each imam among the imams of guidance has twelve proofs by which he is upheld before his people, and their likeness in the earth is the twelve islands, which are the dwellings of the chiefs — on every island a proof. Every proof has summoners, many and few, for He increases in creation whatever He wills, so that the proof may be conveyed — the proof of God to His creation — that people may have no argument against God after the messengers.

Through these causes, the causes of God that I have described to you, the proof of God reaches His creation, and His justice appears among them, outwardly and inwardly. The inward is the religion of God, by which the friends of God (رض) have bound themselves, and the outward is the sacred laws of the religion and their likenesses. So the religion became to the laws a soul and a spirit, and the laws became to the religion a body and a guide. The body cannot stand except by the soul, for the soul is its life, and the soul cannot stand except by the body, for the body is its instrument. In the same way, the outward things — the sacred laws and the rest — cannot stand except by the inward religion, for it is their light and their meaning and the spirit of life within them; and the inward cannot stand except by the outward, for the outward is its body and its proof. The outward is the knowledge of this world, and it is seen only through it; the inward is the knowledge of the hereafter, and it is seen only through it. There is not a single letter among the letters of the inward, nor a single friend among the friends, but that its witnesses in the outward are many, on account of the abundance of the likenesses and the breadth of the sacred laws.

Speech upon this grows long and multiplies, and the more its answer increases from the words of wisdom, the more its rightness is hidden, until its end causes its beginning to be forgotten — on account of its preciousness and the purity of its essence. For the light of wisdom overshadows one part of itself with another, just as the light of the sun overshadows the light of the moon and the stars and makes it faint. In the same way do the words of wisdom adorn the tongue of the wise man.

## 5. The World, the Hereafter, and the Speech of Parables

The boy said: This world, in which so great a creation was set, holds a multitude of people, the first of them and the last, and they are divided over it. Some have given themselves over to the world and chased after it, seeing no worth in anything but heaping more of it up. Others rebuke it and live as ascetics within it, yet without doing away with it altogether, even though much of it is forbidden and only a little truly so. From you I have heard nothing said or reported about it. What is your judgment of it?

The scholar said: As for this world, no one will ever rightly rebuke it, neither the learned man nor the ignorant one. The ignorant man knows its worth by his very ignorance of what lies beyond it, and the learned man knows its worth through the worth of its meaning. None ever rebukes this world but an ignorant man. And how could it be permitted for the creation to rebuke the work of the Creator? Say rather that the world is built, for anyone who cares to see, upon seven things that branch from seven origins: heaven, earth, darkness, light, vessels, prosperity, and misery. These are seven qualities, and from each of them seven further qualities branch.

The heavens are seven, and the earths are seven.

The light has seven days that revolve upon seven nights. Saturday is for slumber and rest. Sunday is for the limits and the sunna. Monday is for the mathani, in the great meaning, al-Kabir. Tuesday has three houses: the apparent, the inward, and the inward of the inward. Wednesday is for the four sacred ones. Thursday is the fifth of the causes of God. And Friday is the greatest law of God, the namus.

The darkness is the seven nights, a repose for the seven days, so that every day has its night.

The vessels are seven: camels, horses, mules, donkeys, elephants, oxen, and ships. By these seven the regions of the earth are crossed. Camels are for the sands, horses for the open plains, mules for the mountains, donkeys for nearby errands, elephants for marshes and wasteland, oxen for salt flats and thickets, and ships for the seas. So they are seven mounts for seven domains.

The blessings of this world rest upon seven: air, water, light, darkness, food, clothing, and marriage. Air is the space between sky and earth, in which the winds rise and the breaths pass to and fro, and it is the very life of the animal. Water is the life of every living thing. Light is the substance of sight. Darkness is what makes the plants grow. Food is the body's sustenance. Clothing is a shield that veils what is shameful. Marriage is the seed of reproduction and the cause of offspring. Some of these are the keys of the good, and some are the mines of blessing. Which of them is to be blamed, when every one of them is among the gifts of God and His blessings, for which His servants owe thanks?

Their opposites in misery are seven as well: heat, cold, hunger, thirst, fear, sickness, and death. These are seven that must be borne with patience, once their meaning is known.

The boy said: These are the attributes of this world, and I do not doubt that anything in it is other than what the Creator has made and composed. So what do the scholars mean when they censure the world, and what do the ignorant mean when they praise it? I see that the ignorant have reached the meaning, even in their ignorance. And if that is so, then the soundness of their case would leave the scholars in the wrong, since the scholars contradict the people of ignorance at every turn.

The scholar said: The scholars did not go wrong in censuring this world, but no creature knows what they mean by it. And the ignorant did not grasp the meaning of what they praised in the world, for they were ignorant of its meaning. Whoever is ignorant of a thing is its enemy.

The boy said: Show me the truth of what the scholars say, so that I may believe them as I ought. And set apart the speech of the people of ignorance by whatever lays bare its falsehood, since the outward appearance points the other way. My chest has grown weary of this ignorance, and I hope for no relief except through knowledge of it.

The scholar said: This world, and all that I have described of it to you, is an outward standing for an inward. It has no subsistence except through that inward, for the inward is to it as the soul is to the body. Whoever knows the soul finds the body made wholesome for him. Whoever does not know the soul and acknowledges only the body dwells upon a corpse, and the corpse is forbidden. From here the scholars forbade this world, because it is dead. They forbade the dead thing that God has forbidden, until one comes to it from where God wills. And the people of ignorance are shown to lie in their praise of this world, because they did not know its inner meaning, nor what God intended by it. They supposed that God had created the world for some meaning other than its own, but God did not create things in vain. Had the world and all within it been created for the world alone, He would have set in it a creation that fades away, with no consequence and no meaning. Yet every act without consequence is play, and every speech without meaning is falsehood, and God is far too exalted for that.

The boy said: The truthfulness of the scholars now stands established, their virtue and excellence are proven, and the people of ignorance are exposed in their falsehood, by the argument that has come to light. There remains for us the knowledge of the inward of the world and of what is in it. And I find that the distress I complained of to you has been lifted.

The scholar said: What is the reward of the one who brought you out of the distress of poverty into the breadth of ease?

He said: Thanks and praise.

The scholar said: And what is the reward of the one who brought you out of the narrowness of ignorance into the breadth of knowledge?

He said: Obedience and works.

The scholar said: This world and all its like are an outward standing for the hereafter and what is in it, for the hereafter is its soul and its life. Whoever labors in this world for the world, with no knowledge of the hereafter, his labor is misguidance, for it has neither meaning nor consequence. But whoever labors in this world for the hereafter, believing in it, his labor has meaning and consequence, and his labor is thanked.

The boy said: You have spoken truly. So make plain to me the features of this world and what is in it, and set the parables against the things they signify from within.

The scholar said: God, blessed and exalted, has created nothing among the things of this world — no beast upon the earth, no bird that flies on its wings, nothing moist and nothing dry among the fixed inanimate things such as mountains, stones, trees, and the minerals of gold, silver, and gems, nor anything at all, great or small — except that it is a parable struck. Do you not see that the atom, for all its smallness and the subtlety of its meaning, has limits: before it and behind it, to its right and to its left, above it and below it? Those are six limits, and the seventh is the atom itself. So it is with everything God has created, from the ant and the mosquito up to what stands above them of heaven and earth and all that lies between, great and small alike: each has six limits, and the thing itself is the seventh. This is a proof pointing to the six origins from which things were created, while the air that encompasses them is the seventh, master over them all. If your breast is wide enough for these questions and for looking into the parables, matching each thing with its counterpart among the inward pairs, then ask about whatever occurs to you.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the heaven, in its height above all things and the breadth of its regions, so that it has gathered all things within it?

The scholar said: Its likeness is the speaking Imam, al-Imam al-Natiq, who is raised above all creation by his virtue and the height of his degrees, and who has gathered within himself all the rulings — the rulings of the Shari'a — by the breadth of his knowledge.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the constellations and the other stars?

The scholar said: They are the likeness of the twelve chiefs, the nuqaba, the chiefs of the speaking Imam. And its stars are his summoners, the du'at, who call to the good by the permission of their Lord.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the earth and its breadth, which was made a resting place and a bed for this creation?

The scholar said: It is the likeness of the gate, the bab, of the speaking Imam during his lifetime, and of his successor, the wasi, after his death. He is a recourse for the people and a place of settling for the one who seeks guidance, and he is a knower of the parables and of the inner interpretation, the ta'wil.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the twelve islands in the earth?

The scholar said: They are the likenesses of the twelve arguments, the hujaj, the arguments of the successors.

The boy said: What is the likeness of its rivers and its springs?

The scholar said: The likeness of those who call to the good, the du'at.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the seven kingdoms of the heavens and their orbits?

The scholar said: Those are causes between God and the speakers, the nutaqa, and they belong to Him. They are the seven and the two orbits, and those are the greatest causes of God.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the seven seas?

The scholar said: They are causes between God and the imams of the speaker, beneath those first causes.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the salt water and the fresh water, and their difference upon the earth?

The scholar said: The salt water is the likeness of the knowledge of the outward, in which there is no summons, no da'wa, and no summoners, just as salt water yields no crop and nothing else. The fresh water is the likeness of the knowledge of the inward, whether as springs within the earth or as water flowing over it. So too is knowledge: the outward, by which the summoners run their course, and the inward, which is kept hidden.

The boy said: How did water come to be a likeness of knowledge?

The scholar said: Because water is the life of every living thing, and likewise knowledge is the life of every knower.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the air that surrounds all things?

The scholar said: The air is too great to be set as a likeness for any thing that might signify it.

The boy said: Is it greater, or are the heaven and the earth its equal, so that they too may be likenesses?

The scholar said: Glory be to God — rather it is greater than both. The breadth of the earth within the atmosphere is like a signet ring in an open desert. The breadth of the atmosphere within the sky is like a signet ring in an open desert. And the breadth of the sky and its regions within the breadth of the air is like an egg in an open desert. What thing to be signified could comprehend this, so as to be a proof and a likeness of it?

The boy said: Then what is its meaning?

The scholar said: It is a proof of the One who has nothing like Him, glory be to Him and exalted above what they associate with Him.

The boy said: But did the Creator not say, "فَلَا تَضْرِبُوا لِلَّهِ الْأَمْثَالَ" (So do not strike parables for God)?

The scholar said: And did He not also say, "وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى" (And to God belongs the highest similitude)?

The boy said: Glory be to Him who has set enigmas in His Book so that one may come to it by its own door, and you are one of its gates. Make plain to us how this is.

The scholar said: Everything has a meaning, and the meaning of the parables is this: the outward is evidence of the inward. He, blessed and exalted, has forbidden that He be measured against anything of His creation, such that one should say, "This is the likeness of God," as one says, "This is the likeness of the Imam," or "This is the likeness of the hujja," or "This is the likeness of the da'i." Their likenesses became signs pointing to them and to their ranks. So it is not fitting, given His greatness, that the Creator be measured against anything of His creation by any word or deed, nor is it said, "This is the likeness of God." Rather, the parables are taken according to their meanings, for their meanings carry what signifies: the small among them points to the great, the great points to the highest meanings, and the highest points to the Most High, the Highest. This is why He said, "وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى" (And to God belongs the highest similitude), meaning the highest proof, for there is no proof of anything higher than the proof of God. And that is the air: highest above the heaven and the earth, encompassing all things while nothing among them encompasses it. It is the highest similitude, and it is the proof of the One who has nothing like Him. He did not say that there is nothing like Him — for indeed there is nothing like Him — but rather He meant that no proof of Him is like other proofs. To Him belongs the highest similitude, and there is no proof like His proof among the proofs of any other, among those of His creation that we have described. So blessed be God, Lord of the mighty Throne.

The boy said: The meaning has grown vast and the reach has widened. Was there, before the air, any thing between it and God, given the greatness of what you have described in it?

The scholar said: Yes.

The boy said: What is it?

The scholar said: The time.

The boy said: And what is the time?

The scholar said: It is the time of the will by which He created what He willed and commanded what He wished.

The boy said: You have plunged me into the depths of the seas of the highest assembly. Return me to the sciences of this world and their likenesses, that I may seek help through them in my affair and be made strong by them to bear what I am meant to bear. For I have grown afraid for myself and grieved for my mind, lest I be led astray into what I cannot fathom.

The scholar said: You rose high by your question, and so the answer rose with you to the highest of the outward meanings, and there your understanding was lost and your mind bewildered. How, then, would it be if I disclosed to you its inward? You and I would be as Moses and al-Khidr, the Knowing One.

The boy said: Does this inward have an inward more inward still?

The scholar said: By my life, the inward has an inward that is loftier in station, wider in power, and more complete in guidance, and it is the utmost of proofs pointing to the road of salvation.

The boy said: Then I see here three layers of knowledge: the outward, the inward, and the inward of the inward. Is there a proof of this?

The scholar said: And where would the proof be, if not for this and for that to which it points?

The boy said: Then show me a likeness of these three layers.

The scholar said: I will show you from the Book of God, exalted is He, from the argument of the intellect, and from what the whole body of the parables points to.

The boy said: I did not think there was any teaching beyond what I had already reached. Truly the affair of God is far greater than anything the imaginings of the pretenders can rise to.

The scholar said: As for the Book of God, exalted is He, His word: "يَا بَنِي آدَمَ قَدْ أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْكُمْ لِبَاسًا يُوَارِي سَوْآتِكُمْ وَرِيشًا وَلِبَاسُ التَّقْوَى ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ" (O children of Adam, We have sent down upon you a garment that covers your shameful parts, and adornment, and the garment of godliness — that is better). This is three: the outward, the inward, and the inward of the inward.

Al-Ghulam said: You have spoken truly.

The scholar said: As for the argument of the intellect: a thing is known only by a name, an attribute, or a meaning. The meaning is what the thing is in itself; the name is what it is called; and the attribute is what points to it. Likewise the religion of God has an outward name and an inward proof pointing to it, and it stands only by the two together. Nothing is pointed to except by a noun or an attribute, as when someone says, "The moon has risen." The meaning is the disk, "moon" is its name, and "has risen" is its attribute. Were he to say "has risen" and then fall silent, it would carry no meaning. And were he to say "moon" and then fall silent, it would carry no meaning, until, joining the name and the attribute, he says, "The moon has risen" — and then the meaning is made plain and the speech is sound. Just so, if you say "outward," it calls for an answer, that one should ask, "Outward of what?" And if you say "inward," it calls for an answer, that one should ask, "Inward of what?" But when you say, "the outward of the religion and its inward," the speech is understood, and it becomes a religion having an outward and an inward, just as the thing has a name and an attribute — as He said: "وَذَرُوا ظَاهِرَ الْإِثْمِ وَبَاطِنَهُ" (And forsake the outward of sin and its inward). So sin became a thing having an outward and an inward. Such too is the greater religion of God, whose laws are called its outward and whose inward is its inward, and it subsists in itself. To the knowledge of its meaning there are many parables that point, such as the hidden egg, which has an outward that shields it, an inward that is the white within, and an inward of its inward, the yellow yolk, which is its essence and its meaning.

The boy said: Are not the world and the hereafter a pair, the soul and the body a pair, and the knowledge of the outward and of the inward a pair — and are not pairs of this kind, as you have described, many?

The scholar said: You have spoken truly. But they are pairs — so of what are they the likeness?

The boy said: They are pairs that point to the religion of God.

The scholar said: You have spoken truly. The pair is an outward name and an inward attribute, pointing to the knowledge of God and to the religion of God, and that is the inward of the inward. Likewise the creation was created in three stations. The first is the creation of the angels, the second the creation of human beings, and the third the creation of the beasts. The outward of knowledge is the limit of the beasts, and whoever knows only that, with no inward, is at the level of the beasts. The knowledge of the inward is the knowledge of the children of Adam and their limit, and whoever knows it is a believer and is at the level of human beings. And the knowledge of the inward of the inward is the knowledge of the angels. Whoever knows it is spiritual in knowledge and bodily in frame, a prophet sent, whom God appoints as His deputy in His earth and by whom He establishes the argument over His creation. He is the veil of the angels, the cipher of revelation and its interpreter to the children of Adam. With him are the keys of the gardens, which none enters but the one who obeys him, and with him is the passage from the Fire, which none enters but the one who disobeys him. And the people are but two men: a divine scholar, and one who learns.

## 6. Three Layers of Knowledge

Of these two men, the scholar went on, the first is one who has been taught the very purpose of knowledge, one whose soul the spirit of certainty has quickened, so that he is a scholar by virtue of what he knows and a master by virtue of what he does. The second is a learner who pursues knowledge for the sake of his own deliverance. The rest of the people are savages and a rabble who know nothing; they follow every false guide, deceived by his error and seduced by his ignorance. They imagine that they are doing well, yet they have been cut off from the true examples set by those who came before them, and they will not escape what Allah has promised, for the disbelievers shall have the like of it.

The boy said: So this is what virtue is, and this is what knowledge and its unfolding are. Blessed are the guardians of God, and blessed are those who follow benevolence. Will I ever attain this great station, which rises above every station beneath it?

The scholar said: My son, this is a station that God has honored above all other stations and degrees, so that you can reach it by nothing except virtuous work. If your soul does its work, and you seek that station with honest certainty and a heart both pure and awake, I have hope that you will realize in it what the guardians of God, the chosen and the righteous, have realized.

The boy said: And who may hope to attain the likeness of this station, to which God has raised His guardians?

The scholar said: Their God is your God. Between God and anyone else there is no kinship; nothing brings the creation near to Him but piety and righteous deeds. The knowledge by which Allah has raised His guardians — you stand now at its beginning, and if you act upon it you will reach its end. It is a debt binding upon God that He bring you to the ranks the righteous among them attained, and that He withhold from you nothing of what He gave them.

The boy said: Can you then help me toward that, by some inner meaning, as your bounty first came to me?

The scholar said: This is a station in which nothing avails but the work of its own people.

The boy said: How can that be, when you were able to help me before?

The scholar said: The sower is able to make the earth fertile, to sow it and to water it, but he cannot bring forth the plant or the sheath. A man is able to bring forth his seed whenever he wills, but he cannot fashion from it what he wills. So it grieves me, my son, that you should ask my help in a thing and I cannot help you to it. May Allah open your breast and light your heart with guidance. Hold fast to the fear of God, and do what is best, for Allah does not waste the reward of the one who does his work well. You are upon the path of salvation, the road of guidance, the way of the people of piety. So walk your road, whose beginning you already stand upon, and cling to the rope you have been called to, until you are guided by a rope from God to the rope of God, for that is the goal of all who seek.

The boy said: Is not the rope of Allah the Imam to whom you have called me?

The scholar said: He is that in what is apparent. He is your rope and the most trustworthy handhold, your hujja, and the door of your Imam.

The boy said: Then what is the rope of Allah, and what is the rope that comes from God?

The scholar said: That is the utmost reach of your guidance and the furthest bound of all the worlds.

The boy said: There is no power and no strength save in Allah, لا حول ولا قوة إلا بالله. He who has no guide has drowned in the depths of ignorance, just as he who has no ship has drowned in the seas of this world.

The scholar said: And what are the hawl and the quwwa which you have said cannot exist except by Allah?

The boy said: We mean that I have no device by which to draw a benefit to myself, and no power by which to ward off from myself a harm I detest, except by Allah.

The scholar said: Then why do you say, there is no hawl except in Allah, and not, there is no hila, no stratagem, except in Allah?

The boy said: It is narrated to us in our sciences that the hawl is the year, from the saying of Allah, حولين كاملين, two complete years, meaning two years. And it is narrated to us, as the common people also relate, that the quwwa is mules, from the saying of Allah, وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْكُنُوزِ مَا إِنَّ مَفَاتِحَهُ لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ, and We gave him of treasures whose very keys would weigh down a band of men endowed with strength. They said that the band was forty men and the strength was mules, and that the keys of his treasures were the load of forty mules.

The scholar said: So it is as though, by your saying there is no power and no strength save in Allah, you meant there is no year and no mule save by Allah. Is this, in your view, the interpretation of your words and their meaning?

The boy said: I have no other saying than this, and it is a feeble one that resembles nothing of the speech of wisdom. Indeed I suspect that the meaning within it is other than this, something stronger in the saying and more firmly fixed in the mind. Is there for these two words an inner interpretation other than this?

The scholar said: The creation has never uttered a word of truth without its having a root in the inner, nor a word of falsehood without its having once had a meaning; for when speech is turned away from its meaning it becomes an absurdity, and the absurdity, once displaced from its place, is altered, and truth, once altered, becomes falsehood. Rather it is a speech by which what comes after is grasped through analogy and forms, just as what came before was grasped through likeness and parable.

The boy said: You have spoken truly. So what is the meaning of these two words and their like?

The scholar said: As for the hawl, he is the speaking Imam of Allah and His truthful Caliph, the master of the twelve captains, just as the hawl, which is the year, is completed only by twelve months. The Imam was named hawl because he turns the speech of the Creator, in the subtlety of its essence, into the speech of the children of Adam, until it is understood and becomes apparent speech. The words of wisdom thereby take on a body and a frame, while the speech of the Creator carries within them the spirit of life and the light of salvation. The words of wisdom are ennobled above all other speech by the nobility of the Creator's speech that lies within them. So the Imam was named the hawl for this turning of speech, and he turns it only by the leave of Allah. And the quwwa is his hujja and his door, whom God has strengthened to bear the heavy weight of the utterance. He receives it from the Imam in its wholeness and parcels it out to the summoners in detail, each according to his station; so the hujja was named quwwa, and it is strengthened only by God. It is for this that the one who speaks says, there is no hawl and no quwwa save in Allah, meaning there is no Imam and no hujja save by Allah, and from Allah come their light and their support. The band, the ones endowed with strength, are the twelve hujjas. So too Joseph (ع) said to his father, يَا أَبَتِ إِنِّي رَأَيْتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ رَأَيْتُهُمْ لِي سَاجِدِينَ, O my father, I saw eleven planets and the sun and the moon; I saw them prostrating to me. By the sun he meant the Imam, by the moon his hujja and his door, and by the planets the completing of the twelve captains. And likewise the brothers of Joseph said, لَيُوسُفُ وَأَخُوهُ أَحَبُّ إِلَى أَبِينَا مِنَّا وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ, truly Joseph and his brother are dearer to our father than we are, though we are a band; meaning the twelve captains, and asking that a man from among them be chosen over the rest. In this, poverty of judgment concerning Joseph (ع) came over them, and the risings of envy toward him appeared in them when God favored him. Yet the command of God was fulfilled in Joseph, and the end of their affair was remorse and repentance before him.

The boy said: Praise be to God, who cleansed them of defilement and returned them to the resort of repentance and the ways of turning back. So what is the meaning of the king's saying, إِنِّي أَرَى سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ, I see seven fat cows being devoured by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others withered? What are they, and what is the vision that is seen in sleep?

The scholar said: As for the vision, it is like the outward work a man performs; it is as the mirage which, when he comes to it, he finds to be nothing. So too is the work of the outward without the inner: nothing of it is accepted, however much of it there may be. And so is the vision in sleep: nothing is realized from what is seen, however often it is seen. The king said, in the common people's telling, I see seven things turning upon seven. As for the seven fat cows, they are the causes of God that stand between Him and His creation. As for the seven lean ones, they are the seven speakers, driven by need to what comes from God at the hands of His seven causes. The seven green ears are causes between God and the legatees. And the seven withered ones are the legatees, driven to what comes from God at the hands of these seven causes. Blessed then be Allah, Lord of the worlds.

The boy said: And what is the likeness of this among us?

The scholar said: That lies in the foundation of Islam and its offspring, like a grain that put forth seven ears, meaning that from his loins came seven imams.

The boy said: How, then, does God forbid this world, when He says, فَلَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا, so let not the life of this world deceive you?

The scholar said: God has spoken truly. He did not say, let not this world deceive you; rather He said, let not the life of this world deceive you. Life carries several meanings: a life apparent in this world, whose end is annihilation, and the life of the Hereafter, whose end is permanence. He said: do not work for the perishing life, but work for the abiding life. This is His saying, يَا لَيْتَنِي قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِي, would that I had sent something ahead for my life, meaning, would that in the perishing life one had sent something ahead for the abiding life. And the two lives, in another sense, are a life through the outward knowledge and a life through the inward knowledge. The outward knowledge is the life of this world, and it is the lower knowledge; the inward knowledge is the life of the Hereafter. For this reason He said, لَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا, let not the life of this world deceive you, meaning, let not the outward of knowledge, and the acting upon it alone, deceive you. For He accepts from you nothing but the inner established together with the outward, and He does not accept the outward without the inner.

The boy said: Consider one who knows the inward knowledge but does not know the outward and does not act upon it. What is his standing among the people of religion?

The scholar said: A wretched standing. His inner will not rise up nor be made sound if he has abandoned a thing he was commanded to do and made it a shield for his inner, like the outward of fruits — for when the rind is peeled from them before their harvest they are spoiled and cannot afterward be set right; and like the body — for when its limbs are severed the soul is not sustained in it for a single hour. Such is the outward of religion: when its obligations are neglected, its inner cannot stand for its owner. Moreover, his leaving of the outward, without leave from the One who imposed it upon him, does not escape one of two things. If he left it out of incapacity, then he is the more incapable in the inner; and if he left it deliberately, out of ability, then he is disobedient to the One who commanded him. The disobedient is a transgressor, and the transgressor does not dwell beside the guardians of God; rather he is their enemy and they are his, because he has severed what God commanded to be joined.

The boy said: And one who knows the outward knowledge and acts upon it but does not know the inner — what is his standing among the people of religion?

The scholar said: An evil standing, for he is like a body that has been formed but into which no soul has been breathed; he is numbered among the dead, and the dead are the abodes of the disbelievers. As one who denies the signs of God, he is an enemy to religion and to its people.

The boy said: So I see that the outward is not made sound except by the inner, and the inner does not stand except by the outward; that this world is lawful only to the one who has known the Hereafter, which is its true life and its inner; and likewise that religion is not made whole for its people until they establish both its outward and its inner.

The scholar said: Yes, this is the meaning, and upon this the work is to be done. For the fulfilling of all that God has commanded is a claim upon His good pleasure, and the abandoning of any of what God has commanded is an exposing of oneself to His wrath.

The boy said: Is not the one who knows the outward together with the inner, and establishes them both, a believer?

The scholar said: Yes, he is a believer in truth.

The boy said: And is it not so that the one who knows and then works for the Hereafter finds this world made wholesome for him, its good things lawful, and the turning about among its gains permitted?

The scholar said: Yes. This world is made wholesome for none but them; they are the most deserving of it and the most entitled to it.

The boy said: Then why is it that I see the people of this description, who are the scholars and the folk of this affair, having cast off the world and all that is in it, until the ignorant among the people suppose it is forbidden to them? They turn away from this world; they look upon it only with a sidelong glance, and their souls are heavy toward it. And I see their colors gone sallow though no illness is upon them, their bodies weak, their skins clinging to their bones, as though they do not eat food, even while they eat.

The narrator said: When the scholar heard the mention of his brethren and this description of them, he was choked with weeping and his eyes overflowed with tears, so that for a time he could not speak from the intensity of it. And the boy wept at his weeping, until the scholar feared that some of their enemies might prevail against them. So he held himself firm and his heart grew settled within him, and then the scholar turned toward the boy.

The scholar said: O my son, the people you have described in this way have looked upon themselves by the light of the true life and busied themselves in their affair with the certainty of salvation, and they have not dealt harshly with themselves. O my son, this world is seen by wealth and children, and the Hereafter is seen by knowledge and certainty. O my son, the faith of these people in the unseen is sound; they held the promise and the warning to be true, so they yearned toward what they were promised and sought it, and the fear of the threat filled them with dread, so they strove; they feared the stupor of death, so they hastened to good deeds. Their bodies grew weak from the persistence of fasting, and their colors turned sallow from the length of their vigils. They do not sleep when the sleepers sleep, out of dread, nor do they eat their sustenance except grudgingly. The longing of contemplation has turned them from the pleasure of ease, and the sweetness of remembrance has held them back from the respite of sleep. Their souls have been drawn away from the appetites, so this world was not made pleasant for them; they remember death often, so life was not made sweet to them; and they have beheld the bounties of the Hereafter with certainty, so they see this world only as a passing fragment. Thus they cast off the world in their preoccupation away from it, without holding it forbidden.

The boy said: This is the sound account, and it was in this sense that the scholars disdained this world, while the people are heedless of them, supposing that they have forbidden to themselves what God has made lawful for them. Now describe to me the delusion of the people of the world in their world, and their heedlessness of the Hereafter.

The scholar said: If those enamored of this world would only reflect upon their own creation and the turning of their states — from weakness to strength and from strength to weakness, from ease to ruin, and from life to death — and if they would then look upon those who passed before them of the vanished generations, among whom were the good and the wicked, and how all of them went down into one plain of earth, and how they saw no reward for the good in this world nor any punishment for the wicked in this world, they would know that they were created for other than this world. But earning weighed heavy upon them, and they would not carry upon themselves the burden of the seeking. So they seized the fleeting thing of this lower life and were content with the ease of this world in place of the Hereafter. This is their habit and this is their meaning. They have no argument before God and no honor before His guardians, and there has already come to them of the tidings that wherein is deterrence.

## 7. The Five Shares and the Long Road to the Shaykh

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has guided us to His religion, for we could never have found guidance except by His favor. Tell me about the beginning of this creation and how it is composed. Is it a single creation or many? For I see how greatly people differ in their opinions and their passions."

The scholar said: "As for the creation of bodies, it is varied, as you can see in the variety of tongues and colors. This variety is itself a proof of God's power, made plain through His signs, so that people may recognize one another: the child knows his father and the father his son, the man knows his wife and no other, and the woman knows her husband and no other. Thus did God create them in His justice. But as for the creation of the instrument — that is, the senses, both those visible and those hidden within — it is a single creation, and what is apparent will tell you the truth of what is concealed. Have you ever seen a learned man given a third eye as a bonus, or an ignorant man left with only one eye as a defect, so that one might say the scholar sees by his surplus and the ignorant man fails to see for his lack? Have you seen any part of the outward instrument given to one man over another? No — you see their creation as one and the same in the outward instrument, and the meaning of the composition is identical in all. From this we come to know that the inner senses follow the same pattern of justice. God would not be just in what is apparent and unjust in what is hidden. He did not fashion two hearts in one breast for the scholar, nor did He deprive the ignorant man of anything. The instrument is one, and the composition belongs to the One who assembled it. By this very instrument, which He made for them and which they failed to use, God has raised His argument against them. The Almighty said: They have hearts wherewith they do not understand, and they have eyes wherewith they do not see, and they have ears wherewith they do not hear. Do you suppose, then, that God, in His bounty and generosity, charged them with what they had no power to bear? No. He is far fairer than that."

The boy said: "I see it no other way, nor would I say otherwise. But I ask you about the difference of passions. Where did it come from, when the instrument is whole and the origin of the composition is one?"

The scholar said: "As for the origin, it is one. All of them descend from Adam, and Adam is from dust, created by his Lord, who breathed into him of His spirit. But they departed from the Sunna in their marriages. They were born on a footing other than the true faith and grew up in something other than guidance. I do not doubt that among them are children of unlawful union who claim as fathers men who are not their fathers. So when the roots were corrupted, the branches grew corrupt and distorted, and their return to the truth became hard — weighed down as they were by arrogance against obedience, sloth in worship, and envy of the friends of God. So they did not keep within the bounds of the lawful, nor hold back from the forbidden."

The boy said: "If the origin is one and the instrument is one, then where does the difference of passions arise, when the aim of their seeking is also one — since everyone seeks the good for himself?"

The scholar said: "By my life, the aim of their seeking is indeed one, but they differ in striking the mark, like archers aiming at a target. They agree in shooting at it with resolve and with the bow, yet for all that they differ in whether they hit it. In the same way, the hearts within the breasts are gathered upon the minds, and the minds are gathered upon the safeguarding of the bodies and the securing of their dignity. But when the call of truth appeared, they parted ways. Some held that answering the Messengers and humbling themselves before them was itself a part of one's dignity and a guarding of the self against folly. Others held that denying the Messengers and raising oneself above obedience to them was a part of one's dignity and a guarding of the self against the narrowness of worship. The root in both was the same: the seeking of ease for the body and honor for the soul. So one group chose what the Messengers had promised them of the reward of God, and though in this world that reward was rare and costly, they chose it for its permanence. The others chose the ease of this world, and though it was perishing, they chose it for its nearness, for how easy it is to ask, and for how swiftly its reward comes. The very hearts by which the first sought the bliss of the Hereafter were the same in kind as the hearts by which the second sought the bliss of this world. Each man looks only to himself, and each supposes himself his own true counselor. So they came together in the seeking and differed in the striking. This is the justice of God, His generosity, and the openness of His giving: to every seeker is given what he sought. Whoever desires the reward of this world is given from it, and whoever desires the reward of the Hereafter is given from it."

The boy said: "But the people of this world are ranked in classes within it. Some are rich beyond any need to labor, kings for the abundance of their wealth; others are poor and in need of wealth, serving the rich to gain from them. Then the bliss of this world gathers them all onto one footing. Are they all alike in the evil of their end, or is there a difference between one and another?"

The scholar said: "It is part of God's justice that He joins them one to another and does not cut them off from their companions and those they loved. They are equal in His punishment, just as they were joined together in disobeying Him."

The boy said: "And what of this religion, which God has honored and perfected, which He has accepted as a religion for Himself and made binding upon His servants? Will He accept a part of it from them without the rest, so that they fall into classes as the people of this world do? Or will He accept it only from one who brings it whole?"

The scholar said: "If the people of this world are ranked in classes within it, then the people of religion are ranked in degrees within their religion. Justice requires that the Creator be worshipped to the full measure of His will, for great is His wealth and great is their need of Him. Yet His mercy widened toward them. He knew that among them were the weak, so He accepted from them whatever they could manage of sincere striving in work and effort spent in obedience, whether little or much. Then He charged each soul according to its capacity and its labor, and gave every possessor of merit his due merit, so that they stand in degrees before their Lord. Then, by His justice, He made the outcome of their obedience to be this: that they be joined to the people of complete religion and excellent works among His servants. The Almighty said: And whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger, those will be with the ones upon whom Allah has bestowed favor: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous. What excellent companions they are. That is the bounty from Allah. This they attain by the effort they poured into obedience — not because their deeds equal the deeds of the righteous, but as a mercy from God toward them, even though they are weak and their works are slight."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has done this for them. Is it to be feared, then, that anyone should be punished for anything other than disobedience, even when his work is little?"

The scholar said: "God is more just than that."

The boy said: "Then is it to be hoped that anyone might enter Paradise without works at all, such as the insane and small children?"

The scholar said: "That falls within the grace of God, His generosity, and the breadth of His mercy."

The boy said: "What, then, is the limit of striving in obedience and the measure of sincere work, beyond which God is bound to accept a man's excuse for all that lies past it?"

The scholar said: "The full reach of his knowledge and the utmost of his strength."

The boy said: "Then describe for me the duty of knowledge and the duty of action, and the goal of each, that I may measure myself against them. If I find I can reach the goal in seeking my Lord's pleasure, I will press on. If not, I will halt at the limit of my strength, and God is the first to excuse me for what I cannot do. I place my trust in the justice of God and feel safe from any wrong on His part, but I fear the evil within my own soul."

The scholar said: "Do not feel fear where you have come to feel safe, and do not feel safe where you ought still to fear. Strive for God with your person and with your wealth. For God does not accept what He hates, nor does He hate what pleases Him. He has hated unbelief for His creation and is pleased when His servants give thanks and act with justice in what He has decreed, so He accepts nothing else. Truthfulness and faithfulness are the two gardens of His Paradise, and none is saved with Him except the people of truthfulness and faithfulness."

The boy said: "Then make plain to me the goal of the work within my own self, and what God has made binding upon me in it."

The scholar said: "Obedience to God ennobles those who render it, even when their origins are humble, and good work is the crown of those who do it. As for what is binding upon you in your own self, it is to perform every obligation and to shun every forbidden thing, for this is the very substance of your religion and the purity of your body. And the whole of what makes God pleased with you and wins His acceptance is this: that you extend to others what you would love to have extended to you, and that you withhold from others what you would love to have withheld from yourself. In this lies justice, and God is pleased with justice."

The boy said: "Yes, this is the binding truth, and whoever does not act by it has no share with God. Is there, then, any allowance beyond what you have mentioned, in a time of dire need?"

The scholar said: "God has opened that door for His servants, out of His tenderness and mercy. He said: Whoever is compelled, without willful transgression, there is no sin upon him."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has laid upon His servants no hardship in religion. I am resolved to hold fast to what He has commanded, and I seek His help in doing as He has ordered me. But what is my duty regarding my wealth, which you joined to my own person when you said I must strive with my person and my wealth?"

The scholar said: "As for your wealth, it is everything God has granted you of every kind of gain. Take it only from what is lawful, and spend it only in its rightful place."

The boy said: "And what is the lawful source from which it is taken, and what is the rightful place in which it is spent?"

The scholar said: "Its lawful source is honest earning by which you have wronged no one and which you have not tainted with falsehood. As for the rightful claim upon it, that is its zakat: look at all that has come into your hands, whether little or much, take out a fifth of its finest — the portion you yourself would most love to receive — and give it to those whose claim upon you is binding. In this way your wealth is purified by charity, just as your body is purified by work. Work is a shield, and zakat is a cleansing, and whoever gives the zakat has truly prospered."

The boy said: "I will do so, and I repent to God for what has passed. Then what am I to do with the remainder of the wealth?"

The scholar said: "You shall spend upon yourself and your family in a fitting manner. If your surplus still exceeds your need, share it with the poor among your brethren and extend your kindness to everyone who turns to you in want, for the one who has no bounty of his own is in need of another's bounty toward him. So be swift to every good and foremost in every act of generosity, for this is the very goal of the work, if only you can be patient in it."

The boy said: "Yes — patience, and then more patience. How could a man in great need fail to be patient toward one who has no need of his service? Every needy person is patient, and every one who has been favored gives thanks."

The scholar, delighted by the boy's words, said: "Praise be to God, who has guided you to speak rightly. I ask Him to help you carry the work through to completion, for that is the perfection of virtue."

The boy said: "For a long time I had been asking another man's hand for the credit of a favor I ought to have done for myself. Now that the credit for it has come to rest with me, will I deny it to my own soul? I can find no one who would cheat my soul more than I would if I did. So here is the whole of my wealth before you, in five shares. One share is its zakat. One share is the expiation for what I have squandered. One share is thanksgiving to God for what I have come to know. One share is for the poor among my brethren. And one share is for you to accept by your grace, that it may become provision for you. Take all of it for yourself, and God will send its like in its place."

The narrator said: The scholar's heart was moved for the boy when he saw how the jewel within him had come clear, how the ordeal had drawn out a praiseworthy result, how his merit had shone and his work had been purified.

The scholar said, his tears running down: "O my son, for the sake of one like you let the scholars travel the long road; for the sake of one like you let the earth stand firm and the heavens hold steady; for the sake of one like you let the bonds of kinship be made plain, and let the rain come down from the openings of the clouds. So rejoice, my son, in what God has given you and in the breast He has opened to Islam. Receive the glad tidings of a light from your Lord and a nearby opening, and accept God's gift to you and the benefits of His grace upon you with abundant work and noble thanks. As for my share of this wealth, I have no need of it. I have come out from wealth, and it is the temptation of wealth that I fled. It would not be right, my son, that you should turn me back to the very thing I have come to detest. As for your brethren's share, keep it for them, so that when they ask you for something you may hand it to them by your own hand, that they may know your kindness and thank God for your goodness. As for the share of expiation, hold it with you until you come to know your sin. As for the share of thanksgiving, it is a trust you hold for God until you reach your maturity and rise to the highest of what you have come to know. And as for the share of zakat, it too is a trust for the one to whom it is owed, held in your keeping. Take all of it for yourself until you meet him, for he is the one who judges for you and against you."

The boy said: "And who is this generous one set over you, whose judgment you do not overstep and whose command you do not transgress?"

The scholar said: "He is the one whose right God has made binding upon me and upon every believer, the one in whose hand are the keys of the gardens and the landmarks of the kingdom, the one whose palms are opened —

The scholar went on speaking of the great one to whom he would bring him: "With the light of the Mount, he is the cause behind the signs, the one whose knowledge has raised him to the loftiest of ends. Through him your light is completed, through him Allah perfects your affair, and at his hand the blessings come to you."

The boy said: "Such kindness as this — to whom does it belong, and who are you yourself?"

The scholar said: "I am his son and one of his servants in good works, seeking his pleasure and shrinking from his displeasure."

The boy said: "I think myself far from his good pleasure, yet near enough that, had I never known of him, I would have an excuse. Now that I know him, is any excuse left to me?"

The scholar said: "An excuse is granted only before one is brought within reach of such knowledge."

The boy said: "Shall I speak, or shall I hold back?"

The scholar said: "Say what seems best to you."

The boy said: "Then should you not, at this very moment, take me by the hand to the gate of remission until I enter, and to the house of light until I draw from it? This is part of the full measure of truthfulness that is binding upon you."

The scholar said: "That is indeed your due — but only with his permission and by his decision."

Then the scholar took his leave of the boy and departed. The boy wished to furnish him with provision for the road, but the scholar was loath to accept it. He went on until he came to his elder father, and described the boy to him, and the father recognized the mark upon him and knew it for the mark of the people of goodness and the mines of virtue, and he marveled at it.

He said: "Bring this boy to me quickly, for I hope he will be a door of mercy that Allah has opened for the people of his age."

The scholar tarried only a little before returning to the boy, and he found him changed in body from the state he had known.

The scholar said: "What is this I see, so unlike the state in which I left you?"

The boy said: "And what have you found changed in me — may my father and mother be your ransom?"

The scholar said: "Weakness of body and a yellowness of color."

The boy said: "That is from a desolation that came over me after you left."

The scholar said: "And what is this desolation, and what is the cause of this weakness?"

The boy said: "Your absence made me desolate, and dread of what might befall me before I met you again made me weak."

The scholar said: "Allah has known what is in your heart, and so He has made the trial light upon you and has cast mercy for you into the heart of the one who has charge of you. So take up your provisions for the journey, for I will carry you to the fullness of your hope and bring you to the door of your goal. And there is no power except by Allah."

At this the boy was so overcome with joy and awe that his soul all but parted from his body, until Allah, knowing what was in him, took hold of it and returned it to him. When he revived he said: "Praise be to Allah, who has never ceased to be merciful to the believers."

Then he gave abundant thanks to his father and bent to kiss his hands, and his feet as well. He rose in haste, took up the gear of travel, and the two set out until they reached the greater scholar. They asked leave and were admitted; they entered, greeted him, and sat by his leave. When the assembly had settled around them, the boy said to his teacher: "Teach me how to speak and how to ask for my need."

The teacher said to him: "It is not lawful for me to teach you anything while you are in this place, out of reverence for its master. You stand before one who knows your need and knows what you intend."

So the boy held his peace, and the two remained with the scholar a while, until his affair was completed and his guidance perfected, until he reached the utmost of his hope and knew what was for him and what was against him. Then they bade farewell and went out.

The two withdrew together, unseen. In the assembly they had spoken nothing but words of peace and glad greeting, and the boy had turned pale from the intensity of his awe and could not speak. The master, the lord of the house, gave orders that the two be treated with honor and that whatever they needed be provided; and he went on with them to his own house. When he had brought them in, greeted them, and shown his gladness at their coming, he said to the scholar — who was his friend, and between whom there was brotherhood and long acquaintance: "My brother, is this the boy of whom we used to hear?"

He said: "Yes. This is the one whose report has grown sweet in the hearts, so that his remembrance is upon the tongues of the believers."

The boy said: "Has word of me truly reached the friends of Allah, so that they recount my mention in their gatherings?"

The scholar said: "Yes, my brother. So you are."

The boy said: "Favors have surrounded me from every side, and bounties have come upon me from every path. I do not know whom to thank, nor whom to repay."

Then they all sat and talked through their night. When morning came they sought leave to enter upon the Shaykh. When they were admitted and had completed their greeting, the Shaykh gave them leave to sit, and they sat; and the Shaykh took up his speech.

He said: "Praise be to Allah, who has opened the sealed hearts by His light and unlocked by His justice what was closed to the seeker; who has made remembrance easy by His gentle making, as a kindness to His servants and a mercy from Him to those who long for Him. He has not charged His creation with the full reality of what is owed to Him, nor has He been content that they deny His blessings; rather He has charged them, out of His justice, with what they are not too weak to bear, so that the matter is set in balance between two extremes, and is therefore named justice. He has spread out His hands with generosity, in kindness and in bounty, disappointing none who call upon Him and denying none who keep silent. His blessings pour down and His favors are unstinting — neither reserved for the one who gives thanks, for the greatness of His reward, nor withheld from the one who is ungrateful, for the greatness of His punishment. He has kept His blessings too noble for any stinting, and made His proofs too sure to be denied. He is too encompassing to be contained, too evident in His signs to be rejected. He is found yet not bounded, known yet not described. Glory be to Him: how mighty, and how gentle to His creation; how compelling, and how merciful to His servants; how mighty in power, and how just in His ruling; how exalted, and how near to His people; how near, and how far above all imaginings. So worship Him and give thanks to Him; to Him you shall return."

Then he said: "As for what follows: it is part of the honor of minds that they be put to work in seeking; and among the fruits of seeking is the finding of what truly is; and among the marks of what truly is, is its sweetness. Every sweet and flowing spring is a drink whose surface is there to be drunk and whose depth is veiled; the one who seeks it never wearies of thought, and creation never reaches the end of it. Its landmarks are a right, and the right of the Real is a duty — and it is for this that it is called a right, for the establishing of its obligation. Whoever denies a right its due is a wrongdoer. Is it not among the rights of thought that the hearts pay their alms through good manners; and among the rights of good manners, the seeking of knowledge; and among the rights of knowledge, that it be acted upon; and among the rights of acting upon it, that it be made pure through obedience to those in authority; and among the rights of obedience, that it be perfected in hardship and in ease alike, in good fortune and in trial the same. And none is granted this save those who are patient, and none is granted it save one of a great share."

Then he turned his face toward the boy and said: "O young man, you are honored as a dear friend newly come, and greeted as a guest with a purpose. What is your name?"

He said: "Ubayd Allah, son of Abd Allah."

The Shaykh said: "That is your description, and word of you has already come to us."

The boy said: "I am a free man, son of Abd Allah."

The Shaykh said: "And who freed you from ownership, that you became free?"

The boy said: "This scholar freed me," and he pointed with his hand to the scholar who had brought him.

The Shaykh said: "Tell me: if this man is himself owned and possesses nothing, is his freeing of you valid for you?"

The boy said: "It is not valid."

The Shaykh said: "Then what is your name?"

At this the boy fell silent, at a loss for an answer.

The Shaykh said: "O young man, how is a thing to be known that has no name?"

The boy said: "Then I am newborn to you; so give me a name."

The Shaykh said: "That waits upon the completing of seven days."

The boy said: "And why is it deferred until seven days are complete?"

The Shaykh said: "For the honoring of the newborn."

The boy said: "And if the newborn should die before the seven days are complete?"

The Shaykh said: "Nothing harms him; he is named even after that."

The boy said: "And this name by which you would name me — is it mine to own?"

The Shaykh said: "Then you would be one who is worshipped, though you were but newly born?"

The boy said: "How, then, is it to be spoken of?"

The Shaykh said: "The name is master over you, and you are owned by the name. So do not press beyond your bound, and withdraw until your term is complete."

At this the boy rose in haste in the beauty of his obedience, and did not answer back, in the beauty of his manners. He went out, and his father went out with him, until they returned to their lodging. The lord of the house greeted them and said: "How fares this brother of ours in his need?"

The scholar said to him: "A promise has been given; the one who was promised is thirsting, and the term is near."

So they waited until the seventh day, then sought leave of the Shaykh. When he had granted them leave, he ordered the boy to wash and to put on his cleanest garments. As the boy set about his preparation, his heart sensed that his need would be granted that day, and that this preparation he had been ordered to make pointed to a good that would follow it. When he had done what he was ordered and the two went out together, the boy went out saying: "A day of good fortune, and a patron of good fortune." He repeated it again and again, moved to it by nothing but the fullness of his joy and the keenness of his longing to know what would bring him nearer to the pleasure of Allah. When the two came in upon the Shaykh, they found that he had taken up the implements of his work. He greeted them with peace, and they returned his greeting.

Then he bade the boy draw near to him, and the boy drew near; and he brought him close, and he came close. And he took up speech such as no fancy has ever encompassed, that no pen has ever traced, that has never crossed the heart of any human being — speech that has no place in the schools of the preachers and is not laid out in the compendiums of books, for the greatness of its worth, and is unveiled to none but its own people. And when he had discharged what was due and brought him out of the wildernesses of bewilderment that had led him astray, to the very end of guidance, his path was made pure and his draught made sweet, and he came to know his Lord. So he released his ihram by magnifying the sanctities of Allah, held fast to the sure rope, circled the Ancient House, spent his rites in remembrance, and completed his Hajj by the greatest sign.

Then he and his father continued to come and go to the Shaykh, studying what he had given, the boy patient in his perseverance, never wearying of the question for its nobility, never sated with the answer for its sweetness. So they remained in that state for a long while, until it was known that the root had drunk deep and the branch had risen high.

Then he said to the Shaykh, in thanks to Allah and to him: "Praise be to Allah, who has made you worthy of this station, and revived through you a faith and put to death through you an ignorance, and made you a place of return for the people and a sanctuary. You are the gate of the people of heaven and the ascent of the people of earth. From the light of your might the lights appeared, and from the pouring of your palm the rivers ran. May you remain so, until the one who is pleased with you grants you his pleasure."

The Shaykh said: "As for you, may Allah bless you in what He has given you, and aid you by His enabling grace to guard what has come to you, and set you upright in His will, and smooth your way by the light of His guidance, and open your breast with the keys of wisdom, and ennoble your affair with the path of the call, and perfect your merit with the confirmations of answered prayer, and bring you, in obedience to Him, to the best of your hope of His good pleasure. And now I take my leave of you, my son, and give you leave to depart. Hard it is upon me to part from you, but three things have driven us to it. The first is that we are bound on a journey to another land. The second is your long separation from your family, together with the burden you took on in going out without your father's permission, for in him there is a heavy grief and a great anger that nothing will lift but your return — though I hold out hope for your father, if you are gentle with him. And the third is the duty owed for the right of this grace that has reached you, for it is binding upon you to live by it and to call others to it."

The boy said: "Your parting is a sorrow to me, and your loss a lack in me; but your counsel is more rightly guiding, your obedience more binding, and your judgment more sound. So enjoin upon me what you see fit, and dismiss me when you will, and be gracious to me by sending my father with me to my land, that I may lean upon him in my work and entrust all my affairs to him."

The Shaykh said: "That I shall do. And I enjoin upon you the fear of Allah who created you, the keeping of the trust for which you have struggled, and the safeguarding of your father, who raised you when you were small and toiled long in your affair, with his own person and with his wealth. Do not clothe him in the garments of disdain, nor set him upon the vessels of ruin; do not blot out his name, nor turn your speech away from his mention, nor deal falsely with him in your city; and in the beginning there is room enough for you."

The boy said: "May Allah reward you, as a lord of grace, on behalf of every grateful one, with good; and may the Lord of bounty repay you for your shepherding, when hands are too weak to repay you. I will keep your commandment. So do not cut off my share from the blessing of your supplication."

Then the two rose, clasped hands, and embraced, and each bade the other farewell, unable to master his tears and unable to speak but by signs. Then they parted, and the scholar and the boy journeyed on until they drew near to the boy's city, in which his father was.

The scholar said to the boy: "My son, you have learned the counsel of the Shaykh, and there is no right guidance but in his word. This is your city, and we have reached its edge; so sit with me apart from the road, for I wish to remind you of something of my own affair and to enjoin upon you what you should do."

When the two had sat, the scholar said: "My son, you know the state of your father and his enmity toward the people of this matter, and this has been compounded upon him by your going out with me and your absence from him without his leave or his assurance. I mean to carry out my own father's counsel by keeping myself hidden in some part of this wilderness, while you return to your father, that his soul may be soothed and his fury toward us may subside. And I hope, if you are gentle with him, that he will be nearer to what you desire than any other. The absence has alarmed him, and the solitude has distressed him."

When the time came to part, the two of them rose, shook hands, and embraced, and each gave his farewell to his friend. Neither could contain himself for the weight of the parting, and neither could speak except by gesture. Then they took their separate ways.

So the scholar and the boy went out together, and when they had drawn near to the boy's city — the city in which his father lived — the scholar said to him: "O my son, I have understood the will of the Shaykh, and you have not, for guidance lies only in his words. This is your city, and we have reached its edge. Sit with me here, apart from the road, for I wish to remind you of some of my concerns and to counsel you in what you should do."

When they had sat down, the scholar said: "O my son, I know your father and his enmity toward the people of this matter. To that has now been added your going out with me and your absence from him without his leave or his trust. I wish to honor your father's view by keeping some of these affairs concealed, so that you may return to him, and his heart may be set at ease, and he may remain among us. And I hope, if you are gentle with him, that he will be nearer to what you desire than anyone else, for your absence has worn him down and his solitude has come to frighten him. Know, my son, that Allah has opened to you the least of your striving — a thing I did not think you would reach until a long stretch of time had passed and you supposed yourself a grown man. Allah has been good to you, so be good to yourself, and honor the one through whom Allah has honored you. We have thought well of you, and our hope in you has been great. Hold fast, then, to the trust your father entrusted to you, and be firm in your affair. Piety and watchful devotion are the guardians of your religion and your work; thanksgiving and patience are an increase to your light. Ask of Allah the opening of your provision and the goodness of His success granted to you."

Then the scholar said: "I commend to you six qualities: that you rely upon Allah when you are resolved; that you speak with a ruling when you are called upon; that you do not grow angry when you are wronged; that you do not weary when you are slandered; that you bear patiently with everyone who answers you toward the good; and that you take for yourself the best of that to which you call others. For Allah will help you and open the way before you."

The boy said: "May Allah reward you with three goods: an imam whose argument you have upheld, a proof of God whose trust you have counseled, and a dead man whose life you have graced. My heart is filled and my breast is brimming with light and gladness at the greatness of what you have set forth. I do not know which of your words is the finer: the outward, which you arranged in its order, or the inward, which you unfolded in its meanings."

The scholar said: "Praise be to Allah, who has made His way easy for you, brought its far distance near, and helped you to bear its weight. Now I take my leave of you and turn away. Is there any need?"

The boy said: "My needs rest upon three: a need already fulfilled, a need for which I hope, and a need I ask you to grant me as a favor."

The scholar said: "I have understood your three needs, and I answer you in what you have asked. As for the need that has passed, it is your life in knowledge after death. May Allah bless you with that life and bring you in it to the best of what you intended. As for the need for which you hope, it is to act upon what you have learned and to have it accepted when you are brought forth; may Allah bring that about for you by His mercy. As for the need you ask of me, it is as though you wished for an increase upon what you have heard."

The boy said: "There is no increase greater than your knowing what is within me. So grant me what you see fit."

The scholar said: "As for my knowing what is within you, none knows the unseen but Allah; yet the believer sees by the light of Allah. As for the increase, I will make its matter plain to you and set you upon the foundations by which you are to work. Look upon all those things whose outward form I have described to you, which you have taken as parables. Whatever among them is praiseworthy is a likeness of the truth and its people — its best for their best, and its signs point to them. And whatever among the parables is blameworthy, I have turned it upon the enemies of the truth and its people — its great ones for their great ones and its small ones for their small ones, and its signs point to them. These are the foundations of the knowledge upon which all practice turns; if you preserve them, you will have no need of any other. And I commend to you the fear of the One who created you when you were nothing, the keeping of His covenant, and the fulfillment of the right He has imposed upon you and upon all His creation."

When he had finished his discourse, brought the boy to his place of safety, and completed his nurture of him, he said: "O my son, take care of yourself, for I have a concern besides you, and this is the parting between me and you."

At the mention of parting the boy gave such a gasp that he almost destroyed himself; his spirit nearly took flight, and his soul nearly left his body. Then he bent to kiss the scholar's feet and wiped their dust upon his cheeks, and he bade him farewell and departed. The boy returned to his house with his heart taken from him in grief, unable to hold back his weeping in sorrow at parting from his elder father — the father who had been the cause of his blessing — until his family and his neighbors grew afraid for him. He passed his day and his night restless with sorrow and alarm. Then his father came in upon him, filled with anger.

## 8. Homecoming, the Father, and the Debate with Abu Malik

His father said: "O my son, is this how sons repay their fathers? I taught you nothing but the best a father can teach a child, and never once did you deny yourself or renounce your beliefs — not until I had grown old and this strange man arrived. You drew him in upon me, you went over to his cause alongside him, and you never once consulted your parents. If what I believed was true, then you have wronged me in my very person, for you hid the truth from me. And if it was false, then you have disobeyed me straight into your own ruin — and whatever has overtaken you has, through you, overtaken me as well."

The boy said: "O my father, I have never known you to be anything but merciful and tender toward me. You raised me as an act of mercy, you nourished me, and you chose me over both family and wealth. My greatest need of you today is that you grant me, as a kindness, one of two things."

The Shaykh said: "Name them — what are the two?"

The boy said: "Either leave me to my courtesy and my good conduct toward you, for I am your son whom you raised from childhood, and our affair rests with Allah; or grant me leave to debate with you, assure me I am safe from your anger, and bring your mind to bear on it."

The Shaykh said: "As for outward courtesy while our hearts differ within — that is a standing reproach between us. How long can outward agreement hold when the inward is at odds? I want none of it. But your debate and your candor are yours to have, so say whatever seems best to you."

The boy said: "Consider that I have stood at your side for forty years upon nothing at all. Does it not come down to one of two things? Either you are a scholar who withheld his knowledge from me — and then do not blame me if, when you forbid me, I seek my own salvation through another. Or you are ignorant — and in that you are excused before me, yet it was you who needed this stranger more than I did. If I reached him before you, it was only because of your age and the lateness of your birth, for I have heard his words exactly as you have heard them, in all that I have seen."

The boy's words tore at the Shaykh's heart. He knew there was no escaping either horn of the argument, and his eyes brimmed over with tears. He said: "O my son, the argument that has fallen upon me for your sake falls upon you for mine in equal measure, if you keep your affair hidden from me. So lay it out for me. If it is right, I will accept it from you and count it an honor to myself; and if it is false, I will turn you back from it, as an honor to you and out of compassion for you."

So they debated for a time and opened their hearts to each other at length, and their affair came to rest upon guidance and right direction: to uphold the religion of Allah, to guard it, to hold fast to it, to call others to it, and to help one another in it. Through the two of them Allah gave life to many of His creatures. The boy sent word to the scholar with the glad news of his father's uprightness and asked him to come to them; and he came, and was a source of good for them both. Through them and at their hands Allah caused blessings to flow over the people of that land. The command of Allah was fulfilled, and the slanderer was left disappointed.

The boy's name was Salih, and his father's was al-Bakhtari. The two of them held a place of honor among their people, won by the favors they had extended and the high name their fine manners had earned them. Word of them spread through the land — and of the religion they now held — until it reached a people who clung to their old creed. Al-Bakhtari had long paid them a standing bounty, and they feared that his leaving their religion would cut it off. So they set out and gathered before a scholar of theirs called Abd al-Jabbar, Abu Malik, whom they honored for his piety, his abundant forbearance, and his defense of his religion, and whom they named their "كعب الأحبار." Among them he stood out for his learning, his grasp of discourse, and his discernment of opinions. When they came in to him they said: "O Abu Malik, do you not see what the boy has gone over to?"

He said: "Which boy?"

They said: "Salih ibn al-Bakhtari."

He said: "And what has he gone over to?"

They said: "We are telling you that a strange man came to our land, with fine speech and a doctrine he calls people to, and we do not know what it is. The boy took to him and left with him without his father's leave, and was gone for a while. Then he came back speaking the stranger's words and calling to what he calls to. We have heard that his father too has come round to his view and backed him in it. This has grieved us — both what they have done, and our own holding back from them — and for three reasons. The first: the bounty that used to flow to us from his father, together with that brotherhood in religion you know of between us, so that we fear estrangement after intimacy and dread division after brotherhood. The second: if these people are upon the truth, then we have fallen short in following them in it and helping them upon it. The third: if they are upon some error into which they have strayed, then we have fallen short in driving them out of it and in aiding the truth and its people. You are a head of our religion and a standard among our standards; we have come to inform you and to seek your counsel, and we will not go beyond your opinion."

Abu Malik said: "Gently now. This word of yours has alarmed me. You know that al-Bakhtari and his son were among those we always reckoned people of forbearance and piety, and that they hold rights and old favors over us. You have said of them what you have said, and you are, in my judgment, people who do not wander from the truth to anything else. This is a report that has come to a people, its argument laid plain before them, so that they have followed it and are content with it. We cannot judge them to be in error and so wrong them; nor judge them to be rightly guided and so wrong ourselves by imitating them; nor say that they were deceived and refute them without knowledge — for in our eyes they are too noble for that. What we must do is look into this report with the eye of fairness, the light of justice, and a steady mind. For it is said: whoever is deliberate in his affairs keeps his authority secure, and whoever is hasty in his affair is never safe from a slip of the foot. A tear in any ordinary thing has its fault mended and its loss made good — like a tear in food, whose harm is slight and whose remedy is near at hand. But a tear in religion is a grave harm and a grievous loss: its fault cannot be mended nor another put in its place — like a tear in the very medicine, which neither releases the one who takes it nor lets him save himself. In this we have been taught that the best of all stores are the treasures of sound minds and the acquiring of good manners, for their fruits come to a praiseworthy end. And among those fruits is following the way of forbearance — and forbearance is the crown of the learned. By it, noble aspirations raise them to the heights of glory, their merit is made plain wherever they are, and with it comes the honor of winning good things with ease of pursuit. We ourselves have laid up some measure of that: a portion of forbearance we took in, and something of manners we acquired in days gone by, amid straitened means and hard striving. We bore it patiently for the sake of its worth, and we look to it for help against the calamities of affairs and the accidents of the age. We cannot let it go to waste, and this is the very hour of our need for it, the hour to put it to use in this new matter that has come upon us among our brothers. There is no sense in leaving them, and what they hold, without laying it bare together with them and debating it with them — with the forbearance of the capable and the understanding of seekers — neither doubting what we ourselves hold, nor charging these people with having turned from the truth in aversion (for they are, in our eyes, too noble for that), nor with having gone out into error (for error is beneath them). Falsehood has no eminence for which plain truth should be abandoned. But reflect with me on the case of these people, for it cannot fall outside three possibilities. Either they are upon the same religion we know, and this new thing among them is merely an addition of manners, sayings, and poetry — and people say a great deal. Or a people has come to them with something added onto the religion they already hold, and they have welcomed it — for knowledge is the lost property of every believer, and more knowledge is better than a lack of it. Or a guidance other than what they had has come to them, from the prophethood of a prophet raised up among them who called them to it, and they followed it upon clear proof — and that is not to be denied of the command of Allah. Creation will never exhaust the utmost of Allah's decree for them and His will concerning them, however hard it strives; and Allah, every day, is engaged in some task."

They said: "O Abu Malik, we came to you with hearts full of rage against these people, ready to declare them unbelievers, to refute their opinion, and to make their blood and their wealth lawful for ourselves. But your words have humbled our hearts, and so has the way you have taken up their argument and defended them."

Abu Malik said: "This argument of mine is not for them; it is for the truth, that I not betray it in its absence. I have upheld the truth's own argument while the one who would plead for it is away. Nor is it a defense of them; rather I have defended you and myself against the speech of falsehood and against hurling stones into the unseen, at what we have not encompassed in knowledge. For otherwise we would be like the tyrants of the nations before us, who grew pleased with their own doctrines in their forbearance and rejoiced in the knowledge they had, until they supposed they had encompassed everything Allah had commanded and taught, and everything He willed for His creation. So they said: Allah will send no messengers after their messengers, and they never imagined that beyond the knowledge they had reached lay any doctrine at all. Thus they passed judgment upon Allah — that His argument should be cut off from His creation and from His successors in His earth. Then they made the knowledge Allah placed among His servants, and all that He sent His prophets and messengers with, the measure of their own minds; whatever exceeded that was, in their eyes, error. So by that opinion they went astray and led many astray. By their own judgment they made lawful what Allah had forbidden, and by their own opinion they killed the prophets of Allah. I take refuge with Allah for you, that you should follow the whims of a people who went astray before you. For people of intellect can be swayed by whim toward every notion and lured away from every path — save the path of religion, for it is the most forgiving in its pursuit, and its merit is not hidden."

They said: "O Abu Malik, we will not oppose your command, and your counsel is not lost on us. We would not obey you at the start of our affair only to disobey you at its end. You have pointed us to the opinion and hinted at the pursuit. If you think it right, take us to these people, so that their doctrine may be laid bare to us and we may look into it. If it is right guidance, we will follow it; and if it is misguidance, we will shun it."

Abu Malik said: "That is doubt on your part about what you already hold — and one who doubts knows neither his own right nor another's falsehood."

They said: "Then we hold firm to our own truth and reject as false whatever else there may be, be it what it may."

Abu Malik said: "That is not how meaning is sought, nor is guidance found by rejecting things as false."

They said: "How, then, is the seeking done, if we neither reject falsehood nor affirm the truth?"

Abu Malik said: "If you already know the truth and its acceptance, and know falsehood and its rejection, then you are not of the ranks of seekers at all; you are of the ranks of those learned in prophethood and guidance, who rule by revelation over the people of the world."

They said: "Then we are content with the truth we hold, we do not doubt it, and we excuse ourselves from the burden of seeking."

Abu Malik said: "Were you unwilling that they should hold the merit over you in a single respect — only to grant them now the merit over you in many? If you would not consent to stand wanting in the stations of the seekers, will you then stand ignorant in the stations of those who sit back?"

They said: "How has this come upon us, and in what have they been given merit over us?"

Abu Malik said: "Their merit over you lies in knowing what you know, and in an addition of what you do not know. Here is a matter that has come to a people. If you do not seek them out for it, they will seek you out over it; and if you will not listen to it, they will strive against you upon it — and so they will win merit over you by being first, by seeking, and by striving. As for sitting back from the seeking, that is ignorance entire, a squandering and a fault — a fault upon the learned until he teaches, and upon the ignorant until he learns."

They said: "O Abu Malik, you have taken us by the argument from every side, and every word of yours rings true. So open for us a door of your own counsel by which we may act."

Abu Malik said: "Conceit in one's opinion is blindness, neglect of the seeking is error, and there is no blame upon seekers for seeking. But the seeker needs to know the doors of the seeking, so that he seeks the truth knowing how to seek. Whoever seeks the truth without knowing how to seek is the quicker to reject it as false — for falsehood shows its first signs in ostentation and in agreement with received opinion, while truth shows its first signs in trial and in opposition to whims. And none forsakes the following of whim and bears patiently the onset of trial except one of sound heart."

They said: "Here a door has opened for us, where you point to the knowledge of seeking. Make plain to us what that knowledge is, and what the sound heart is."

Abu Malik said: "As for the knowledge of seeking, it is that you know yourselves to be in need. The one in need seeks his need with the humility of want before the thing he seeks. As for the sound heart, it is a heart that does not persist in rejecting as false whatever comes upon it, be it what it may, until the truth itself makes itself plain and shows its worth, and falsehood lays bare its own defect. Then the sound heart tells the falsehood apart by its intellect and receives the truth with acceptance."

They said: "We have been taught that whoever stands in this station — neither affirming the truth nor refuting the falsehood — is ignorant in his conduct, since he neither knows the truth so as to follow it nor knows falsehood so as to shun it."

Abu Malik said: "You have spoken truly in what you relate. But has he not, in your view, passed out of the rank of the liars and into the rank of the ignorant?"

They said: "Yes; in our view he is ignorant, not a liar."

Abu Malik said: "The ignorant man is the more to be hoped from, for good and for its seeking. For either he seeks knowledge for himself, or the learned seek him out — since ignorance is the native ground of the created, and there is no blame upon one who seeks knowledge out of his ignorance. But rejecting the truth as false is the worst of wares; the one who does it wins no guidance by it and is never made safe in what has been prescribed for him."

They said: "You have spoken truly. You have shown us the fault of rejecting things as false, so that we will never again reject anything until we know it. And you have shown us the door of seeking, through the humility of want before the thing sought. Is this humility and self-lowering before the thing sought to come after its right has been made plain, or before the plain proof?"

Abu Malik said: "No — only before the plain proof, so that by humility you may attain the plain proof."

They said: "Then if we humble ourselves before the thing sought until what it holds is made plain, and its plain proof turns out to be error, what becomes of that humility? Has it not been wasted?"

Abu Malik said: "No. The humility returns to Allah, and the self-lowering is to His command, and its reward is yours even if the one you humbled yourself before did not deserve it — because the seeker has discharged the duty owed to the seeking, though the truth's own due was not discharged for him. His likeness in that is the petitioner who stands before one of the kings to seek his bounty, and in his seeking nothing will serve him but humility."

Abu Malik said to them: "What you have related, you have believed. But has not this one passed beyond the bounds of the liars and become two ignorant men gathered into one?"

They said: "Yes. In our view he is ignorant, not a liar."

Abu Malik said: "The ignorant man hopes for the good and seeks it. He seeks knowledge for himself; and if he does not, the learned seek it out for him — because ignorance is the root condition of creation, and there is no shame in it for one who seeks knowledge on account of his ignorance. But lying is the worst of wares. The liar is not guided by his lying, and he is never believed in what stands recorded against him."

They said: "You have made us sure of it. We have come to know the defect of lying, and so we will never lie about anything until we know it for what it is. And you have opened to us the door of the request, which is reverence toward what is sought. Is this reverence and humility for the thing sought to be shown after it has made its right plain, or before the statement is given?"

Abu Malik said: "Rather before the statement, so that by reverence you may reach the statement."

They said: "But if we revere the one sought until what he holds becomes clear, and then his statement turns out to be misleading, what becomes of our reverence then? Has it not been wasted?"

Abu Malik said: "No. It was reverence to God and lowliness before His command, and you shall have its reward, even if the one before whom you humbled yourself did not deserve it. For the seeker has discharged the duty of the request, even if he has not discharged the duty of the truth. His likeness in this is the petitioner who stands before some king to ask his gift, and can ask only with reverence, flattery, and insistent pleading. If the king grants him something of what he asked, the king has fulfilled the duty owed to grace and holds the reward of what he gave. And if he grants nothing, the petitioner has still discharged the duty of asking by the gentleness of his plea, while niggardliness binds the withholder to his refusal. So too the seeker of knowledge: he must begin with reverence, humility, and patience under trial until he has fulfilled the duty of the request; and then time will bring him afterward whatever it brings."

They said: "This is the clear, manifest truth. Will you permit us, then, to go out to the people upon what you have counseled us, and not to overstep what you have commanded?"

Abu Malik said: "I would not counsel you to a thing and then be sparing of myself in it. Were it not for the fear of falling into fault by turning you back from the path of Allah, I would have counseled you to hold back while I went on ahead of you to the good — as an honor to myself and a preferring of my own soul above every soul. But we go together, and I share myself with you, as an honor to you and in thanks for the good you have made me a partner in."

They said: "It is as though you were expecting this matter before today."

He said: "This very thing, no. But I have never ceased expecting relief from Allah on every side — hoping for good from every road, inclining my ear toward every speaker, seeking increase from his words, whether he be in the right or in the wrong."

They said: "Increase from the words of the truthful — that we understand. But how do you hope for it from the words of the false?"

Abu Malik said: "Increase of truth upon truth is light; and to know falsehood for what it is in the false one is an increase in certainty. And the more certainty grows, the greater the heart's light grows from it. None are saved with Allah but the people of certainty. I am rising now to go to these people. If you are resolved to rise with me, then look to yourselves: whoever knows a sin in himself, let him repent to Allah; whoever bears a debt, let him pay it back to its owner. Wash yourselves clean of every defilement, put on for the request the purest garments of resolve, and bring to the thing sought the intention of one in need — for this is what I myself am doing. For our setting out is a setting out to Allah: if He knows truthfulness in us, He will grant us to reach His truth; and if He knows in us a mockery of the faith, He will turn us from His signs with the least humiliation, keeping His friend pure of the mockers."

They said: "Yes. We will do what you have commanded, within three days."

He said: "No."

They said: "Within two days, then?"

He said: "No — only within this very day of ours. If you will do it, well and good; and if not, then I am going on alone."

They said: "Then we repent to Allah of every sin, we clear away every debt from us, and we do what you have commanded. There is no power except in Allah — لا قوة إلا بالله."

So they did this from that same day, and they all set out together until they reached the gate of al-Bakhtari. When they had greeted him and sat down, Abu Malik said to al-Bakhtari: "O Abu Salih, how is your good son Salih, and where is he?"

Al-Bakhtari said: "Today Salih is my father, and I am the son of Salih; and Salih is with his Lord."

Supposing that Salih had died — since his father had said he was with his Lord — Abu Malik said: "إنا لله وإنا إليه راجعون — to Allah we belong and to Him we return. Has Salih died?"

Al-Bakhtari said: "Salih has not died, and will never die. He abides as long as time abides."

Abu Malik said: "Here are three matters whose meanings have been turned upside down. The child has become the father; the Lord is made present in the earth, and the Lord has a servant through whom one comes to Him; and the one created to perish has become everlasting. Alas for seekers, at such tangled speech. Behind this there is surely a wonder."

Al-Bakhtari said: "O Abu Malik, come to your need, and be sure in your asking, for you are at the very beginning of the ordeal."

Abu Malik said: "You have spoken truly. The beginning of the truth is an ordeal. And my need is a meeting with the righteous servant."

Al-Bakhtari said: "There is no way to that. Yet perhaps you mean my son Salih?"

He said: "Yes."

He said: "That is by his leave."

So the old man rose in haste and came in upon his son Salih, and told him the news of Abu Malik and his companions, rejoicing at it, for he knew how excellent their minds were. And Salih said: "O Allah, open the hearing of the hearts of Your servants, guide them by Your mercy to the signs of what is sought, and expand our breasts with the light of Your veiled light." Then he wiped his hand over his face, had the assembly cleared for them, and gave word to his father to bring them in.

When they entered upon him, greeted him, and sat down — Abu Malik and those below him taking their places in order — Salih drew Abu Malik near and seated him close beside himself. The first thing Salih said was: "O Abu Malik, you have favored us with your visit and outstripped us to the good by your generosity; yet you had the greater right that we should visit you, to keep your affection and to pay the duty long owed to you."

Abu Malik said: "And when were you ever wanting in excellence, O Abu al-Khair? You were forbearing among us when we were small, and honored us when we were grown; you became among us a caller and a warner to the good. Your root is sound, your branch is high, your work is pure. So joy be to you."

Salih said to him: "Have you come to me as an accuser, O Ka'b al-Ahbar, or as one merely following others? Where is that perfect mind and that excellent forbearance I have always known in you?"

Abu Malik said to him: "Your worth is too high for accusation, and religion is too high for blind following."

Salih said to him: "Then how do you name me a warner, when the warner is a prophet, and the prophet is a proof from Allah between Him and His creation — for them and against them? How can he be a proof for you when he is no proof against you?"

Abu Malik said: "You have spoken truly. This is the due. We do not proceed here on the ease of speech; we have begun with you by what is fairer. I saw that truthfulness better befits one who has set himself up as a veil for religion and a chief for the truth, and has opened its doors to seekers. We have come to know the excellence of asking, so we sought you out and set our hope on you as the one to be sought. Come, then, to our need."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, is not the one whose excellence you have come to know also one whose right has become owing from you?"

He said: "Yes, it is owing."

He said: "And when you came to know the excellence of asking, did you come to know its right?"

He said: "And what is its right?"

Salih said to him: "To know its faces, and then to seek out, from among them, the one in which the truth becomes owing to you."

Abu Malik said: "And what are its faces, and how many are they?"

Salih said to him: "Seeking has three faces: a seeker who knows, a seeker who does not yet know, and a seeker who knows that he does not know. Which of the three are you?"

Abu Malik said: "I am in more need of knowing the three than of anything else. How should I lay claim to a station I do not know?"

Salih said to him: "Then how did you claim to be a seeker at all, when you do not know the faces of seeking?"

Abu Malik said: "I did not know them. Make them plain to me."

Salih said: "Wealth is the treasure of the people of this world, and knowledge is the treasure of the people of the Hereafter. The seeking of wealth has three kinds: a seeker who seeks what he already owns, a seeker who seeks a thing due at an appointed term, and a seeker who seeks the gift of alms. So too the seeking of religion and the treasures of the Hereafter has three faces. The first is the seeker who knows — and he is the divine scholar, the rabbani, who seeks out the dead in their ignorance to revive them by his knowledge. The second is the seeker who is coming to know — and he is the learner who has grasped some of the degrees of knowledge and now seeks its farthest reach and limit. The third is the seeker who is coming to recognize — and he is the ignorant man, known by his ignorance, who knows nothing beyond his knowing that he is in need; so he seeks out the scholars in order to learn. These are the three faces, and these are the descriptions of the seekers."

Abu Malik said: "Yes. These are the faces of seeking, for religion and for the world alike. And I am the seeker coming to recognize, who knows nothing yet knows that he is in need. So teach me."

He said: "Did you come to know, or did you come to know by sight?"

Abu Malik said: "And what is the difference between the two?"

Salih said: "Knowledge is report; recognition is seeing with the eye."

Abu Malik said: "This too is something one needs to know."

Salih said: "As for report: that is a man who is ignorant and does not know that he is ignorant, until he meets a scholar, and the scholar informs him of his ignorance; then he knows he is ignorant and in need of knowledge, by that scholar's word. So his knowing of his ignorance reached him as report. As for recognizing his need and his ignorance: that is a man who has already known by his own mind that he asks about something but does not know the shape of its answer, so his breast is straitened by ignorance, and he hopes for no relief except by knowing what he was ignorant of; and so his ignorance itself became a recognition by his own mind. Ignorance is want, want is narrowness, and narrowness is a severe need that drives the creature to seek breadth — and the breadth is the breadth of knowledge, for knowledge is wide and spacious. From this I have known that you are in need."

Abu Malik said: "I find everything you have described within myself. I have come to know that I am in need — in severe want. So relieve my want by your excellence."

Salih said: "I see you hurrying into the request before being sure of it."

Abu Malik said: "Want hurried me on by its narrowness, so I hurried; and I knew the excellence of breadth, so I sought it."

Salih said to him: "If want has hurried you and you know the excellence of breadth, then how did you come to know the one to be sought?"

Abu Malik said: "Your excellence was made sure to me, so I turned to you; I hinted at my meaning, and so I sought you."

Salih said to him: "But from where was it made sure to you that I am the one known for breadth, and not you — so that you sought me? And from where did you learn that I am the one to be sought, so that you came? No scholar of prophecy pointed you to me, that you might learn; nor have you seen in me the tokens of the proofs, that you might recognize. Do you not see that in your seeking you are rising toward the ease of imitation? In that you wrong wisdom and you wrong yourself — like a man who measures out grain by the bottom of the measure: if he does not level it off, he wrongs his creditor, and if he presses it down, he wrongs himself. So it is with one who takes knowledge by imitation: if he acts on it, he earns no thanks; and if he abandons it, he is called an unbeliever. And you, in my estimation, are wiser than that."

Abu Malik said: "I know full well that it is so. Yet I had hoped that the truth would be something that establishes itself, and that I might spare you the labor of establishing it — for dispute breeds contention, and contention breeds rancor, since each of two adversaries seeks the upper hand over his fellow. And when will he ever reach the truth who persists in defending the false?"

Salih said to him: "Excellent is what you say. The truth does indeed establish itself by its own excellence and honor, and when quarreling grows, it only distracts hearts from what is worthier than it. Yet there is no forsaking what the scholars have laid down, for they have said: every seed for which the ground has not first been sweetened does not thrive, and every knowledge for which hearts have not first been made pure by proof does not grow."

Abu Malik said: "Then bring forward whatever proofs you wish. For the words of wisdom are like a jewel: if you turn it over, its light gleams, and if you set it aside, its excellence is still not hidden."

Salih said to him: "You speak truly — but only when no counterfeit of coated glass has been set beside the jewel; then the jewel is not hidden. Yet when claims grow many, and the tyrants of the world contend over religion until they dress it in what is not its own and coat it over with a semblance before the seekers, then it becomes the seeker's duty to put the jewel to the test, so that its excellence may become plain and the counterfeit, together with its people, may be exposed."

Abu Malik said: "You have dealt fairly and fulfilled the duty of the truth. As for the counterfeiting of these people, we have come to know it and its defects, and so we have cast it off, and held fast to this opinion by which we came to know you, and we have called it the sound jewel of speech. We took our stand upon it, you agreed with us upon it, and together we all clung to its rope. Now if you have found a meaning more precious as a jewel than this — then, by my life, jewels do surpass one another, some above some. So show me where the excellence of your jewel lies, by a test through which its excellence is known. As for our jewel, we have known it and made it known; so say of it whatever seems right to you."

The talk between them had come round to the testing of true knowledge, which Salih likened to a precious jewel. The duty of the seeker, he had said, is to have the jewel assayed, so that its worth is made plain and the counterfeit, together with those who trade in it, is exposed.

Abu Malik said: "You have dealt fairly and fulfilled the duty of the truth. As for the counterfeit of this doctrine, we have come to know it and its defects, and so we rejected it and held fast to the opinion by which we know ourselves, saying: This is the sound jewel of speech. We upheld it, you agreed with us upon it, and together we held to its rope. If you have found a meaning more precious in substance than ours — then, by my life, jewels do indeed surpass one another. So show me the excellence of your jewel by a test through which its worth may be known. As for our own jewel, we have known it and you have known it; say of it what seems right to you."

Salih said to him: "As for your jewel, it stood with us as a thing of honor, in the place of the sound jewel, until we presented it to the jewelers and the people of the mines, and they found it false and would not accept it from us as a jewel. They said: This is glass in disguise — and they showed us its defects, so that it fell away from the very bounds of what a jewel is. For a jewel is raised up only by its own preciousness, and by the knowledge of the people of insight who prize it and vie for it, until its price mounts to every sum. When the jewelers who know it reject it, who else recognizes it, and who accepts it afterward save the people of delusion, who neither buy nor sell? So it is with knowledge: it is not knowledge until it is presented to those who know the science of heaven. If they accept it, it is true knowledge; and if they do not, it is idle nonsense — and idle nonsense has no place in wisdom, nor do the wise accept it."

Abu Malik said: "And who are these that know the science of heaven, after the prophets and the messengers? We know of none of them in our age, nor have men held any covenant with such since revelation was cut off with the closing of the books; and the nations are now called to account only by reports. What do you say to this?"

Salih said: "Any religion held to by way of report alone is not to be called a religion, for religion is one — upright, upheld by God in its truth, admitting no difference within it — whereas the reports, as you well know, are shot through with disagreement, each denying the other. And the religion of God is dearer than that."

Abu Malik said: "But when they differ over a report, they refer it back to the books. What agrees with the books they accept, and what contradicts them they set aside as false."

Salih said to him: "Yet what is found in the books only in general terms — who will make it particular for them? And what is set down in detail — who will draw it together for them? Who among them is to be trusted concerning what God has made lawful and what He has forbidden? I heard you on the day of the dispute, when a distressed man came to you and said: O Abu Malik, I swore an oath by divorce and then broke it. You said to him: Some hold that she is severed from you and not lawful to you until she marries another husband, and some hold that she is still your wife and is not forbidden to you by a single pronouncement. The man said to you: Then which of the two shall I act upon? You said: Act upon whichever of the two you wish. So he rose from your presence not knowing whether he was going home to what is lawful or to what is forbidden. Is this how judgment is given in the religion of God? Is this how His lawful and His forbidden are made known?"

Abu Malik said: "The Sharia of my age has indeed been ruined, suspended, and thrown into disorder, and we now hold to nothing but the name of religion. What, then, is the state of religion with you?"

Salih said to him: "And what is religion, O Abu Malik?"

He said: "Command and prohibition, the lawful and the forbidden, the Sunnah and the obligations."

Salih said to him: "And is Sharia, in your view, anything other than this?"

Abu Malik said: "I know of nothing beyond what I have described to you."

Salih said to him: "Then you have gathered religion and Sharia onto a single ground?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said to him: "Then religion has been thrown into disorder by the suspension of Sharia and corrupted by its corruption. What, then, is left with you?"

Here Abu Malik held back from answering for a time, his head bowed, turning over the matter of his religion. Then he raised his head and said: "I do not see that anything at all is left with me. So what is religion with you?"

Salih said to him: "Religion, as I hold it, is that whose door is barred against the corrupters and whose gates are thrown open to the seekers, so that whoever brings it is ennobled by being foremost to it, and whoever seeks it attains the good through it. By it works are honored, and by its justice the laws come into flower. It is a bond joining heaven to earth, connected and unbroken: the firm handhold of those who take hold, the rope of those who cling fast, the ark of tranquility, the ship of deliverance, and the light of life — generous in what it holds, guarded in its sciences, marked out in its jewels, its excellence hidden from none who look upon it."

Abu Malik said: "If God has a religion by which He will judge on the Day, one He is pleased with and for which He rewards and punishes, then it must be in this sense. And now that the excellence of the description has been made plain, what of the One described?"

Salih said to him: "The good comes only from the good, O Abu Malik. Set your light in the lamp-niche of your reason and grasp what is meant for you. The claims of what is decreed have contended over you, the robes of delusion have made you heedless, and you have taken upon yourself a charge that none but you can bear — along with the affairs of those who follow you in good faith, who have listened to your word, accepted your guidance, and trusted your judgment. It is your duty to guard yourself and them from a Fire you cannot avoid coming to; so seek the way of escape from it. You are the most favored of them in the grace of God and the most bound to give Him thanks, for I have known you to recognize the good and to hope for its reward. As for what you have asked — the confirming of that to which I call you — it is my duty to establish it for you from whatever side you wish."

Abu Malik said: "All that you have said before is firm and true. As for the reports of the common people, religion is not set right by them, nor are they fit for it. Nothing remains for us but to know that to which you call us."

Salih said to him: "I call you to the justice of God and to His oneness."

Abu Malik said: "How excellent is that to which you call. We ourselves hold this very saying and know its excellence above all doctrines."

Salih said to him: "It is not where your fancy has gone, O Abu Malik. That doctrine you have in mind is a describing of justice without knowledge of the One described, and a swerving into the names of God without knowledge of His oneness. What I call you to is the knowledge of God's justice and the landmarks of His unity. If you wish, I will bring you to see the corruption of that saying, as far as you please."

Abu Malik said: "If the saying of the people of justice is corrupt and their monotheism a swerving, then there is no firm truth upon the earth — unless the meaning be something other than all the doctrines. Show us, then, the corruption of justice and monotheism, and where it comes from."

Salih said to him: "Justice and monotheism are among the noblest of all doctrines. Monotheism is too exalted to be corrupted, for monotheism has no opposite; and justice cannot be corrupted, for it is the judgment between God and His creation. But the doctrines are all corrupted by the way their people have corrupted the saying of justice and the rest — for it is a claim by which no statement is established. They do not encompass the knowledge of what they say until they defer to the command of God. So take up whichever of the two you wish and hold to it, until I strip it from you by the proof of truth."

Abu Malik said: "I would not defend a saying I do not hold, nor one whose author was the one questioned about it rather than I, and I have not set myself up as anyone's agent, nor am I keeper over the authors of these doctrines. I will defend before you nothing but my own saying. I say that God is One, that nothing is like Him, just in His ruling, who does not burden His servants with what they have no power to bear. He commanded them and forbade them, yet He did not command by absolute decree nor forbid by compulsion; rather His command is a granting of choice and His prohibition a warning, so that none obeyed Him under coercion and none disobeyed Him as one overpowered. This is my meaning and what I hold."

Salih said to him: "Did I not tell you that this doctrine is among the noblest? Yet these are names and attributes that avail nothing unless their meaning is present. It is as when you say: Fire is hot. You have spoken truly, but with a truth that does not profit you — for your saying, Fire is hot, is a name and an attribute whose mention does you no good if no food is cooked by it and no darkness is lit by it, apart from the meaning that is the very presence of fire. And if names and attributes could stand in things in the place of meanings, then the mere mention of fire would burn your tongue, and the mention of food would fill you."

Abu Malik said: "As for the saying about these things, you have spoken truly, for their names and attributes profit nothing except by the presence of their meaning. So show me that my saying of justice and monotheism are names and attributes that call for the knowledge of a Named and Described One."

Salih said to him: "Your saying that God is one, and 'one' — واحد — is four letters, of which we see two joined and two apart. Which of them is 'one' — or are all of them together 'one'? And your saying 'Allah' — الله — is four letters. Which of them is Allah — or are all of them together Allah, or are they signs that point to God, or do you say otherwise?"

Then Abu Malik was struck silent for a time. Then he said: "I find no way out of this except that they are signs that point to God."

Salih said to him: "Which of them points to God, and by what do they point to Him — by their being apart or by their being joined? Or singly and jointly, as when you say الله, where the alif stands alone and the three letters are joined together? Which points to God: this one by standing alone, or these by being joined?"

Abu Malik said: "I do not know what to say to you in this. Take us on to some other door."

Salih said to him: "The door to the knowledge of monotheism has been shut in your face by whoever taught you this in this way, O Abu Malik."

He said: "Yes."

Salih said to him: "Then I ask you about your knowledge of God: where is it from, and from whom did you take it? Was it from an attribute that pointed you to One described?"

He said: "No."

Salih said: "Then from a name of that One, pointing to a Named?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said: "And what is the name that pointed you?"

He said: "Allah."

Salih said: "Is Allah a name, or the Named?"

He said: "The Named."

Salih said: "Then what is the name?"

He said: "Allah."

Salih said: "So Allah is a name for Allah?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said: "Then what is the difference between the name and the Named? And which of the two do you worship — or do you worship both?"

Abu Malik was bewildered for a time, turning over his answer. Then he said: "I find no way in this except that I have looked upon all the crafts, and each of them has a maker; and so I judged that this great creation has a Creator who resembles nothing of His creation, just as the makers do not resemble what they have made."

Salih said to him: "O Abu Malik, you have led us away from the first point, and the narrowness of the meaning of monotheism has driven you to seek Him by analogy and to set up parables for Him. God is dearer than that — for everything made is made from some thing, whereas God did not create things from anything that was before them. These makers you know only by having watched some of them with your own eyes, how each one works; and so you passed judgment on a making whose maker was hidden from you, by what you had observed of a doing that resembled the doings of the makers you had seen and known — which is analogy on your part. But when God created, He did not make you witness the creation of what He made, nor was there for Him a likeness who makes as He makes; yet you have testified from the present likeness to the absent likeness. I have never known you, O Abu Malik, to be content with analogy in your religion — so how are you content to seek the knowledge of your Lord by measurement? And your saying, Nothing is like Him — ليس كمثله شيء — proves, out of your own words, that He has a likeness and that nothing is like that likeness. Had you intended monotheism, you would have said, He is not like anything — ليس كهو شيء — and you would not have said, Nothing is like Him. Glory be to Him whom no far reach of aspiration attains by thought, and whom no soaring of the pointing hand attains by perception; nay, He is exalted high above the description of those who are charged with the law. O Abu Malik, had you aimed this quest at one of His servants, or an angel of His, you would still have been left unable to grasp what is said of him and how he was made — how then the One who made him? The lights of the people of the earth fall short of grasping the light of the sun, and the sun is but one of the lights of this world; so how shall one who falls short of grasping a servant of His grasp the Creator? Say what seems right to you."

Then Abu Malik cast his eyes to the ground in bewilderment and could find no path to speech.

Salih said to him: "O Abu Malik, raise your head and say whatever comes to you."

Abu Malik said: "Speech is gone; there is nothing to say. Minds are bewildered before the answer, imaginations are struck dumb and turned away from thought, and the instrument is too weak, so that nothing is grasped by it but seeking and asking."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik — the One who is of such greatness, do the eyes grasp Him?"

He said: "No, He is higher than that."

Salih said: "Is speech heard from Him?"

He said: "There is no speech except from a speaker, and the speaker is described by speech, and God is too exalted for attributes."

Salih said to him: "This Vast One in greatness, this High One in power — would He be unjust in His creation, charging them with what they have no power for and commanding them with what they cannot grasp, and then punishing them if they fail to do it?"

Abu Malik said: "Whoever charges the obedient with what he has no power for has charged him with disobedience, and to punish him for it is an outrage; and God is dearer than that."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik — the One who so holds Himself far above injustice and above outrage, would He be content that ignorance should keep company with His servants?"

Abu Malik said: "Ignorance is a sure denial of thanksgiving and a persisting in disbelief, and God does not love the unbelievers."

Salih said: "Were they, then, created as knowers who already knew what was wanted of them?"

He said: "No — rather He created them ignorant, knowing nothing."

Salih said: "Then from where do they come to know what He wants of them, so that it becomes binding upon them to seek His good pleasure and to guard themselves against His wrath?"

Abu Malik said that men had learned this from God.

Then Salih pressed him: If He is higher than sight can reach, wider than perception can grasp, and greater than the limits of speech, then how does the one whom He commands and forbids come to know His will — so as to obey Him rather than disobey, and thank Him rather than deny — unless there are causes for this knowledge set between Him and His creation? Must there not be those who deliver His justice and bear witness to His witnessing, for the servants and against them — whom He has chosen by His knowledge and singled out from all His creation, made messengers to them and proofs against them, so that they stand as the just ones between Him and His servants, conveying His command and His prohibition? Obedience to them is then obedience to God and the winning of His pleasure, and disobedience to them is His wrath and His punishment. For otherwise, how could the command reach God's creation from Him at all?

This, said Abu Malik, belongs to the foundations of justice. Once creation stands in need of God's justice to teach them what He wants of them, and He teaches them through the hands of the righteous one He has appointed, then each shall thank his benefactor, and be rewarded, reproached, or punished by Him as He wills.

Salih asked whether it was permissible to rule with justice in any way other than this. It is permissible for none but God, Abu Malik answered, and God is the wisest of judges.

Then Salih posed the harder case: Would it be permissible for Him to delegate the command to some of His people, setting them up justly to correct their own wrongdoing, and then to assign two others to the very same task — yet without guiding them by an equal measure of His justice, as He had guided the first?

The judgment can only be one, said Abu Malik, because justice is one. If the ruling were split into two meanings, one of the two would inevitably fall into wrong.

Has it not been established for you, Salih said, that the judgment of God is one, with no difference in it, and that His way is a single way, unchanged among His messengers? Were it not for the succession of the messengers, and the standing of command and prohibition, the truth would have no increase and creation no meaning. Yes, said Abu Malik, this is the truth.

And is not this succession of the messengers, sent from God to His creation, so that He may lay obedience upon them as an obligation, and set them as an authority between Himself and His creatures — bearing witness for those who obey them, so that their obedience is accepted, and against those who disobey them, so that they are turned away? The judgment could not have been otherwise, Abu Malik said, for otherwise He would have given the lie to His own messengers and broken His promise, and God is too generous for that. The affair of the messengers has made it plain that they are of His justice, and that people worship Him by obeying them. But tell me of their ranks: are they equal in the sight of God, or do some hold a merit above others?

How could they stand level in rank, Salih answered, when they are bound to obey one another? Were they all on one plane, none would owe obedience to another. Rather, by His justice He has preferred some above others, so that obedience passes from the lesser to the more excellent, and from the excellent to the one above him, until it reaches its utmost, and they obey and worship to the very limit of what God wills.

Then is the one who obeys the excellent among them, Abu Malik asked, equal in reward to the excellent one himself? Yes, said Salih. This is the justice of God, which grants every obedient servant his reward, and one does not ask whether the measure of the superior or of the lesser applies.

Is each of them, then, like his fellow? No, said Salih, for they come from the Creator bearing knowledge of the unseen and the Books He has revealed to them, and no one can produce their like except from God.

Then can anyone imitate the lesser one among them, Abu Malik asked, if no Book is sent down to him carrying knowledge of the unseen? No one, said Salih, can lay claim to that station.

And how can that be, when the lesser ones did not set themselves apart from the rest of creation by the unseen, as the excellent one made himself plain?

If the excellent one, Salih replied, set himself apart from others by knowledge of the unseen in the revelation, the tanzil, then the lesser one likewise sets himself apart from others by knowledge of the unseen in the interpretation, the tawil. Both the revelation and its interpretation are from God, and what is with God is attained only through revelation.

And the lesser one too is inspired? Abu Malik asked. Yes, said Salih; and for that reason obedience to him became obedience to God.

But if you place him level with the excellent one in obedience and in inspiration, why do you name him the lesser? Because of the obedience he owes to the excellent one, Salih said, and his need of him.

And what need has he of a creature, Abu Malik pressed, when the revelation reaches him from the Creator? Though the revelation reaches him, Salih answered, it reaches him as the interpretation of the sayings and the Books, and of the remainder that the excellent ones left behind. So it was revealed to Lot at the hands of Abraham, and Abraham's excellence was made plain; and so it was revealed to Ishmael and to Isaac, while the goodness, the blessings, and the merit were all reckoned to Abraham. The revelation from God is joined to His just ones in His earth, each according to his rank. In His justice He does not will to cut off His command, His prohibition, and His worship from His servants after He has favored them with these, so that worship should be severed and ignorance made lawful.

The bearer of the revelation, Abu Malik said, has shown his truthfulness by the knowledge of the unseen in his Book. By what does the bearer of the interpretation show that the knowledge of the unseen is with him, so that the revelation bears witness for him and lays obedience to him alone as an obligation, as it did for the bearer of the revelation? He comes in his interpretation, Salih said, with knowledge from heaven and a declaration from the highest assembly, al-mala al-a'la, and the Book bears witness to it.

Then I bear witness, said Abu Malik, to what the Books have testified: that every interpretation to which the revelation itself testifies is a revelation from God. Otherwise all the Books and the messengers would have to be denied, since their meaning is one and the same. If some of them give the lie to others, all must be reckoned liars; and if some confirm others, all must be believed.

Such is justice, Abu Malik went on, and by it the truthfulness of those who hold to it is known, and their merit. Obedience is due to those confirmed to be among the messengers who are His justice in His creation, whom He charged to convey His message. And if obedience is obligatory by prophethood, by revelation, and by interpretation, then what of those who sit in the seats of the messengers, resembling them and yet not of them? They have all agreed that after their prophet there is no prophet and in their time no warner, that they themselves are the ones who uphold the command of God and His religion, and that what they say leads to God's reward or His punishment. Yet you say otherwise. How, then, could the scholars of this community have failed to look into this matter?

They have not left it wholly unexamined, Salih answered, but they have not looked into it with a sound looking, and no one has spoken of it to them so that they might lend it the ear of their minds; for it has been steeped into their hearts that they are worthier of the command of God and His religion, and that there is no prophet after their prophet — just as the misleaders of the nations before them said. Every nation has claimed that there is no prophet after its own.

And what, Abu Malik asked, was the origin of this, and what did they intend by it?

Its originators, Salih said, were three in every age: a rebellious devil, a stubborn tyrant, and a hypocritical jurist. By this saying they sought to cut off the marks of the prophets from the distinctions of prophethood and to make it a possession passed among themselves. They could not do so until they had steeped the hearts of the nations in the saying, addressed to every nation, that God had sent none better than your prophet, and that after him there is no prophet and no warner. So they whispered to them from where they did not perceive it, and drew them away from where they did not know. Every nation clung to its own prophet by calling all who came after him liars, supposing that this drew them nearer to their prophet, and each held that every prophet after its own was false. Thus, on the strength of that saying, the mention of prophethood and the marks of the prophets ceased among all the nations, so that they neither sought what they had lost nor would have believed it had they sought. So the misleading devils gained their power over those they misled, and the tyrants gained their power to kill the prophets and killed them, and the doubt of the corrupt jurists worked its way in, until they were adorned in their own eyes — and thus they perished and made others perish. And all of this because no hearts among creation inclined toward the prophets when they appeared, nor sought them out when they were gone, so that despair of the prophets ran to its end among them, until the prophets called them openly to the truth and were called liars, and were killed in their midst, and no one was moved to anger.

Here is the nation of the Magi, clinging to its tyranny, worshipping its idols upon its fire, saying — as they claim — that God will send no messenger after their messenger. Here is the nation of the Torah, clinging to its scripture, devoted to its calf, standing within the bounds set by its prophets, humbling itself before its Judge, saying that God will raise up no messenger after Moses. Here is the nation of the Gospel, speaking by its likening, claiming that its prophet is its God, drawing near with its crosses, exalting its priests, saying that God will send no messenger after Jesus. And here is your own community, led along the paths of those who went before it, misled by what misled them: it inherited the Book from other than its rightful people, walked the religion by other than its own way, followed its great ones into error, obeyed its masters in misguidance, and its jurists bowed before the tyrants, so that they set up for the people a religion other than the Sunnah, performing the prayer at other than its time and rendering the alms to other than its rightful due. They are agreed to be patient with these leaders, constant in their need of them, certain of their independence from any other — saying that God will send no messenger and no warner after their Messenger.

So you and those like you followed the saying of the great multitude, and what won you over was the abundance of the consensus and the force of the sultans. You accepted it from them, adorned it with the beauty of your doctrines, gave it preference by the beauty of your words, and raised upon it an argument of justice in a matter where no such argument can be brought against you. You followed those above you among the mighty out of awe, and those below you followed you out of desire, and they supposed it was the truth. Now if this community must be believed in what it has decreed against God — that His proofs are cut off from His creation on the strength of a single strand of consensus — then the nations before it have the better claim to be believed, by three qualities that establish their argument against you, and by which the truth is theirs rather than yours.

And what, Abu Malik asked, are those qualities in which their merit over us is such that they must be believed if their word is true, or held liars if their word is crooked? Their word is like your word, Salih said, and your word is of the same kind as theirs; your hearts resemble one another — and yet the merit in the argument is theirs. The first quality: they say they came earlier to the religion of God than you, and so theirs is the merit of the foremost. The second: the consensus, both among themselves and between them and you, agrees in affirming what they hold, and so theirs is the merit of the truthful. The third: you yourselves testify that their prophets are truthful and are the messengers of God, while not one of them has borne witness to any prophet of yours, neither to his prophethood nor to his truthfulness. So the merit over you is theirs, by your agreeing with them and their standing apart from you; and they say that the knowledge you possess is copied from their knowledge, and that theirs is the root of yours. This is their argument against you, clear in speech and plain in justice. If the ruling of creation is binding upon the Creator, then the ruling of these is the more binding. But if the Creator is Himself the Ruler over His creation — abrogating what He wills and confirming what He wills, with none to overturn His judgment — then, by my life, the word of all of them falls away, and the Creator is foremost over the creation and over the command. Every day He is engaged in some affair — "كل يوم هو في شأن" — and His act is not to be denied, though He send a warner every day.

This community, said Abu Malik, has fallen into a far straying. Most of those before them strayed as well, Salih answered, for they cast the friends of God, the awliya Allah, out of their right, let the foolish overpower them in their affair, and made lawful what God had forbidden — and did so deliberately, that they might not seem to imitate prophethood and so make the people long for the prophets and refrain from killing them. Instead they diverted the people with kingship and largesse and led them along in the following of their desires. In this they were helped by those known outwardly for truthfulness and piety, who made a show of it before the people, until the very ones who had commanded the community to the good turned it away from the good, and the ones who had forbidden the evil guided it toward the evil, and few remained who kept faith with the friends of God. So truth and falsehood came to alternate like light and darkness in their difference. Were it not for the remnant of God's friends and His just ones in the earth, and the revelation that comes to them, the little that is true would never rise above the much that is false. For by what could God tell the foul from the good, save by His just ones? And how could God's argument reach all creation, save through them?

There is no escape, Abu Malik said, and no evading the justice of God and His command; with adversaries so many, none remain but the just servants of God, and by them the argument of God to His creation is upheld.

Then, since it is established that the company of the prophets of God are the just ones, Salih said, is not each single one of them called a just man? Yes, said Abu Malik, he is a just man, as it is said: So-and-so bore witness, and he was a just man among the just.

So it is now established for you, Salih said, that the just one is the friend of God, and that he has passed beyond the bounds of your talk of a mere attribute and become one who is himself described. Yes, said Abu Malik, this is a true saying.

And whoever does not attain the one described, Salih asked, does the description profit him? The description does not profit him, Abu Malik answered, except by attaining the one described.

Salih said to him: Then whoever does not know the just one of Allah and His guardian — can his own speech be just?

At this the narrator relates that Abu Malik smiled and said: By Allah, you have drawn me out of my whole position on justice and monotheism, and nothing has drawn me out of it but the truth itself, in the clearest of arguments and the gentlest of speech. After this, what position is left for me to defend?

Salih said: From here, O Abu Malik, the coated glass falls from the hands of those who trade it as the colored gems it is not. Its worth was never truly known; but when it was set before the jewelers it was exposed, and its deceived owners were exposed along with it.

Abu Malik said: You have spoken truly, and the crookedness of these doctrines and the straying of the nations has now become plain to me. But how are we to find safety from them, and to know the means that lead to Allah — or even one such means — when we did not live in the age of the prophets, and there are in our own time neither prophets nor messengers as there were among the nations before us?

Salih said: Glory be to Allah, O Abu Malik. You have spent a long stretch of your life affirming the justice of Allah, yet the moment we press it upon you where it becomes binding upon you to accept it, you turn back and speak as though Allah would deceive you, and you ascribe injustice to Him.

Abu Malik said: God forbid. I say no such thing, for whoever says it has transgressed and lied against Allah. From where did you bind me to that?

Salih said: From your own words — that the age of the prophets has passed you by, and that in our time there is no prophet and no messenger. Tell me: was His sending of the messengers among the former peoples an act of justice from Him toward His servants, or was it done in vain?

He said: God forbid that it be anything but justice from Him.

Salih said: Then what has cut us off from that justice? Are we not His servants as they were? Were they not created as we are created, and are we not bound in service as they were bound? Has He then wronged us after being just to them, so that we who come after are left excused? Or has His giving run dry in our time, so that we cling only to the bounty He gave to them, and He is content with that from us? Or has the way of Allah changed with us after it stood firm with those before us, so that we should pass judgment upon Allah that He has changed? Or is His justice an obligation owed to us and His ruling in force upon us, just as it was His way with those who came before us — so that there is no altering the words of Allah and none to overturn His judgment? And who deserves the name of the just one except the guardians of Allah? Among all that Allah has created, none is just except the one Allah has chosen, who became just by His command. It is for this reason that a ruling is called just — on account of the justice of the one who decreed it — and a word is called true on account of the truthfulness of the one who spoke it.

Abu Malik said: It can only be as you say, and we are not permitted to say of Allah anything but the truth. Yet we walked the road of our own opinion in speech and judged by the absence of the rightly guided from among us. We held that this too was part of Allah's ruling and His way with us, as it had been with those before us in the interregnums between one messenger and the next — like the interval between the religion of Abraham and the time of Moses and the Torah, and the interval between the Torah and the time of Jesus and the Gospel. On that footing we spoke and acted, working our way through an age of interruption, holding fast to the traditions and the established way.

Salih said to him: The religion of Allah is far too precious to Him for that. What interregnum was there after the religion of Abraham, when Allah sent after him Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Jonah, and Shu'ayb, leading on to Moses? He inspired them to do good works, to call to the religion of Abraham, to establish the prayer according to its way and to render the zakat according to its obligations, until He sent down the ruling of the Torah upon Moses. And what interregnum was there after the religion of Moses, between him and the Gospel, when Allah sent after Moses Joshua son of Nun, and Elias, and Talut, and David, and Solomon, and Zechariah, and John, leading on to Jesus? They upheld the ruling of the Torah, holding to its religion, calling to it, and reciting its Psalms, until the ruling of the Gospel was sent down upon Jesus. So it was with those who went before them and those who came after, and with the many generations between, whom none knows but Allah. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs; for each people Allah set forth His parables, and each was called to the truth. Where, then, is this interregnum among them, and when did it ever fall? Allah said only, "after a succession of messengers" — عَلَى فَتْرَةٍ مِنَ الرُّسُل — and by these words He affirmed both the messengers and the pause. When the call arose, it arose out of fear, so they called in secret and concealment. The earth was never left empty of the justice of Allah for the blink of an eye, but that His just one was in it — either openly present or hidden away in fear.

Abu Malik said: This is a statement that cannot be turned back, a settled word in which there is no doubt. As for the standing of Allah's proof through justice and the succession of the messengers, that is now established and made clear. So point me now to the meaning of their absence in this age of ours. What is its cause?

Salih said to him: What was the cause of the absence of Moses when he went out from Pharaoh's city and passed beyond it in fear, watchful, until he reached the land of Shu'ayb?

Abu Malik said: He fled from the people of Pharaoh, hiding himself from them, because he feared for his life when they had agreed together to kill him.

Salih said to him: Have you heard, in this law of yours, of any one of the just — the just ones of Allah and the sons of His prophets — who was killed while patiently bearing the burdens of the religion?

Abu Malik said: Yes. A great many of them have been killed.

Salih said to him: Then which just one of the just of Allah do you wish would appear to you and to the people of your age? One who was slain, whom Allah would raise up and send to you after death? Or one in hiding from among you, whom you would drive back to the very killing he fled — when he has only followed the way of Allah in His prophets and messengers, patient under fear and in concealment, until Allah judges between him and us, and He is the best of judges?

Abu Malik said: There is no blame upon one who follows the way of the righteous by fearing for himself and fleeing from his people. The blame lies upon the evil nations and the helpers of the oppressors, for their killing of the prophets of Allah and His just ones, and for their agreeing together upon that deed.

Salih said: Then what do you say of one who kills the just one of Allah and His guide? Where is his end? And what of the one who helped him?

Abu Malik said: Both are bound for the Fire, and so is anyone who raised a banner for them or handed them an inkwell.

Salih said to him: And what do your jurists say of this?

Abu Malik said: They ask mercy for the slain, yet they see no sin in the killer.

Salih said to him: And whoever obeys the killer of his own accord — is he not a partner with him in the blood of the slain?

Abu Malik said: Yes, by Allah. He is his partner, and worse than he is.

Salih said to him: His partnership is plain, from his aiding him. But how has he become worse than the killer himself?

Abu Malik said: Were it not for the reports of the jurists, the rule of the tyrants could never have been established through the fools who follow them.

Salih said to him: You have spoken the truth, O Abu Malik. Have you then accepted for yourself their kingdom as your home, their land as your dwelling place, their armies as your neighbors, and their enforcers as your close companions — while you serve as their just man, judging for them by your knowledge and settling their affairs by their authority? You have prayed for them in your petitions and thanked them for what you sought from them. Upon their tyranny you set the crown of your justice, and they grew proud; over their filth you cast the robe of the sacred law, and they overstepped all bounds; and you lit up their falsehood by leaving your own truth to lie idle. So the weak were deceived by your conduct, and every scholar who was like you said: These are the jurists of the community; we have in them a pattern to follow. They went astray through you, and you went astray through the ways of the tyrants, and you supposed that the one held to account would be someone other than yourselves. You forgot the words of Allah, glory be to Him — "وَلاَ تَرْكَنُوا إِلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا فَتَمَسَّكُمُ النَّارُ" — And do not incline toward those who have done wrong, lest the Fire touch you. So you severed what Allah commanded to be joined, and you saw what you were doing; you spoke of the outward forms of justice while wandering lost from its path. You turned away from His saying — "أَطِيعُوا اللهَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ وَأُولِي الأَمْرِ مِنْكُمْ" — Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you — and you claimed that these oppressors were your rulers, even though they had disbelieved. You forgot the words of Allah, exalted is He — "قَاتِلُوا الَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُمْ مِنَ الكُفَّارِ" — Fight those of the disbelievers who are near you. So you disobeyed the one you were commanded to obey, and obeyed the one you were commanded to fight, all the while supposing that you were doing good work. Error carried you away, and your own opinion led you on, until you said that Allah would send no messenger and no warner in your age. Is it that the goodness of your deeds has set you above all need of the guardians of Allah for the proof? Or is it that the ugliness of your deeds has cut you off from all hope in Allah's acceptance of repentance? Or is it justice that you should pass judgment upon Allah without justice, and be content, on behalf of your Messenger, with what none of you would be content with for himself in his place? Would any one of you consent that his own son be killed, or that his house and wealth be taken from him without right?

Abu Malik said: No one consents to that. If a child of one of us were killed, or he were driven from his property, he would rage, and his people would rage with him, and they would not be appeased until he was appeased.

Salih said to him: Then Allah and His Messenger have the greater right that you should rage for their sake, when His vicegerent is killed and His guardian is driven from the earth.

Abu Malik said: It is bitter to us that the guardian of Allah should be killed among us or driven from his land. But they themselves chose to withdraw from us, and the tyrants rose to power over us.

Salih said to him: Had you supported the guardians of Allah, they would have appeared; had you forsaken His enemies, those enemies would never have prevailed. But the case has ranged you, before the guardians of Allah, in three ranks. There is the empowered killer, who never tires of the pursuit and is not satisfied even when he has won. There is the gloating approver, who bites his fingertips in rage, sparing no effort to incite others against them, never heedless of them even when they are heedless of him. And there is the one who loathes what is done, who professes love for the guardians of Allah yet forsakes them, having no clear sight in faith and no trust to be placed in him for keeping a secret. For this cause the guardians of Allah have withdrawn from you, in prudent concealment — for your sake and for the sake of this vast multitude, who have taken their own desires as their god, fallen greedily upon the goods of this world, and joined hands over the killing of the prophets. They knew nothing of the fault in what they did, so they never held themselves to account for the wrong they committed, and never humbled themselves to repent of what they had done. Instead they turned the injustice of their own deeds back upon Allah and ascribed it to Him, and said: Allah will send no messenger after their messenger, and no warner. So they made lawful the killing of the guardians of Allah without right. They wronged in deed, deviated in word, and transgressed by their denial.

Abu Malik said: Yes. This is the very description of this community of ours, and this is exactly what they say. Where, then, is the deliverance from it, and where is the escape from the punishment of Allah?

Salih said to him: To Allah. Seek His protection, and He will protect you. Turn to Him in repentance, and He will accept your repentance. Ask Him for the way of His guardians, and by your works He will save you from the deeds of those who did wrong. So it is with this whole multitude: were they to turn to Allah in repentance, He would accept their repentance — and what then would Allah do with punishing them? Allah does not wrong them by the weight of a single atom, though they have wronged and lied, and He does not hasten the punishment upon them, though they hasten to disobey.

Abu Malik said: Then I seek refuge from the punishment of Allah by turning to Him in repentance and seeking the means to what pleases Him. So show your kindness to the one who seeks it of you, grant guidance to the one who asks to be guided, and your Lord will reward you.

Salih said to him: If you do that, you will find Allah nearer in His pardon of you than your own repentance is to Him, for He loves those who turn to Him; and you will find me eager to guide you and watchful over you.

Then the eyes of Salih filled with tears at the mention of repentance, and he broke off his speech. He heartened them, and bade them go to his father's house. He himself went in to his father — the learned man who had first sent him out — and consulted him about the affair of Abu Malik and his companions.

The learned man said: You know your companions better than I. If you find good in them, guide them by your guidance; and if you have cause to warn them, do not let eagerness move you to be quick in punishing them. Test them by leaving them to themselves for a time, yet without harshness, and instruct your father to treat them well and to show them kindness in due season. For the one who longs for good cannot stay hidden, and Allah will not abandon you at your back. He will open for you, from the light of His enabling grace, that by which He orders the affairs of His creation, and from the gates of guidance that which will show you the deeds of the rightly guided.

And so, the account continues, Abu Malik and his companions passed through the working out of their trial until their affair came to completion, and they came to know their own guidance, and the guardian of Allah among them turned toward that guidance with tenderness. They gave thanks to Allah for it, and returned to their people as warners, and through them Allah guided many of His servants to His religion.

The author said: Whatever in this took the shape of a story was no idle invention, but a confirmation of what Allah has commanded; for in it lies the steadfastness of the messengers, the signs of the guardians, and the manners of the seekers.

Praise be to Allah, first and last. May Allah bless His Messenger, our master Muhammad — sent forth from Him to His creation as a bearer of good tidings and a warner — and bless his guardian, the Imam of the God-fearing, the radiant, blaze-marked leader (القائد الغرّ المحجّلين), the beloved of the Lord of the Worlds, and the Imams of the people of his house, upon whom Allah has bestowed His favor, from whom He has removed all defilement, and whom He has purified with a complete purifying. Allah is sufficient for us, and how excellent a Guardian; how excellent a Master, and how excellent a Helper. There is no power and no strength except by Allah, the Most High, the Most Great.
