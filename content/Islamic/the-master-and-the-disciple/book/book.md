# The Master and the Disciple

## How to Read a Conversation Made of Doors

This book is a translation of *Kitab al-Alim wa-l-Ghulam*, "The Book of the Master and the Boy", a teaching dialogue that the manuscript itself attributes to Jafar ibn Mansur al-Yaman (جعفر بن منصور اليمن) and that belongs to the Ismaili dawa (دعوة) tradition of the Fatimid period. It is not a treatise with a doctrine to state. It is a story that argues: a man seeks knowledge and finds it, a boy seeks him and is tested at the door, and almost everything the book teaches is taught inside a conversation between them.

Nobody narrates it in his own person. The book speaks as a transmitted report — "We have been informed" — and keeps that distance to the last page. When a first person appears, it belongs to whoever is speaking at that moment, not to a narrator and not to this edition.

Five figures carry the whole of it. **The Master** is a Persian who came to knowledge late, after years of ignorance, and who spends the rest of his life carrying it to others. **The boy**, the youngest and most thoughtful of a company that hears the Master speak, follows him, is refused, and is admitted only on conditions; his name, given much later, is Salih (صالح). **His father** is al-Bakhtari (البختري), a chief among the Arabs, honorable in lineage and hard against the people of religion. **The Shaykh** (الشيخ) is the greater teacher at the far end of the boy's journey, who tests him on his name and holds his naming back for seven days. **Abu Malik** (أبو مالك) is the scholar whom the grown Salih debates in the final chapter, and that debate is where the book puts its whole argument on trial.

One name has to be kept straight from the start. The pages immediately following this introduction — the book's own opening — begin with believers putting a question to "a Master among them". That teacher is not the Master of the story. He answers once and is not heard from again; everywhere after those opening pages, "the Master" means the Persian.

From the fourth chapter onward a set of terms recurs that a reader may take for ordinary religious vocabulary: the speaking Imam (الإمام الناطق), his gate (باب), his successor (الوصي), his summoners (الدعاة). The terms first appear in chapter four and the book glosses each when it names it, in chapter five. It sets them out as ranks in a single structure in chapter four itself — imam and gate and summoners figured as sun and moon and stars — so the cosmology of chapters four through six is describing that structure rather than decorating it.

As an edition, this one adds nothing. It translates the whole of the source without abridgment, keeps the Arabic the source carries with the English set beneath, and supplies the Arabic of Quranic verses the source quotes in translation only. Where the words are the book's own they are left unvowelled exactly as the scan leaves them; where they are Quran they carry the vowelling of the mushaf. The editorial notes and the notes to the reader are fenced and labeled, so a reader can always tell the book's voice from a note about it.

### The book's own opening

We have been informed that some groups among the believers, and a number of the preachers of religion, came to a Master among them and spoke as men speak when they have been given something they cannot easily name. "You have set us free," they told him, "by making us aware of what you have made us aware of, and in doing so you have laid upon us an obligation we are bound to fulfill." That obligation, as they understood it, fell into three parts, and each part was a debt of thanks: thanks for the summons itself, for that to which he had called them; thanks for the knowledge he had shown them; and thanks for the work he had commanded them to do.

So they asked him to make each of these plain. Show us, they said, from every one of them, what a person who wills to render such thanks may actually do. They did not ask out of ignorance of the shape of the thing, for they had already understood, from the limits of religion, what is obligatory upon them and what falls to them to perform: what belongs to the student in the framing of his question, and what is required of the one asked in the giving of his answer. And they had understood, too, from the doctrines of the righteous and the manners of the seekers, as much as is easy to put into words. What remained was to be told how thanks is turned from a feeling into a practice.

The Master answered them by first setting the whole matter in its proper light. The affair to which I have called you, he said, is the one I hold in honor. May Allah (الله) be with His servants, and may He bring it to completion for them, and ennoble those who answer His call. Then he opened the structure of it, and it was here that the conversation revealed itself to be built of thresholds, each thing giving onto another.

He has made for the first of every thing a last, and for the last of every thing an end, and for every end a further end whose limit is never reached. These are the stations of those who possess understanding, the houses through which the traveler passes:

> مفاتيحها ذكرى، وأولها بلوى، وأوسطها هدى، وآخرها تقوى

Their keys are remembrance: the first of them is affliction, the middle of them is guidance, and the last of them is piety.

To read the conversation rightly, then, is to read it as a passage through these houses in order. Whoever has set himself to know the structure of things, and has taken his stand upon the limits of obligation that lie within his reach, has already turned his face toward guidance by the very features of the request. He has entered upon the road, and he walks it in the best of ways. The seeking is not separate from the arrival; the shape of a sincere question is itself the first motion toward the answer.

Only after establishing this did the Master return to the three thanks and give each its practical form, so that none of them should remain a mere sentiment.

As for thanking the Master, it is to obey him. The gratitude owed to the one who guides is not spoken but enacted, and its whole substance is obedience.

As for thanking the knowledge, it is twofold: to act upon what is in it, and to call others to it. Knowledge that is received and hoarded has not been thanked; it is honored only when it is lived and then passed onward.

And as for thanking the work, it too is twofold: to be patient with it, and to call others to it. The labor that has been laid upon a person is repaid by endurance in it and by summoning others to share in it, so that the work does not end with the one who first took it up.

Thus each of the three debts, which had seemed at first like feelings too large to discharge, was shown to have a door a person could actually walk through: obedience, action and summons, patience and summons. The conversation that began as a question about how to give thanks ended as an opening onto the road itself.

## 1. The Persian Who Was Dead and Revived

As for the doctrines of the righteous and the manners of those who seek: it has reached us that there was a man among the people of Persia over whom the decree had run, leaving him in an affliction of ignorance. He was not without gifts. He possessed a heart of keen retention, a sound and lofty intellect, and a refinement of manners he had won for himself. Yet ignorance had gained the upper hand over him in the very knowledge we have been describing, for it had come upon him early, and he had grown up within it. So he wandered about in the heat of his thirst, taking the shimmer of the mirage for the whiteness of water, until, when at last he reached it, he found it to be nothing at all, and found Allah (الله) there before him, and his account was settled. Then his Lord honored his resting place and lifted from him the covering that lay over him. He found him astray and guided him, found him in want and enriched him with knowledge, so that by that favor he became one of the scholars of Tur (الطور) and one of the dwellers of the Bayt al-Mamur (البيت المعمور).

When his guidance was made complete and he had attained the utmost of his hope, thanks fell due from him to the One who had made him, and it became his charge to labor toward his Lord. Now he had heard from his father a saying that lived in him as the soul lives in the body:

> إن أفضل الحسنات إحياء الأموات
>
> "The best of good deeds is the reviving of the dead."

So he said within himself: I was dead and He revived me, ignorant and He taught me. I was not the first to be ignorant, that I should be the one who outran all people to knowledge, nor am I the last who will be ignorant, that teaching should come to an end with me. It is a truth binding upon me that I be among those who give thanks for this blessing, delivering this trust to those who come after me just as it was delivered to me by those who came before. For this affair belongs first of all to Allah, and it reached me only through many hands, the first passing it to the second and the second to the third, until, having descended from the highest assembly to the creatures who are the people of this world, it tore through the veil, and the doors gave way before it one after another, until it came at last to me.

Am I, then, its end and its final destination? Never. Those who carried it and kept faith with it have the prior claim upon its gains and its merits. And if their gains are not mine, that I might lean upon them, then I am rather the one who has been gained for them by their labor. And if their bounty does not excuse me from labor of my own, then I remain as poor before the seeking of knowledge through action as I once was poor before the seeking of knowledge itself. This he said within himself, and he knew that what was owed from him was the very likeness of what had been owed to him, and that the last of his affair, in the reckoning, was as its first.

So he went out from his family and his wealth toward his Lord, answering His call and praying for the good, summoning others to the same obligation, that there might fall due to him of thanksgiving the like of what had fallen due from him. He set out journeying through the lands, passing among the peoples of the non-Arabs and the tribes of the Arabs, searching their faces and laying his questions before them. Yet he found none to answer and met no fellow seeker, until he came to the farthest reach of the island. There he entered one of its cities at a moment when its people were heedless. And as he walked, recalling the signs of his Lord and studying their marks, his eye fell upon a company of the people of that land quarreling over religion without guidance and plunging into the depths of their own caprice. So he sat down a little apart from them, holding himself aside, listening to their talk and reading their faces to see which among them held to the soundest way.

## 2. A Stranger in the City

As he watched them, he said to himself: these are the foremost among men, for three qualities show even in the way they dispute. The first is that they carry an argument through to its end. The second is that they belong to the people of the Umma. And the third is that they stand nearer to right guidance, for they are alert to religion, keeping it together and searching after it together; and whoever seeks a thing is on the verge of finding it.

When they had finished the discussion they were engaged in, they turned toward him and asked, "Who are you, young man, and where are you from?"

He answered: "I am a servant of Allah (الله), and one of the dwellers in His sanctuary."

They said: "What is your need, and what is your occupation?"

He said: "My need has slipped past me, and my occupation is to seek it."

They said: "Did anything in what we said please you?"

He said: "All of it is strange, to one who marvels."

They said: "Wonder is of two kinds, one praiseworthy and one blameworthy."

He said: "Yes, I have learned that. And speech, likewise, is either praiseworthy or blameworthy."

They said: "Which kind is the praiseworthy, in your view?"

He said: "The kind that is correct."

They said: "And what is the correct?"

He said: "That whose origin is from Allah, and which calls to Allah."

They said: "And what is the blameworthy?"

He said: "That whose origin is caprice, and which calls to something other than guidance."

They said: "You speak truly. Have you nothing of your own for us to hear?"

He said: "I have nothing to say on my own account; I am only a follower of the true saying."

They said: "Then let us hear from your words what will show us your way. Your manner has been sweet to us and your fellowship pleasant to us, and we hope that you may be a door of mercy that Allah has opened for us."

He said: "Yes." Then he composed himself to speak; the company fell silent before him, and he turned his face toward them.

Then he said: "He who created the creation by His power set each thing in due measure to what He decreed for it. It is He who laid open the broad highways of the pasture and made their outward and their inward free of caprice — a way that is neither clouded with ambiguity nor worn away to nothing. Were it otherwise, imaginings would lose their way in the quest for Him and minds would fail to reach Him. Yet He does not step forth into visible being, that His attributes might be claimed by another or the goal of His path be turned aside. Rather He is generous and hidden: His knowledge is veiled by its very grace, and His secrets are kept for those to whom they belong. Its honor is the plumage of guidance, and its most perfect adornment is the garment of piety, marked with the light of lights, laid up as a trust in the breasts of the chosen and the righteous — the keepers of the Word, the noble scribes,

"a trust which they discharge and a limit which they do not overstep. It is the law of the Judge and the bounties of the Most Merciful. He ennobled it and set it within, He guarded it and veiled it, He gave it its due and crowned it with His covenants. So the resolves of the thinkers do not reach it, and the schemes of the schemers do not lay hold of it. Its outward is a remembrance and a proof for the one who is guided; its inward is a clear-flowing drink, whose heaven is brought near to those who gaze upon it and whose earth is made level for those who search, with stations marked out for those who seek. It has watering-places for those who journey toward Him, and signs whose wonders follow one upon another, pointing by their likenesses toward Him. Its course sets forth excellence, and its method lays it out in detail; its rulings are obligations and its practices ordained ways. Truthfulness has ennobled its schools and justice has made wholesome its gains, so that for every good it is a leader and a road, and to its inner sanctum a shield and a guide. Then minds were driven, by ignorance and by need, to seek knowledge and sufficiency."

> فتبارك الَّذِي جَعَلَ اللَّيْلَ وَالنَّهَارَ خِلْفَةً لِمَنْ أَرَادَ أَنْ يَذَّكَّرَ أَوْ أَرَادَ شُكُورًا، وصلى الله على من اختاره من عباده وجعله للعالمين نذيراً.

"Blessed, then, is He who has made the night and the day a succession for whoever would take heed or would give thanks; and may Allah bless the one He chose from among His servants and made a warner to the worlds."

Then he said: "Fear Allah with the fear of one who dreads Him, and dread Him with the dread of one who hopes in Him. Seek knowledge, and you will come to know; and act, and you will be shown mercy."

## 3. The Boy at the Door — Limits and Conditions

His eyes brimmed over with tears, and he broke off his words. He took his leave of the people, rose, and set out upon his journey. They wept at the warmth and the eloquence of his sermon and longed to rise and go with him; but out of courtesy they held back, unsure of what accompanying him would ask of them, and so they returned to their houses.

One of the company followed him: a youth, the youngest of them in years and the most thoughtful of them in mind, whose training had perfected his intellect and whose reflection had filled his heart. All of this was joined to what he had heard from the Master, to what had been laid before him and granted to him. The boy had a father who was a great chief of the Arabs, the most honorable of them in lineage and the harshest of them against the people of religion. So, with the Master's leave, the boy kept his company until the Master reached the end of his journey. The Master entered his lodging and brought the boy in with him, then called for food. When they had finished and had settled into quiet talk, the boy spoke.

"O wise one," he said, "you have spoken and I have heard; you have preached and I have been instructed. You adorned the religion with the finest ornament, made it desired with the best of longing and the fullest of provision, and called to it by the noblest of its trusts. You made minds yearn to question you by the blows your words struck upon the hearts. Such excellence could come only from one whose inward knowledge has been made whole and whose outward forbearance has grown wide, for he is a fountain of wisdom springing from a firm root whose height towers over the worlds, and from whose branches hang fruits for those who seek. I hope that you are one of those blessed branches, for I see in you noble judgment, a sound core, and a perfect intellect. You have said that minds are driven by ignorance to seek knowledge and capacity, and my mind is among the neediest of them and the most destitute. Is there for me a way to life?"

The Master said: "He who created the creatures by His power did not create them in vain, nor did He make them scholars from the outset, nor does He abandon them to wander in ignorance, nor is He content that stupidity be their culture or the garments of ignorance their clothing. He has appointed for them a way out of all of this, by the very faculties with which He composed their frame and completed their creation. He made their hearing, their eyes, and their hearts filled with lights, an honor from Him to them and a proof standing against them. Then He placed the treasures of His wisdom and the marks of His will within the paths that please Him among His chosen creatures. He charged them with religion and called them to it, and laid upon them the duty of answering by the faculties we have named, so that God might sift the corrupt from the pure. Thus the seeker is made able by his seeking, and the ignorant is exposed by his falling behind, so that no argument remains against Allah (الله) after His Messengers, and no excuse remains for going astray after the evidence has come."

The boy said: "The absence of any excuse has frightened me, and the presence of the evidence has made me long for it. I beg you for a guide, and I will be your servant."

The Master said: "God did not create men already scholars. He created them as small children, ignorant, knowing nothing. None is great except after being small, and none is a scholar except after ignorance. Smallness is the prelude to greatness, and ignorance is a guide toward knowledge. In the same way, knowledge cannot be borne except by the introduction of a lesser knowledge before it, one swifter to the heart and smoother to the understanding, so that its beginnings may settle in the mind, the limbs may be humbled to its duties, the breast may tremble at its depth, and the intellect may carry its purpose through to the end. This is the way of Allah with His servants. Let us not depart from the community by our own opinion, nor follow with our guidance anything other than the established way."

The boy said: "Then I am too small for what I want, so raise me; I am ignorant of what I seek, so teach me; I am bound by your bounty, so have mercy on me. You yourself were once in this very condition, and God led His favor to you by the hands of one whose trial before you was great and whose thanks became due upon you. I know the state of one who suffers the narrowness of ignorance and the need that comes of a feeble resourcefulness."

The Master said: "The state you describe is indeed as you say, yet I will not show you mercy by doing for you what your own effort ought to do. Every claim is accepted only when it is truthful; knowledge does not profit until it is acted upon, and action does not profit until it is accepted. The truthfulness of speech is its justice, and the proof of knowledge lies in the open roads of deeds and the documented examples that have endured across the lands, over which night and day have passed in turn. Do not pursue an argument heedlessly, do not dismiss one heedlessly, and do not accept one upon mere imitation, lest the first doubt that rises against it tear it from your heart. Do not reject a proof once it stands firm, or you will be an advocate for falsehood; and do not abandon inquiry into it and understanding of it, or your judgment will be barren. Think well of me without holding back, strive in your questioning without suspicion, seek the religion as one like you should seek it, and ask God's help in your affair. God does not let the reward of the seeker go to waste, nor does He deny recompense to anyone He sees seeking the good."

The boy said: "You have dealt justly with yourself in a way both fitting and effective, and you have set forth the truth in the best of speech. I ask God's help, and I ask you about this matter to which you call me: what is it, whose is it, and to whom does it belong?"

The Master said: "It is the religion of Allah, which He has approved, and which is from Him, so that none but it is accepted from Him. He delivered it first to the elite among His Messengers, then in general to all His creation. Upon the one specially favored is thanks for it, action upon it, and the call of others to it; upon the one deprived of it is the seeking after it until he knows, and obedience to the one who guides him."

The boy said: "This favor that Allah has singled out for His friends—are all such favors equal in their excellence, or do some surpass others?"

The Master said: "Have we not said that every knowledge has a knowledge before it that taught it, and that each bearer carried his own portion, so that they stand at graded ranks before their Lord, each charged only with what he acquired of knowledge? Then each possessor of merit is given his merit, in justice from God toward His creation and in mercy toward His servants."

The boy said: "This to which you call me—is it that favor itself, or is it something other than it?"

The Master said: "It is neither the whole of it nor wholly other than it. It is drawn from it, as a single cup of water is drawn from the breadth of the Euphrates. I call you to what Allah has approved for His servants and honored on the day He perfected it: precious, guarded, generous, hidden. Its honor, through its reward, is dignity for the one who upholds it; its retribution is debasement for the one who lets it fall. Its outward form is figures, and its inner reality is knowledge. Its ruling is a light, and its justice is plain. This is what I call you to."

The boy said: "What has made you more entitled to this matter than others, and more deserving of it than the rest, when you claim it is from God for all creation, so that everyone lays claim to it against everyone else? What has made you more worthy of the name of truth than they, when this matter reached you only from where it reached them?"

The Master said: "There is no scholar who utters a word of truth but that he has taken it from the very roots from which we ourselves have taken; in this they agree with us, by an acknowledgment from us and from them. What then divided us from them was their departure from those roots while we held fast, our obedience where they disobeyed, our safeguarding of what we were entrusted with where they squandered it, our fulfillment of what we were charged with where they neglected it. They agree with us in acknowledgment and differ from us in deed. Their acknowledgment is a witness for us against them, and their loss is the sign of their poverty toward us and their need of what is in our hands.

"They are like brothers who inherited great wealth from their father and divided it equally between them. Some squandered their share until they fell into poverty, while others kept theirs, preserved it, traded with it, and multiplied the wealth in their hands. Then the poor among the brothers turned to the wealthier ones, to serve them and to seek their bounty. So too those who lost the way of their Messengers were driven by their ignorance to seek knowledge from the people of the noble roots. The proof that we are more deserving of the truth than they is their need of us and our sufficiency without them, so that our learning has become the answer to their ignorance, our garment covers their nakedness, and our abundance satisfies their hunger. Thus the truth is binding upon the one who would be virtuous, the credit belongs to the one who confers favor, and thanks is owed to the one who bestows kindness."

The boy said: "As for your superiority over them, that has become clear, and as for the argument of truth, it stands against every honest seeker who lets it slip. But what argument have you against me, when I already agree with what you have said, stand by what you have described, deny what you have denied, and know the truth and its worth as well as falsehood and its people?"

The Master said: "My argument against you is the same as my argument against them, since you differ from them in description but agree with them in meaning."

The boy said: "How is that, when I acknowledge the truth to its owner while they deny it?"

The Master said: "If your acknowledgment sets you apart from them where they deny, you still join them in withholding where they withhold. What use is acknowledgment without a turning to act upon it, and what harm is denial when the setting itself is right?"

The boy said: "Then bring me to the people of truth that I may know them, or to an agent who can establish it for them. Show me which of the two you are able to do."

The Master said: "Do you not see that you have been driven to need the proof of the rightful owner of the truth, or of his agent, just as the common people have been driven to need a proof of the truth itself? Deed has parted you from them, even as ignorance has joined you to them."

The boy said: "You have spoken truly, and the matter has become clear to me."

The Master said: "Have I not firmly established my argument against you and against the common people?"

"Yes," he said.

The Master said: "Can anyone establish for himself a truth that belongs to another?"

"No one is able to do that," he said.

The Master said: "Then the argument stands against you and against the common people, and nothing remains but submission and a turning toward the one whom you cannot do without and who has no need of you."

The boy said: "As for my poverty, I acknowledge it. As for the One who is rich while I am in need, I ask of Him, and I ask Him for mercy."

The Master said: "Have you seen anyone driven by poverty to a man of bounty obtain anything from him except by earning or by gift?"

The boy said: "Yes. Nothing is obtained from him except by earning or by gift."

The Master said: "And do the people of gift attain what belongs to the people of earning by way of earning?"

"They do not," he said.

The Master said: "Then seek this matter by work, and you will attain it as a thing deserved. Do not seek it by flattery, or you will attain it as a mere gift and so be left deprived."

The boy said: "You are the key of good and its guide. Show me the more meaningful of the two paths and the nearer to guidance. The greatness of my need of you has bound me fast to you."

The Master said: "O my son, there is no distance between us and no blame upon you. What you have hoped from me will reach you. But it has limits that are not to be crossed and conditions that are not to be forgotten."

The boy said: "Set whatever limits you love, and lay down whatever conditions you see fit. I hope you will find me patient under your trial and grateful for your kindness."

The Master said: "The first of the limits is to uphold the outward meaning of the Book and its laws, and to act upon what you know of it. For whoever acts for God according to what he knows, God will guide him to what he does not know."

> فإنه من عمل لله بما يعلم، هداه الله إلى ما لا يعلم.
>
> For whoever acts for God according to what he knows, God will guide him to what he does not know.

The boy said: "But the Books are many, and all of them are from Allah. For each Book there is a scholar among the creation who holds fast to it, and all of them are agreed upon safeguarding it and upon the religion it carries."

The Master said: "We have come back to our earlier words, and now we must settle what should never have needed settling."

The boy said: "How is that?"

The Master said: "By your saying that the Books are many, that all of them are from Allah, and that each has a scholar of its own. Do you then suppose that the words of God and His Books cancel one another, that the first of them gives the lie to the last and the last to the first?"

The boy said: "I do not suppose that. But what is the proof?"

The Master said: "Had the creation acted upon what is in the first Book, it would have pointed them to the second; and had they acted upon the second, it would have pointed them to the third, until the work culminates in the last of them. The last is more entitled to be followed than what came before it, even though all of them are from Allah, because the last is nearer to the present age and clearer in its way, since it confirms and supersedes what came before it, and nothing came after it to supersede it."

The boy said: "You have spoken truly and made plain the knowledge of the truth and the mark of truthfulness. I will hold to the limits you have commanded me. So what are your conditions upon me?"

The Master said: "My conditions upon you are five. Do not fail me if I summon you. Do not conceal anything from me if I ask you. Do not seek me out until I answer you. Do not ask me for anything until I begin with you. And do not mention my affair to your father."

The boy said: "I will do all that you have named, and it is easy for me set against the greatness of what I hope from you. But how shall I contrive with my father? He is among the heaviest of trials upon me and the sorest that I bear. Will you permit me to remove myself from him?"

The Master said: "Withdrawing from him would be a sign to others. Rather, remain with him, treat him well, and please him with some measure of outward agreement. Keep your secret from him and conceal your affair, for God will suffice us against his evil and the evil of others."

The narrator said: Then the Master and the boy would meet and part for a stretch of their days, the boy perplexed in his affair, knowing nothing but what the law had brought him to stand upon, unable to find where the Master had settled, and unable to seek him out because of the condition laid upon him, while the term lengthened and the Master watched his patience and his endurance of what had been asked of him. At last, on a quiet night when the creation was still and the spies had grown heedless, the two settled together in their assembly. The boy felt his need drawing near and the chance of privacy with his companion at hand, and he fell down before him in prostration. When he raised his head, the Master spoke.

The Master said to him: "I saw you prostrate in thanksgiving. Thanksgiving before the deed breeds suspicion, and thanksgiving cut short after the deed makes the deeds themselves void."

The boy said: "All your words are truth, and all your deeds are mastery. I thanked you only for the kindness that has already come from you, and by that thanks I hoped for more from you."

The Master said: "Thanksgiving is fitting, for gratitude is what makes thanksgiving increase. Your right has been fulfilled. So come to me with your heart, make your understanding present, and listen to what is set before you."

The boy said: "I have brought the instrument of understanding and taken up the tool of hearing. Say what seems good to you."

The Master said: "The religion has a key that opens it and closes it, like that which distinguishes forbidden union from lawful marriage."

The boy said: "This key you have named is great in the sight of Allah, since it is what parts the lawful from the unlawful and truth from falsehood. What is it?"

The Master said: "It is the covenant of Allah, which establishes His rights, gathers His obligations, and makes the Garden binding for His friends. It is the rope of God upon His earth and the safe haven among His servants. I will recite it to you and bind you to it."

The boy said: "Yes. Take from me whatever you love. I do not refuse your opinion, and I do not overstep your command."

The narrator said: Then the Master set about reciting the covenant to the boy, conveying it to him and binding him fast by it, while the boy could not contain himself for awe, his tears streaming from the depth of his instruction, until he reached the end of the covenant. He praised God and glorified Him and thanked Him for what had been granted him, and he knew with certainty that, by accepting their pledge, he had entered the Party of God (حزب الله) and the company of His friends. Then he fell silent in his composure, and the Master began to expound and to make clear.

## 4. How the World Was Made

<!-- bridge:end -->

The Master began with the first of the causes of the manifest order and with the beginning of its creation. God, he said, is the Creator of things and the One who composes them; He created them and brought them into being. He is not the first of created things in the sense of having taken some earlier creation as evidence for what came after; rather He is the First of all firsts and the Inheritor of every thing. He began the creation of what He created out of light, and from that light three words branched. The first is the will; from the will branched the command; and from the command branched the saying, so that He says to whatever He wills:

> كُنْ فَيَكُونُ
>
> "Be," and it is.

So the first of creation is the will, then the command, then the saying.

These are three words. From them is derived كُنْ, which is two letters, and فَيَكُونُ, which is five — seven letters in all, and from them seven things branch. Out of the light He created air, the first of them: singular, without consort, unique among things, a mixture that surrounds all of them yet is surrounded by none. It is a proof of the One of whom it is said:

> لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ
>
> There is nothing like Him.

Then, out of those three words, He created water, darkness, and light. Out of the water He created smoke, out of the heat of the darkness condensation, and out of the light fire. These are three things branching from three things before them, and each became a consort to its origin, since it was created from it. The seventh, air, encompasses them all. This was the beginning of creation and the beginning of things.

When creation had been established upon seven principles, and the path of forms had been laid down according to likeness and analogy, He created out of the smoke seven heavens and their signs, and out of the condensation seven earths and their powers. These seven branch from those seven origins.

We turn next to the signs of the heavens and the powers of the earth, asking what they are and what bears witness to them. Here we return to the three words and their letters, from which the seven were created — namely, the will to command by saying. That is twelve letters. Their signs in heaven are the twelve constellations, and their witnesses are seven kingdoms; their signs upon the earth are twelve islands, and their proofs are seventeen seas. These are the signs of the heavens and the earth, set before us, which we now pass over.

Then the heavens and the earth were veiled in darkness by the black heat, which is the authority of the earth. Then He pierced that darkness with His lights, so that light and darkness, the two distinct realities, were intermingled. He set them apart by the night and the day and made them two signs. He made the night and made its sign a course of darkness, and apportioned it; and He made the day and made its sign a course of light, and apportioned it. He adorned the night with its darkness, its sign the moon and the stars, and made the day shine with its light, gleaming through the sign of the sun. Then out of the two the days were brought forth — states passing between night and day — and they are seven days. Each day of them has twelve hours, and each night of them has twelve hours.

When the creation of things had been joined to these principles, He made them pairs, male and female. I do not mean male and female in the literal sense, but in the sense of the active and the receptive. Then the sunna (السنة) of creation in pairs went forth, and out of its essences He created every living thing: humans, beasts, birds, and crawling creatures. Then this sunna extended into speech, and speech too became pairs — adjectives and things described, names and meanings.

> فَسُبْحَانَ الَّذِي خَلَقَ الْأَزْوَاجَ كُلَّهَا
>
> Glory be to Him who created all the pairs.

He made them manifestations of His command, exemplars of His religion, and signs of His power, pointing through things to the One who brings them into being, and through the creation to its Maker.

Then He pierced it with His light, opened it with His command, created it by His knowledge, and sealed it, among His names, with the letters of:

> بِسْمِ اللَّهِ الرَّحْمَٰنِ الرَّحِيمِ
>
> In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful.

When He had completed them all by His making and composed them in pairs by His measure, He chose for Himself, from the choicest of His will and the secrets of His unseen, an abundant knowledge and a clear light, and made the two of them a religion by which God is served and through which nearness to Him is sought. Then He honored that religion with laws and with books, made it manifest through parables and through the sunna, and raised up for it, from the best of His creation, banners and signs and rulers. He made them the houses of His prophethood, the treasuries of His knowledge and His wisdom, and the interpreters of His revelation, and He appointed them His successors in His earth as guides upon the path of salvation, in what is apparent and in what is hidden. Among them are the imams and the speakers; among them are the arguments and the chiefs; and among them are the summoners to the good, and the learned. In their stations they are like the stars of heaven, by whom works are set aright and in whom the parables reach their end. Their imam is like the great sun among the lights, veiled from sight by his own splendor; he is their apparent one and their likeness. The argument, the gate of their imam, is like the shining moon, its appearance and its likeness. Their summoners are like the radiant stars, their appearances and their likenesses.

All the pairs I have described to you are apparent and inward, figure and thing figured. The apparent of the seven heavens are parables, and their inner meaning is the seven speakers, the messengers of firm resolve. The seven earths are outwardly parables, and their inner meaning is the imams of guidance. For each speaker among them there are twelve chiefs, and their like from the seven heavens are the twelve constellations. Likewise, each of the imams of guidance has twelve arguments through which the case of God is pressed upon his people, and their like in the earth are the twelve islands, the dwellings of the chiefs, and on every island stands an argument. For every argument there are summoners, many and few, for He increases in creation whatever He wills, that the argument of God may reach His creation:

> لِئَلَّا يَكُونَ لِلنَّاسِ عَلَى اللَّهِ حُجَّةٌ بَعْدَ الرُّسُلِ
>
> That mankind may have no argument against God after the messengers.

Through these causes — the causes of God that I have described to you — the argument of God reaches His creation, and His justice appears among them, outwardly and inwardly. The inner is the religion of God, by which the friends of God (أولياء الله) are bound; and the outer is the laws of the religion and their likenesses. So the religion became a soul and a spirit to the laws, and the laws became a body and a guide to the religion. The body has no standing except by the soul, for the soul is its life; and the soul has no standing except by the body, for the body is its instrument. So it is with the apparent laws and all else: they have no standing except by the inner religion, for that is their light, their meaning, and the spirit of life within them; and the inner has no standing except by the apparent, for the apparent is its body and its proof. The apparent is the knowledge of this world, which can be seen only by it; and the inner is the knowledge of the hereafter, which can be seen only by it. There is not a single letter among the letters of the inner, nor a single one of the friends of God, but that its witness in the apparent is manifold, because of the abundance of the parables and the breadth of the laws.

Speech on this grows long, and the more one answers of it from the words of wisdom, the more its truth is hidden, and its end makes one forget its beginning — for its preciousness and the purity of its essence. For the light of wisdom overwhelms one part of itself with another, as the light of the sun overwhelms the light of the moon and the stars and dims them. So too do the words of wisdom adorn the tongue of the wise.

## 5. The World, the Hereafter, and the Speech of Parables

The boy said: "This world, in which so vast a creation was set down, holds a great multitude, the first of them and the last, and they differ concerning it. Some have given themselves over to it and pressed hard in its pursuit, seeing merit in nothing but the gathering of more. Others reproach it and practice renunciation within it, yet cannot do without it — even though all that is forbidden is much, while only a little of the world is forbidden. From you I have heard neither mention of it nor report. What, then, do you say of it?"

The Master said: "As for this world, no one will ever truly reproach it, neither the learned man nor the ignorant. The ignorant man knows its worth by the very ignorance that keeps everything beyond it hidden from him; the learned man knows its worth through the worth of its meaning. None reproaches the world but an ignorant man. And how could the creation ever be permitted to reproach the act of the Creator? What I say, rather, is that the world was set up, for the one who would see, upon seven things branching from seven origins: heaven, earth, darkness, light, vessels, prosperity, and misery. These are seven qualities, and from each of the seven there branch seven more.

(a) The heavens are seven.

(b) The earths are seven.

(c) The light has seven days that turn upon seven nights. Saturday is for repose and rest; Sunday is for the limits and the sunna (السنة); Monday is for the pairings within the great meaning, al-Kabir (الكبير); Tuesday has three houses — an apparent, an inward, and an inward of the subtle; Wednesday is for the four, which is the sanctuary; Thursday is the fifth of the causes of God; and Friday is the greatest law of God.

(d) The darkness is the seven nights, a covering of paradise over the seven days, a night for each day.

(e) The vessels are seven: camels, horses, mules, donkeys, elephants, oxen, and ships. By these seven the open reaches of the earth are crossed. Camels are for the sands, horses for the plains, mules for the mountains, donkeys for near destinations, elephants for the marshes and the wastelands, oxen for the salt-flats and the thickets, and ships for the seas. Seven mounts for seven domains.

(f) The blessings of this world rest upon seven: air, water, light, darkness, food, clothing, and marriage. The air is the open space between heaven and earth, in which the winds arise and the breaths pass to and fro, and it is the life of every living thing. Water is the life of the animal. Light is the substance of sight; darkness, the increase of what grows; food, the sustenance of bodies; clothing, a shield that veils what is shameful; and marriage, the seed of reproduction and the cause of offspring. Some of these are the keys of the good, and some the mines of blessings. Which of them is blameworthy, when all of them are among the gifts of God and His blessings, for which His servants are bound to give thanks?

(g) Their opposites in misery are also seven: heat, cold, hunger, thirst, fear, sickness, and death. These are seven things that must be borne with patience, once their meaning is known."

The boy said: "These are the attributes of this world, and I do not doubt that anything in it is other than the making and composition of the Creator. So what do the scholars intend when they censure the world, and what do the ignorant intend when they praise it? I see that the ignorant have arrived at the meaning, even while ignorant of it. And if that is so, the rightness of their case would seem to lay error upon the scholars, since the scholars contradict the people of ignorance in every sense."

The Master said: "The scholars did not err in censuring this world, but the creation does not know what they mean by it. And the ignorant did not perceive the meaning of what they praised in the world, for they were ignorant of its meaning. Whoever is ignorant of a thing is merely accustomed to it."

The boy said: "Show me the truth of what the scholars say, that I may rightly hold them true. And mark out the speech of the people of ignorance by what exposes its falsehood, since the outward appearance points the other way. My breast has grown weary from ignorance of this, and I hope for no relief except through its knowledge."

The Master said: "This world, and all that I have described to you of it, is an apparent standing over an inward. It has no subsistence except through that inward, for the inward is to it as the soul is to the body. Whoever knows the soul, for him the body is made wholesome; and whoever does not know the soul but acknowledges only the body dwells upon a corpse, and the corpse is forbidden. From here the scholars forbade the world, because it is dead. They forbade the dead thing that God has forbidden — until it be taken from where God wills.

> من حيث يشاء الله. وبان كذب أهل الجهل بمدح الدنيا إذ لم يعرفوا باطنها وما أراد الله بها. وظنوا أن الله خلقها لغير معناها، والله لم يخلق الأشياء عبثاً. ولو كانت الدنيا وما فيها لم يخلق إلا للدنيا، ينشئ فيها خلقاً

From where God wills. So the falsehood of the people of ignorance in praising the world stood plain, for they did not know its inward nor what God intended by it. They imagined that God created it for a meaning other than its own, yet God did not create things in vain. Had the world and all within it been created for the world alone, He would have brought forth within it a creation that fades away, with no consequence and no meaning. Every act without consequence is play, and every speech without meaning is falsehood, and God is far too exalted for that."

The boy said: "The truthfulness of the scholars is established, their merit and excellence are proven, and the people of ignorance stand apart, their falsehood laid bare by what has appeared of the argument. There remains for us the knowledge of the inward of the world and of what is in it. I see now that the distress I complained of to you has had its narrowness lifted away."

The Master said: "What is the reward of the one who brought you out from the narrowness of poverty into the breadth of ease?"

He said: "Thanks and praise."

The Master said: "And what is the reward of the one who brought you out from the narrowness of ignorance into the breadth of knowledge?"

He said: "Obedience and action."

The Master said: "This world and all its likes are an apparent standing over the hereafter and what is in it, for the hereafter is its soul and its life. Whoever labors in the world for the world, without knowledge of the hereafter, his labor is misguidance, for it has no meaning and no consequence. But whoever labors in the world for the hereafter, believing in it, his labor has meaning and consequence, and his labor is met with thanks."

The boy said: "You have spoken truly. So make plain to me the features of this world and what is in it, and set the parables before me against the things they represent, drawn from their inward."

The Master said: "God, blessed and exalted, has created nothing among the things of this world — no beast upon the earth, no bird flying on its wings, nothing moist nor dry, no fixed and inanimate thing such as mountains, stones, trees, and the minerals of gold, silver, and gem, nor anything at all, great or small — except that it is a parable struck for a meaning.

> وجميع الأشياء ما كبر منها وما صغر، إلا وهو مثل مضروب. ألا ترى الذرّة على صغرها ولطف معناها لها حدود، أمامها وخلفها ويمينها وشمالها وفوقها وتحتها؟ فذلك ستة حدود وهي الحدّ السابع. فكذلك جميع ما خلق الله من الأشياء

Do you not see that the mote, for all its smallness and the subtlety of its meaning, has limits — before it and behind it, to its right and its left, above it and below it? Those are six limits, and it is itself the seventh. So too is everything God has created, from the mote and the gnat up to what is above them of the heaven and the earth, and all that lies between them, great and small, upon this same pattern. Each thing has six limits and is itself the seventh, and this points to the six origins from which things were created, while the air that encompasses them is the seventh that holds them all. If your breast is wide enough for these questions, and for looking into the parables and matching each thing to its counterpart among the inward pairs, then ask of whatever appears to you."

The boy said: "What is the likeness of the heaven, in its height above things and the breadth of its regions, so that it has gathered all things within it?"

The Master said: "Its likeness is the speaking Imam (الإمام الناطق), who is raised above all creation by his merit and the loftiness of his degrees, and who has gathered within him all the rulings — the rulings of the Sharia (الشريعة) — by the breadth of his knowledge."

The boy said: "What is the likeness of the zodiac and the rest of its stars?"

The Master said: "The likeness of the twelve chiefs (النقباء), the chiefs of the speaking Imam. Its stars are his summoners (الدعاة), who call to the good by the leave of their Lord."

The boy said: "What is the likeness of the earth and its breadth, made a place of rest and a bed for this creation?"

The Master said: "It is the likeness of the gate (باب) of the speaking Imam in his lifetime, and of his successor (الوصي) after his death. It is a place of return for the people and a resting-place for the one who seeks guidance, and it is knowing of the parables and the inward interpretation (التأويل)."

The boy said: "What is the likeness of the twelve islands in the earth?"

The Master said: "They are parables of the twelve arguments (الحجج), the arguments of the guardians."

The boy said: "What is the likeness of its rivers and its springs?"

The Master said: "The likeness of those who call to the good."

The boy said: "What is the likeness of the seven kingdoms of the heavens and their orbits?"

The Master said: "They are the causes between God and the speaker (الناطق), and they are His. These are the seven and the two orbits, and these are the greatest causes of God."

The boy said: "What is the likeness of the seven seas?"

The Master said: "They are the causes between God and the imams of the speaker, below those first causes."

The boy said: "What is the likeness of the salt water and the sweet, and the difference between them upon the earth?"

The Master said: "The salt water is the likeness of the knowledge of the apparent, in which there is no summons and no summoners, just as the salt water yields neither crop nor anything else. The sweet water is the likeness of the knowledge of the inward, whether as springs within the earth or as water flowing over it. So too is knowledge: the inward is the knowledge of the hidden, while there is an apparent by which the summoners run their course, and an inward that is kept concealed."

The boy said: "How did water come to be a likeness of knowledge?"

The Master said: "Because water is the life of every living thing, and likewise knowledge is the life of every knower."

The boy said: "What is the likeness of the air that encompasses all things?"

The Master said: "The air is too great to be set as a parable for any thing it might represent."

The boy said: "Is it greater, or are the heaven and the earth as great as it, since they are made parables?"

The Master said: "Glory be to God, it is greater than both. The breadth of the earth within the atmosphere is as a ring cast upon an open plain; the breadth of the atmosphere within the heaven is as a ring cast upon an open plain; and the breadth of the heaven and its regions within the breadth of the air is as an egg upon an open plain. What thing represented could ever grasp this and serve as an evidence and a likeness for it?"

The boy said: "What, then, is its meaning?"

The Master said: "It is an evidence pointing to the One who has nothing like Him, glorified and exalted above what they associate with Him."

The boy said: "But did the Creator not say:

> فَلَا تَضْرِبُوا لِلَّهِ الْأَمْثَالَ

So do not strike parables for God?"

The Master said: "And did He not also say:

> وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى

And to God belongs the highest likeness?"

The boy said: "Glory be to Him who set riddles within His Book, that it might be approached through its own door — and you are one of its gates. So make plain to us how this is."

The Master said: "Everything has a meaning, and the meaning of the parables is this: the apparent is an evidence of the hidden. He, blessed and exalted, forbade that He be measured against anything of His creation, so that it be said, "this is like God," as one says:

> هذا مثل الإمام

This is like the Imam, or:

> هذا مثل الداعي

This is like the caller, or, "this is like the argument." Their likenesses became signs pointing to them and marking out their ranks, one above another. In the matter of His greatness, it is not fitting that the Creator be measured against anything of His creation in word or deed, so it is not said, "this is like God." Rather, the parables are to be taken according to their meanings, for their meanings bear signifiers: the lesser points to the greater, and the greater points to the supreme. For this He said:

> وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى

And to God belongs the highest likeness — meaning the highest evidence, for there is no evidence higher than the evidence that points to God. That is the air, highest above the heaven and the earth, encompassing all things while nothing among them encompasses it. It is the highest likeness, the evidence pointing to the One who has nothing like Him. He did not say that there is nothing like Him — for indeed there is nothing like Him; He meant rather that there is no evidence for Him like that evidence. To Him belongs the highest likeness, and no evidence among the evidences of any other, of those of His creation whom we have described, is like His evidence. Blessed be God, the Lord of the Mighty Throne."

The boy said: "The meaning has grown vast and the range has widened. Was there, before the air, something standing between God and it, given the greatness you have described in it?"

The Master said: "Yes."

The boy said: "What is it?"

The Master said: "Time."

The boy said: "And what is time?"

The Master said: "It is the time of the will, by which He created what He willed and commanded what He wished."

The boy said: "You have plunged me into the deeps of the seas of the highest assembly. Return me to the sciences of this world and their parables, that I may seek help by them in my affair and be strengthened by them to bear what I am meant to bear. I have feared for myself and grieved for my mind, lest it be led astray into what it cannot fathom."

The Master said: "You rose high by your question, and so the answer rose with you to the loftiest of the apparent meanings, and there your judgment lost its way and your mind was bewildered. How then would it be were I to unveil to you the inward of it? You and I would be as Moses and the knowing one."

The boy said: "Is there, beyond this inward, an inward yet more inward than it?"

The Master said: "By my life, the inward has an inward that is higher in station, wider in power, and more complete in guidance, and it is the utmost of evidences toward the path of salvation."

The boy said: "I see here three layers of knowledge: an apparent, an inward, and an inward of the inward. Is there an evidence for this?"

The Master said: "Where would the evidence be, if not for this and for that to which it points?"

The boy said: "Then show me a likeness of these three layers."

The Master said: "I will show you from the Book of God Most High, from the argument of the intellect, and from what the great body of the parables points to."

The boy said: "I did not think there was any doctrine beyond what I had reached. Truly the affair of God is greater than the fancies of those who strain after it can rise to."

The Master said: "As for the Book of God Most High, His word:

> يَا بَنِي آدَمَ قَدْ أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْكُمْ لِبَاسًا يُوَارِي سَوْآتِكُمْ وَرِيشًا وَلِبَاسُ التَّقْوَى ذَلِكَ خَيْرٌ

O children of Adam (آدم), We have sent down upon you a garment that veils your shameful parts, and feathers for adornment; and the garment of piety, that is better. These are three: an apparent, an inward, and an inward of the inward."

The boy said: "You have spoken truly."

The Master said: "As for the argument of the intellect, a thing is not known except by a name, an attribute, or a meaning. The meaning is the very reality of the thing, the name is what it is called, and the attribute is what points to it. So too is the religion of God: it has an apparent, which is a name, and an inward, which is an evidence pointing to it, and it stands only by the two together. Nothing is pointed to except by a name or an attribute, as in the saying of one who says:

> طلع القمر

The moon has risen. The meaning is the disk; "moon" is its name; and "has risen" is its attribute. Were he to say "the disk" and then fall silent, it would carry no meaning; and were he to say "moon" and then fall silent, it too would carry no meaning — until, joining the name to the attribute, he says, "the moon has risen," whereupon the meaning is made plain and the speech is sound. Likewise, if you say "apparent," it calls for an answer, that one ask, "the apparent of what?" And if you say "inward," it calls for an answer, that one say to you, "the inward of what?" But when you say "the apparent of the religion and its inward," the speech is understood, and it becomes a religion having an apparent and an inward, just like the thing that has a name and an attribute. So He said:

> وَذَرُوا ظَاهِرَ الْإِثْمِ وَبَاطِنَهُ

And forsake the apparent of sin and its inward. Thus sin became a thing having an apparent and an inward. So too is the religion of God, the Greatest — of which the laws are the apparent and the inward is its inward — and it stands by itself. And for the knowing of its meaning there are many parables that point to it, such as the hidden egg, which has an apparent that shields it, an inward that is the white within, and an inward of its inward, the yellow yolk, which is its essence and its meaning."

## 6. Three Layers of Knowledge

The boy said: "Are not this world and the Hereafter a pair, and the soul and the body a pair, and the knowledge of the apparent and the knowledge of the inward a pair, and so on through many other pairs of just this kind?"

The Master said: "I grant it. But are they pairs of a single kind?"

The boy said: "The pairs point to the religion of Allah (الله)."

The Master said: "I grant it. In every pair the outward is a name and the inward is an attribute, and the attribute signifies the knowledge of God and the religion of God, which is the inner of the inner. In the same way, creation was set upon three stations. The first is the creation of the angels, the second the creation of the children of Adam (آدم), and the third the creation of the beasts. The apparent is the knowledge that marks the limit of the beasts, and whoever knows this alone, without the inward, stands at the level of the beasts. The knowledge of the inward is the knowledge proper to the children of Adam and their limit, and whoever knows it is a believer and stands at the level of true human beings. The knowledge of the inner of the inner is the knowledge of the angels, and whoever knows it becomes spiritual in his knowing while bodily in his frame. Such a one is a prophet whom God has sent into His land, by whom He establishes His proof over His creation — the veil of the angels and the interpreter of revelation to the children of Adam. And so the people are no more than two men. One has grasped the very purpose of knowledge, and his soul has been quickened by the spirit of certainty, so that he is a scholar by his knowing and a master by his working. The other is a learner upon the path of his own deliverance. As for the rest of the people, they are savages and rabble who know nothing, followers of every braying caller, led astray by his error and seduced by his ignorance. They imagine they are doing well, yet they have been cut off from the true examples that passed away before them; and Allah will not fail in what He has promised. For the deniers there is the like of it."

The boy said: "So this is what virtue is, and this is what knowledge and its exposition are. Blessed are the friends of God, and blessed are the followers who do good. Can I attain this great station by way of the stations that lie beneath it?"

The Master said: "Far from it, O my son. That is a station God has honored above all stations and degrees, and it is reached only by excellent work. If you do its work and strive its striving with truthful certainty and a pure, wakeful heart, I hope you will attain of it what the chosen and excellent friends of God have attained."

The boy said: "And who may aspire to reach the like of that station to which God has raised His friends?"

The Master said: "Their God is your God, and between God and anyone there is no kinship. Nothing brings the creation near to God but piety and righteous work. As for the knowledge by which God has raised His friends, you stand at its beginning, and if you work by it you will reach its end. It is a due upon God that He bring you to the same reaches as the righteous among them, and that He deprive you of nothing of what He gave them."

The boy said: "Then can you help me toward that by some meaning, as your favor was first extended to me?"

The Master said: "This is a station in which nothing avails but the work of its own people."

The boy said: "How can that be, when you were able to help before?"

The Master said: "The sower can sweeten the soil, cast the seed, and give it water, but he cannot bring forth the plant and its sheath. A man can sow his seed whenever he wills, yet he cannot shape from it what he wills. It grieves me, O my son, that you should seek my help in a thing and I not help you in it. May God expand your breast and light your heart with guidance. So keep the fear of God and do what is best, for God does not waste the reward of the one who works well. You are upon the road of deliverance, the way of guidance, the path of the people of piety. Walk your way, whose beginning you already stand upon, and hold fast to the rope to which you have been called, until you are led by a rope from God to the rope of God — for that is the goal of all who seek."

The boy said: "Is not the rope of Allah the Imam (الإمام) to whom you have called me?"

The Master said: "He is what is apparent of it. He is your rope and the most trustworthy handhold, your proof, and the gate of your Imam."

The boy said: "Then what is the rope of Allah, and what is the rope that comes from Allah?"

The Master said: "That is the end of your guidance and the utmost reach of the worlds."

The boy said: "There is no power and no strength except in Allah. He who has no guide has drowned in the depths of ignorance, just as he who has no ship has drowned in the seas of this world."

The Master said: "And what is this strength you have named, which cannot be had except by Allah?"

The boy said: "We say that I have no device by which to draw a benefit to myself, and no strength by which to ward off from myself a harm I dislike, except by Allah."

The Master said: "Then why do you say, There is no strength except in Allah, and not, There is no device except in Allah?"

The boy said: "It is narrated to us in our sciences that the *hawl* (الحول) is the year, by the words of God:

> حَوْلَيْنِ كَامِلَيْنِ

two complete years, meaning two years. And it is narrated to us, as the common people also relate, that *quwwa* (القوة), strength, means mules, by the words of God:

> وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْكُنُوزِ مَا إِنَّ مَفَاتِحَهُ لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ

And We gave him of the treasures such that their keys would weigh down a band of men possessed of strength. They said the band was forty men and the strength was mules, and that the keys of his treasures were the load of forty mules."

The Master said: "So it is as though, by your words There is no strength and no power except in Allah, you meant, There is no year and no mule except by Allah. Is this, in your view, the interpretation of your saying and its meaning?"

The boy said: "I have no other word than this, and it is a feeble one that resembles nothing of the speech of wisdom. I reckon its meaning is other than this — something stronger in speech and firmer in the mind. Have the two words an inward interpretation other than this?"

The Master said: "The creation has never uttered a single word of truth but that it has a root in the inward, nor a word of falsehood but that it once had a meaning — for speech was turned away from its meaning until it became absurd. What is absurd has been moved from its place, and truth, when it is altered, becomes falsehood. This is a speech by which what follows is grasped through analogy and pattern, just as what came before it was grasped through likeness and parable."

The boy said: "You have spoken truly. So what is the meaning of the two words and their like?"

The Master said: "As for *al-hawl*, he is the speaking Imam of God, His truthful successor, the master of the twelve deputies — just as the *hawl*, which is the year, is completed only in twelve months. The Imam was named *hawl* because he turns the speech of the Creator, in the fineness of its essence, into the speech of the children of Adam, until it is understood and becomes plain speech. So the speech of wisdom takes on for him a body and a frame, while the speech of the Creator carries within it the spirit of life and the light of deliverance. The excellence of the speech of wisdom over all other speech is the excellence of the speech of the Creator that lies within it. The Imam was named *al-hawl* by this turning of speech, and he turns it only by the leave of God. And *quwwa*, strength, is his proof and his gate, whom God has strengthened to bear the heavy burden of utterance. He takes the speech from the Imam in sum and distributes it among the summoners in detail, each according to his station; and so the proof was named strength, and he is strengthened only by God. For this reason the speaker says, There is no strength and no power except in Allah, meaning there is no Imam and no proof except by Allah, and from Allah come their light and their sustenance. As for the band, those possessed of strength, they are the ten proofs. So too did Joseph (عليه السلام) say to his father:

> يَا أَبَتِ إِنِّي رَأَيْتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ رَأَيْتُهُمْ لِي سَاجِدِينَ

O my father, I saw eleven stars and the sun and the moon; I saw them prostrating to me — meaning by the sun the Imam, by the moon his proof and his gate, and by the stars the completion of the twelve deputies. And likewise the brothers of Joseph said:

> لَيُوسُفُ وَأَخُوهُ أَحَبُّ إِلَىٰ أَبِينَا مِنَّا وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ

Truly Joseph and his brother are dearer to our father than we are, though we are a band — meaning the twelve deputies, that he should choose over them one man from among them. So there befell them a failure of judgment in the matter of Joseph (ع), and the risings of envy toward him appeared in them when God favored him. Yet the command of God was fulfilled in Joseph, and the end of their affair was remorse and turning back to him."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who cleansed them of defilement and returned them to the ground of repentance and the ways of turning back. What is the meaning of the king's saying:

> إِنِّي أَرَىٰ سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ

I see seven fat cows being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others dry. What are they, and what is the vision seen in sleep?"

The Master said: "As for the vision, it is like the outward work a man does, which is as a mirage: when he comes to it he finds it nothing. So too is the work of the outward without the inward — nothing of it is accepted, however much there be of it. And likewise the vision in sleep: nothing is gained from what is seen, however often it be seen. The king said, I see, in the account of the common people, seven things turning upon seven. As for the seven fat cows, they are the causes God has set between Himself and His creation. As for the seven lean ones, they are the seven appointed speakers, driven by need to what comes to them from God at the hands of His seven causes. The seven green ears are al-Khidr, a cause between God and the guardians. The seven dry ones are the guardians, driven by need to what comes to them from God at the hands of these seven causes. So blessed be God, Lord of the worlds."

The boy said: "And what is the likeness of this among us?"

The Master said: "That is in the foundation of Islam (الإسلام) and its offspring — like a single grain that put forth seven ears, meaning that seven Imams came forth from his loins."

The boy said: "How then did God forbid this world when He said:

> فَلَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا

So let not the life of this world deceive you?"

The Master said: "God has spoken truly. He did not say, Let not the world deceive you. He said, Let not the life of this world deceive you. Life has four meanings: a life apparent in this world, whose end is passing away, and the life of the Hereafter, whose end is abiding. He said: do not work for the perishing life, but work for the abiding life. That is His saying:

> يَا لَيْتَنِي قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِي

Oh, would that I had sent forward something for my life — meaning, would that in the perishing life he had sent forward something for the abiding life. And the two lives have a further meaning: a life by the knowledge of the outward and a life by the knowledge of the inward. The knowledge of the outward is the life of this world, and it is the lower knowledge; the knowledge of the inward is the life of the Hereafter. For this reason He said, Let not the life of this world deceive you — meaning, let not the outward of knowledge, and the working by it alone, deceive you. For He will accept from you only the inward established together with the outward, and He does not accept the outward without the inward."

The boy said: "What then of one who knows the inward knowledge but does not know the outward and does not stand by it? What is his station among the people of religion?"

The Master said: "It is an ill station, and his inward will neither stand nor be made sound, once he has abandoned a thing he was commanded to do and made it a shield for his inward. He is like the outward of fruits: when their rind is peeled from them before their harvest they are spoiled and cannot be set right afterward; and like the body, when its limbs are cut away the soul is not sustained in it a single hour. So it is with the outward of religion: when its obligations are neglected, its inward will not stand upright for its owner. And his abandoning of the outward, without leave from the one who laid it upon him, cannot escape one of two traits. If he abandoned it out of incapacity, then toward the inward he is more incapable still; and if he abandoned it deliberately, out of ability, then he is a rebel against the one who commanded him. The rebel is a transgressor, and the transgressor is no neighbor to the friends of God. Rather he is their enemy and they are his, because he has severed what God commanded to be joined."

The boy said: "And one who knows the outward knowledge and stands by it, but does not know the inward — what is his station among the people of religion?"

The Master said: "An evil station, for he is like a body that has grown but into which no soul has been breathed; so he is counted among the dead, and death is the abode of the deniers. As one who denies the signs of God, he is an enemy to the religion and its people."

The boy said: "I see that the outward is not made sound except by the inward, and the inward does not stand except by the outward; and that this world is lawful only to the one who knows the Hereafter, which is its true life and its inward. So too is religion: it is not made complete for its people until they establish both its outward and its inward."

The Master said: "Yes. This is the meaning, and upon this the work must be. For to establish all that God has commanded is to deserve His good pleasure, and to abandon any part of what God commanded is to expose oneself to His wrath."

The boy said: "Then is not the one who knows the outward together with the inward and establishes them a believer?"

The Master said: "Yes, he is a believer in truth."

The boy said: "And is it not so that whoever knows, and then works for the Hereafter, is granted this world made wholesome for him, its good things made lawful, and free dealing in its gains?"

The Master said: "Yes. This world is made good only for them. They are the most deserving of it and the foremost to it."

The boy said: "Then why is it that I see the people of this quality — who are the scholars in this affair — having cast off the world and all that is in it, until the ignorant among them suppose it forbidden to them? They turn away from this world and look upon it only askance, and their souls are heavy toward it. And I see their colors gone yellow with no illness, their bodies weak, their skin clinging to the bone, as though they do not eat their food, while in truth they do eat it."

The narrator said: When the Master heard the mention of his brethren and their description, he choked with feeling and his eyes overflowed with tears, and for a time he could not speak from the depth of his emotion. The boy wept at his weeping, until the Master feared that some of their enemies might come to overpower them. So he took hold of himself, and his heart grew calm, and then he turned to the boy.

The Master said: "O my son, the people you have described in this way looked to themselves by the light of life and busied themselves in their affair with the certainty of deliverance, yet they did not deal harshly with their own souls out of vanity. O my son, this world is seen by wealth and children, and the Hereafter is seen by knowledge and certainty. O my son, the faith of these people in the unseen is sound, so they held the promise and the warning as sure. Their souls yearned toward what they were promised, and they sought it. The dread of the threat alarmed them, and they strove. They feared the agony of death, and they hastened to good deeds. Their bodies grew weak from the toil of fasting, and their colors grew yellow from the length of their vigils. They do not sleep with those who sleep, out of fear, nor do they eat of their sustenance except as a grudging morsel. The longings of reflection have turned them from the pleasure of ease, and the sweetness of remembrance has kept them from the respite of sleep. Their souls have turned away from the appetites, so the world has not been sweet to them; they remember death often, so life has not grown pleasant to them; and they have beheld the bounties of the Hereafter with certainty, so they have looked upon this world only askance. Thus they refused this world, being turned away from it, without holding it forbidden."

The boy said: "This is the true report, and in this sense the scholars held this world hateful, while the people were heedless of them, supposing that they had forbidden to themselves what God had made lawful for them. You have described to me the delusion of the people of the world in their state and their heedlessness of the Hereafter."

The Master said: "If the people who cling to this world would only reflect upon their own making and the turning about of their states — from weakness to strength and from strength to weakness, from ease to ruin and from life to death — and would then look at those who passed before them out of the vanished ages, among whom the good and the wicked alike went down into a single ground, the good receiving no reward and the wicked no punishment in this world, they would know that they were created for something other than this world. But the labor lay heavy on them, so they would not bear upon themselves the burden of the seeking. They took this paltry, nearer portion and were content with the ease of this world in place of the Hereafter. This is their way and this is their meaning. They have no argument before God and no honor before His friends, and there has surely come to them of the tidings that which holds a warning."

## 7. The Five Shares and the Long Road to the Shaykh

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has guided us to His religion; we could never have been guided but for His favor. Now tell me about the beginning of this creation and how it was composed. Is it a single creation, or many different ones? For I see so vast a difference among people in their opinions and their passions."

The Master said: "As for the creation of bodies, it is manifold, as you can see in the difference of tongues and colors. That very variety is a proof of God's power, made plain through His signs, so that created beings may know one another: the child knows his father and the father his son, the man knows his wife and no other, and the woman knows her husband and no other. Thus God fashioned them in His justice. But as for the creation of the instrument — that is, the senses, both the apparent and the hidden among them — that is a single creation, and what lies hidden within tells you the same as what shows without. Have you ever seen a learned man with three eyes, that you might call it an increase in him? Or an ignorant man with a single eye, that you might call it a deficiency in him — so that one might say the learned man saw by virtue of his surplus and the ignorant man failed to see by virtue of his lack? Have you seen any increase at all in the instrument of one man over another? You see their creation as one level thing in the outward instrument, and the meaning of their composition is one and the same. From this we have come to know that the inner senses follow the very same pattern. This is justice, for God would not be just in what is apparent and unjust in what is hidden. He did not set two hearts in the breast of the learned man, nor strip the ignorant man of anything. The instrument is one, and its composition belongs to the One who gathered it. By this very instrument God has raised His argument against them — an instrument He gave them, which they did not use. The Almighty said:

> لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ أَعْيُنٌ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ آذَانٌ لَا يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا

They have hearts wherewith they do not understand, and they have eyes wherewith they do not see, and they have ears wherewith they do not hear.

Do you suppose that God, in His bounty and His generosity, has laid upon them a burden they cannot bear? Never. He is fairer than that."

The boy said: "I see it no otherwise, nor do I claim otherwise. But I ask you about the difference of passions. Where did it come from, when the instrument is whole and the origin of the composition is one?"

The Master said: "As for the origin, it is one. All of them are from Adam (آدم), and Adam is from dust, whom his Lord created and into whom He breathed of His Spirit. But they departed from the way in their unions; they were begotten upon a course that was not of the faith, and they grew up in something other than guidance. I do not doubt that among them are children of illicit birth who claim fathers not their own. When the roots were corrupted, the branches too were corrupted and grew strange, and their return to the truth grew hard — all the more so for the arrogance that entered them against obedience, the sloth that entered them against worship, and the envy they bore toward the friends of God. So they did not hold themselves within the bounds of the lawful, keeping clear of the forbidden."

The boy said: "If the origin is one and the instrument is one, then where does the difference of passions arise, when their meaning in seeking is one — for every man seeks the good for himself?"

The Master said: "By my life, their meaning in seeking is indeed one, but they differ in striking the mark, like archers aiming at a single target. They are of one mind in loosing their arrows at it with resolve and with the instrument, yet for all that they differ in whether they hit it. So too the hearts within the breasts and the minds are gathered as one, and the minds are gathered upon safeguarding the body and securing its dignity. But when the call of truth appeared, they parted ways. Some held that to answer the Messengers and to humble themselves before them was part of their own dignity and a guarding of the self against folly. Others held that to deny the Messengers and to raise the self above obedience to them was part of their own dignity and a guarding of the self against the narrowness of worship. The root was one in both: to seek ease for the body and honor for the soul. So one company chose what the Messengers had promised them of the reward of God — though in this world it was rare and hard to come by — choosing it for its lastingness. The others chose the bliss of this world, though it perishes, choosing it for its nearness, the ease of its asking, and the swiftness of its reward. The very hearts by which the first sought the bliss of the Hereafter were the hearts by which the others sought the bliss of this world. Each looked to himself, and each supposed himself his own good counselor. So they came together in the seeking and differed in the striking. This is of God's justice, His generosity, and the breadth of His giving: to every seeker among them is given what he sought. Whoever desires the reward of this world, We give him from it, and whoever desires the reward of the Hereafter, We give him from it."

The boy said: "But the people of this world are ranked in classes within their world. Some are rich beyond need of labor, kings by the abundance of their wealth, and some are poor and in want of money, serving the rich to receive from their hands. Then the bliss of this world gathers them all upon one footing. Are they all alike in the evil of their end, or is there for some of them a portion apart from the rest?"

The Master said: "It is of God's justice that He joins the one to the other and does not part them from their companions and those they held dear. So they are equal in His punishment, just as they were joined together in disobeying Him."

The boy said: "And this religion which God has honored and perfected, which He has accepted as a religion for Himself and laid as a duty upon His servants — will He accept it from some men and not others, so that they too fall into classes like the people of this world? Or will He accept it from none but the man who brings it whole?"

The Master said: "If the people of this world are ranked in classes within their world, then the people of religion are ranked in degrees within their religion. Justice makes it binding that the Creator be worshipped to the full measure of His will, the greatness of His sovereignty, and the greatness of their need of Him. But His mercy widened toward them. He knew that among them are the weak, so He accepted from them whatever sincere striving in work was within their reach and whatever effort in obedience they could give, whether little or much. Then He charged each part of His creation according to its capacity and its work, and gave to every man of merit his due of merit, so that they stand at degrees before their Lord. Then, of His justice, He made the end of their obedience to be that He joins them to the people of complete religion and excellent work among His servants. The Almighty said:

> وَمَن يُطِعِ اللَّهَ وَالرَّسُولَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ مَعَ الَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ النَّبِيِّينَ وَالصِّدِّيقِينَ وَالشُّهَدَاءِ وَالصَّالِحِينَ وَحَسُنَ أُولَٰئِكَ رَفِيقاً ذَلِكَ الْفَضْلُ مِنَ اللَّهِ

And whoever obeys God and the Messenger, those are with the ones upon whom God has bestowed favor: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous. What excellent companions they are. That is the bounty from God.

This is by their exertion of effort in obedience, not by the merit of their deeds, for their deeds are not the equal of the deeds of the righteous. It is rather a mercy from God to them, though they themselves are weak and their works are slight."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has done this for them. Then is it to be feared that any man should be punished for something other than disobedience, even when his work is little?"

The Master said: "God is more just than that."

The boy said: "Is it to be hoped, then, that any man should enter the Garden without works at all, like the mad and like little children?"

The Master said: "That falls under the grace of God, His generosity, and the breadth of His mercy."

The boy said: "Then what is the measure of effort in obedience and of sincerity in work, such that God is bound to accept a man's excuse in all that lies beyond it?"

The Master said: "The full reach of his knowledge and the utmost of his strength."

The boy said: "Then describe to me the duty of knowledge and the duty of action, and the goal of each, that I may test myself by them. If I am able to reach the goal in seeking my Lord's pleasure, I shall press on; and if not, I shall halt at the limit of my strength, and God is the first to excuse me for what I could not do. I trust in the justice of God and feel safe from any wrong on His part, yet I fear the evil within my own soul."

The Master said: "Do not feel fear where you have come to feel safe, and do not feel safe where you ought still to fear. Strive for God in your work with your own person and with your wealth. For God does not accept what He hates, nor does He hate what pleases Him. He has hated disbelief for His creation and been pleased with thankfulness from His servants and with justice in His judgment, and He accepts nothing else. Truthfulness and faithfulness are the two gardens of His Paradise, and none is saved with Him but the people of truthfulness and faithfulness."

The boy said: "Then make plain to me the goal of the work in my own person, and what God has made binding upon me in it."

The Master said: "Obedience to God ennobles its people, even when their roots are humble, and good work is the crown of those who do it. As for what is binding upon you in your own person, it is to fulfill every obligation and to shun every forbidden thing, for that is the very sustenance of the religion and the purity of the body. And the whole of what wins you God's pleasure and His acceptance is this: that you extend to another what you would love to have extended to yourself, and that you withhold from him what you would love to have withheld from you. In that is justice, and in justice is God's pleasure."

The boy said: "Yes, this is the binding truth, and whoever does not act by it is in nothing with God. But is there any room for ease in it when necessity compels?"

The Master said: "God has indeed made that broad for His servants, out of His compassion and His mercy. He has said:

> فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ

Whoever is compelled, neither desiring it nor transgressing, there is no sin upon him."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has laid no hardship upon His servants in religion. I am resolved to hold fast to what He has commanded, and I seek His help in doing as He has ordered me. But what is the duty regarding my wealth, which you joined to my own person when you said: work with your person and your wealth?"

The Master said: "As for your wealth, it is whatever God has granted you of every kind of gain. Take it only from what is lawful, and spend it only where it is due."

The boy said: "And what is the lawful source from which it is taken, and what is the due place in which it is spent?"

The Master said: "Its lawful source is the good of earnings — that in which you have wronged no one and which you have not tainted with falsehood. As for its due, that is its purifying alms. Look upon all that has come to your hand, whether little or much, and set aside a fifth of it, from the best of it and the part most beloved to you, and pay it to the one whose claim upon you is binding. In that way your wealth is purified by the alms, just as your body is purified by work. Work is a shield, and the alms are a cleansing, and he who purifies himself has surely prospered."

The boy said: "I shall do so, and I turn to God in repentance for what has passed. Then what am I to do with the remainder of my wealth?"

The Master said: "You shall spend upon yourself and your household in what is fitting. And if your surplus grows beyond that, share it with the needy among your brethren and give your kindness freely to everyone who turns to you in want, for the man who has no surplus of his own stands in need of the surplus of another. So be swift to every good and foremost in every bounty. This is the goal of the work, if you can be patient in it."

The boy said: "Yes, I shall be patient, and then patient again. And how should a man in great want fail to be patient toward one who has no need of his labor? Every needy man is one who bears patiently, and every man given a favor is one who owes thanks."

The Master, gladdened by the boy's words, said: "Praise be to God, who has guided you to speak rightly. I ask Him to help you carry the work through to its completion, for that is the perfection of merit."

The boy said: "I had long been seeking from another's hand the credit of a favor I ought to have done for my own soul. And now that the credit has fallen to me from my own hand, shall I deny it to my soul? I can find no one who would cheat it more than I would. So here is the whole of my wealth before you, in five shares. One share is its purifying alms. One share is expiation for what I have squandered. One share is thanksgiving to God for what I have come to know. One share is for the poor among my brethren. And one share you are to accept by your grace, that it may be a provision for you. Take all of it to yourself, and God will restore its like."

The narrator said: The Master's heart went out to the boy when he saw how the jewel within him had come clear, how the trial had drawn from him a praiseworthy result, and how his merit now stood plain and his work purified.

The Master said, his tears running down: "O my son, it is for the like of you that scholars set out on the long road. It is for the like of you that the earth is held firm and the heavens kept standing. It is for the like of you that the bonds of kinship are made to shine. Upon the like of you the rain comes down from the breaks in the clouds. So rejoice, my son, in what God has granted you and in the breast He has opened to Islam (الإسلام). Receive the glad tidings of a light from your Lord and of a nearing deliverance; take God's gift to you and the increase of His favors upon you, and meet them with abundant work and noble thanks. As for my own share of this wealth, I have no need of it. I have come out from wealth and fled its temptation, and it would not be fitting, my son, that you should turn me back to the very thing I have come to loathe. As for your brothers' share, you shall hold it in trust for them. When they ask you for something, you will hand it to them yourself, so that they may know your kindness and give thanks for your generosity. As for the share of expiation, keep it by you until you come to know your fault. As for the share of thanksgiving, it is a trust held for God until you reach your maturity and rise to the highest of what you have come to know. As for the share of the alms, that too is a trust, held for the one to whom the alms are owed. So take all of it into your keeping until you meet Him, for He is the Ruler over you and on your behalf."

The boy said: "And who is this generous one before you, whose judgment you do not overreach and whose command you do not transgress?"

The Master said: "He is the one whose right God has made binding upon me and upon every believer; in whose hand are the keys of the gardens and the landmarks of the kingdom; whose palms are opened with the light of Sinai (الطور). He is the cause behind the signs, and his knowledge calls to the loftiest of ends. Through him your light is completed, through him God perfects your affair, and at his hand the blessings come flowing down to you."

The boy said: "Kindness such as this — to whom does it belong, and who are you yourself?"

The Master said: "I am his son, and one of his servants in good works, seeking his pleasure and shrinking from his displeasure."

The boy said: "I fear that I am far from his good pleasure and close to his displeasure, for I did not know him. Have I any excuse in that?"

The Master said: "An excuse is granted only before one has been brought within reach of that knowledge."

The boy said: "Shall I speak, or shall I hold back?"

The Master said: "Say whatever seems best to you."

The boy said: "Then is it not your duty, at this very moment, to take me by the hand to the door of his chamber that I may enter, and to the house of light that I may draw from it? This is part of discharging in full the trust that is binding upon you."

The Master said: "This is indeed your right — but only with his permission and by his decision."

Then the Master took his leave and departed. The boy wished to press provisions upon him for the road, but thought better of forcing them, and the Master would not accept. He went on until he reached his own elder father, and set before him the news of the boy, describing his qualities. The father recognized in them the marks of the people of goodness and the mines of merit, and he marveled.

He said: "Bring this boy to me quickly, for I hope he will be a door of mercy that God has opened for the people of his age."

So the Master tarried only a little, then returned to the boy, and found him changed in body from the state in which he had left him.

The Master said: "What is this I see in you? It is not the state I left you in."

The boy said: "And what have you noticed in me — may my father and mother be your ransom?"

The Master said: "A weakness of body, and a yellowness in your color."

The boy said: "That is from a desolation that came over me after you left."

The Master said: "What desolation is this, and what is the cause of the weakness?"

The boy said: "Your absence left me desolate, and the dread that some misfortune might strike before I could meet you again drained my strength."

The Master said: "God has known what is in your heart, and so He has made the trial light for you, and has cast into the heart of the one who has charge of you a mercy toward you. So take up your provisions for the journey, for I mean to carry you to your hope and bring you to the door of your goal. And there is no power except by God."

At this the boy was so overcome with joy and awe that his soul nearly departed his body, until God, knowing his condition, took hold of it and returned it to him. When he had revived, he said: "Praise be to God, who has never withdrawn His compassion from the believers."

He gave abundant thanks to his father, and pressed his lips to his palms in service, and to his feet as well. Then he rose at once, took up the traveling kit, and the two set out together until they reached the greater Master. They sought permission and were admitted; they entered, gave their greetings, and sat down at his leave. When the gathering had settled around them, the boy said to the Master: "Teach me how I should speak, and how I should ask for what I need."

The Master said to him: "It is not lawful for me to instruct you in anything while you stand in this station, out of reverence for its master. You stand before one who already knows your need and knows what you intend."

So the boy held his peace. They remained with the greater Master for a time, until the boy's affair was completed, his guidance perfected, and he had reached the utmost of his hope and come to know what was his and what was owed of him. Then they took their leave and went out together, keeping themselves hidden. In the gathering they had spoken no word beyond the greeting of peace and words of welcome, for the boy had turned yellow from the intensity of his awe and could not bring himself to speak. The master of the house gave orders that the two be honored, that their dignity be upheld, and that whatever they needed be provided. He led them to his own dwelling, and when he had brought them in, he greeted them and showed his gladness in them.

He said to the Master — for between them was brotherhood and an old companionship in learning: "My brother, is this the boy we used to hear of?"

He said: "Yes. This is the one whose remembrance grew sweet in hearts and rose upon the tongues of the believers."

The boy said: "Has the report of me truly reached the friends of God, so that they recount my mention in their gatherings?"

The Master said: "Yes, my brother. So it is with you."

The boy said: "Then favors have surrounded me from every side, and open hands have overtaken me from every road. I do not know whom to thank, nor whom to repay."

Then they all sat and talked through the night. When morning came, they sought leave to come before the Shaykh (الشيخ). When they were admitted and had finished their greetings, the Shaykh gave them leave to sit, and they sat. Then the Shaykh began to speak.

He said: "Praise be to God, who has parted the closed hearts by His light, and opened by His justice what was locked away of every sought-after thing; who by His making has made remembrance easy, as a gentleness toward His servants and a mercy from Him to those who long for Him. He has not burdened creation with the full measure of what is due to Him, nor has He been content that they should deny His blessings. Rather, out of His justice, He charged them with only what they are not too weak to bear, so that the affair was set balanced between two extremes, and it was named justice. He has spread out His hands with generosity in kindness and grace, disappointing no one who calls upon Him and denying no one who keeps silent. His blessings overflow and His favors are abundant — neither reserved for those who give thanks, on account of the vastness of His reward, nor withheld from those who deny them, on account of the greatness of His punishment. He has kept His blessings too noble to be stinted, made His proofs too firm to be denied, encompassed all that exists so that He Himself is beyond being contained, and set Himself, in the evidences, beyond denial. So He is present without limit, and known without being described. Glory be to Him: how great He is, yet how gentle to His creation; how mighty, yet how merciful to His servants; how powerful, yet how just in His ruling; how exalted, yet how near to His people; how near, yet how far beyond every imagining. So worship Him and give thanks to Him. To Him you shall return."

Then he said: "As for what follows: it belongs to the dignity of minds that they be set to work in seeking; and among the fruits of seeking is a true finding; and among the marks of what is found is its sweetness. Every sweet freshwater spring is a drink whose surface lies plain but whose depths are veiled: the one who seeks it never wearies of the thought it demands, and creation never reaches the end of its purpose. Its landmarks are a right, and the right of the truth is an obligation — and from that it is called a right, in confirmation of its being due. Whoever withholds from the truth what is owed to it has done wrong. Now, among the rights of thought is that hearts pay their zakat (زكاة) through good manners; and among the rights of good manners is the seeking of knowledge; and among the rights of knowledge is that it be acted upon; and among the rights of acting upon it is that it be kept pure through obedience to those charged with authority; and among the rights of obedience is that it be perfected in hardship and in ease alike, in good fortune and in trial the same."

And here he named the word of God:

> وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا الَّذِينَ صَبَرُوا وَمَا يُلَقَّاهَا إِلَّا ذُو حَظٍّ عَظِيمٍ

"And none is granted it except those who are patient, and none is granted it except one of great fortune."

Then he turned his face toward the boy and said: "O youth, you have been honored as an estranged friend on his arrival, and greeted as a visitor who came with purpose. So what is your name?"

He said: "Ubayd Allah (عُبيد الله), son of Abd Allah (عبد الله)."

The Shaykh said: "That is your description, and your news has already reached us."

The boy said: "I am free, son of Abd Allah."

The Shaykh said: "And who freed you from a master, that you should become free?"

The boy said: "This world possessed me, and I have handed it back to the One who summoned it" — and he gestured with his hand toward the Master who had called him.

The Shaykh said: "Consider: if this one were himself owned and owned nothing, could his freeing of you be valid?"

The boy said: "It would not be valid."

The Shaykh said: "Then what is your name?"

At this the boy fell silent, at a loss for an answer.

The Shaykh said: "O youth, how can a thing be known when it has no name?"

The boy said: "Then I am newly born to you. Name me."

The Shaykh said: "That must wait upon the completion of seven days."

The boy said: "And why is it deferred to the completion of seven days?"

The Shaykh said: "For the dignity of the newborn."

The boy said: "And if the newborn dies before the seven days are complete?"

The Shaykh said: "Nothing harms him. He is named after that."

The boy said: "And this name by which you would name me — is it truly mine?"

The Shaykh said: "Would you then become an object of worship, though you have only just been born?"

The boy said: "How is such a thing to be said of it?"

The Shaykh said: "The name belongs to you, and you belong to the name. So it does not appear except within your limit, and it runs its course with your term."

So the boy rose and hurried away in fair obedience, not returning the word, out of good manners. He went out, and his father went out with him, until the two came back to their lodging, where the master of the house greeted them.

He said: "How stands our brother in the matter of his need?"

The Master said to him: "A promise has been given. The one who awaits it is thirsting, and the appointed term is near."

So they remained until the seventh day. Then they sought leave of the Shaykh, and when he had granted it, he ordered the boy to wash and to put on his cleanest garments. As the boy took up this preparation, his heart sensed that his need would be granted that very day, and that the preparation he had been commanded pointed to a good that was coming after it. When he had done what was commanded and they had all gone out together, the boy went out saying, "A day of good fortune, and a patron of good fortune," repeating it again and again. Nothing drove him to this saying but the abundance of his joy and the intensity of his longing to know what would draw him nearer to the pleasure of God. When they came before the Shaykh, they found that he had taken up the implements of his work. He greeted them with peace, and they returned the greeting.

Then he ordered the boy to draw near, and he drew near; and he brought him close, and he came close. He began to speak of matters that no imagining had ever encompassed, that no pen had ever traced, that had never crossed the heart of any mortal — matters not to be uttered in the assemblies of preachers nor set out openly in the collected books, on account of their surpassing worth, and unveiled only to their own people. When he had discharged what was due and brought the boy out of the wastes of bewilderment, in which he had wandered lost, to the very end of guidance, his way became clear and his draught grew sweet, and he came to know his Lord. So he released his pilgrim's consecration by magnifying the sanctities of God, held fast to the sure rope, circled the ancient House, spent his rites in remembrance, and completed his pilgrimage by the greatest sign.

They and his father kept returning to the Shaykh, studying what the boy had been given — the boy patient in his perseverance, never wearying of the question for its nobility, never sated with the answer for its sweetness. They stayed in that state for a season of the age, until the Shaykh knew that the root had been watered and the branch raised high.

Then the boy said to the Shaykh, in thanks to God and to him: "Praise be to God, who has made you worthy of this station, so that through you He revives a religion and puts an ignorance to death, and has made you a stronghold for the people and a place of safety. You are the gate of the people of heaven and the ascent of the people of earth. From the light of your might the lights appeared, and from the pouring of your palm the rivers ran. May you never cease to be so, until the One who is pleased with you makes you pleased."

The Shaykh said: "As for you, may God bless you in what He has given you, aid you by His enabling grace to keep what has come to you, set you upright in the doing of His will, smooth your path with the light of His guidance, open your breast with the keys of wisdom, ennoble your affair with the way of the call, complete His grace upon you with the truthful proofs of response, and bring you, in obedience to Him, to the best of your hope in His good pleasure. And now I bid you farewell, my son, and give you leave to depart. Our parting weighs heavy upon me, yet three things have driven us to it. The first is that we are setting out to a country other than this. The second is your long separation from your family through all this time, together with the hardship you took upon yourself in leaving without your father's permission, so that there is grave grief in him and a great anger that nothing will clear away but your return — though I hope, if you are gentle with him, for good in your father's case. And the third is the duty of rendering the right of this grace that has reached you, for it is binding upon you to worship by it and to call others to it."

The boy said: "Your parting is a grief to me, and your loss a diminishment. But your counsel is the more rightly guiding, your command the more binding, your judgment the more sound. So charge me with what you see fit, dismiss me when you will, and be kind to me by sending my father along with me to my country, that I may lean upon him in my work and entrust all my affairs to him."

The Shaykh said: "That I shall do. And I enjoin upon you the fear of God who created you, the keeping of the trust for which you have struggled, and the safeguarding of your father, who raised you when you were small and toiled long in your affairs, with his own self and with his wealth. Do not clothe him in the garments of disdain, do not set him upon the mounts of ruin, do not leave his name unspoken, do not let yourself be turned away from his remembrance, and do not deal falsely with him in your city, for at the very beginning there is room enough for you."

The boy said: "May God reward you, on behalf of the Master of every grace, with the good that every grateful one could give, and may the Lord of bounty repay you for your shepherding, when hands fall short of repaying you. I will keep your charge. So do not cut off my share from the blessing of your prayer."

Then the two rose, clasped hands, and embraced, each bidding the other farewell, unable to hold back his tears and unable to speak except by a sign. Then they parted. The Master and the boy set out and traveled on until they drew near the boy's own city, where his father was.

The Master said to the boy: "My son, I have grasped the counsel of the Shaykh, and you have not yet grasped it. There is no right guidance except in his words. This is your city, and we have reached its edge. Sit with me here, apart from the road, for I wish to remind you of something of my own affair and to counsel you in what you should act upon."

When they had sat down, the Master said: "My son, I know your father's state and his enmity toward the people of this way, and now your going out with me and your absence from him — without his leave or his safe-conduct — have come one upon another to weigh on him. I mean to carry out my own father's counsel and keep hidden somewhere in this open country, while you go back to your father, so that his soul may be set at ease and his anger toward us may subside. And I hope, if you are gentle with him, that he will be nearer than any other to what you desire, for the absence has alarmed him and the loneliness has distressed him. Know, my son, that God has opened for you, by the lightest of your striving, what I did not think you would attain except after a long while. God has been good to you, so be good to yourself, and honor the one through whom God has honored you; for our opinion of you has been good and our hope in you great. So you must guard the trust your father laid upon you, and stand firm in your affair. Piety and God-wary discretion are the twin guardians of your religion and your work, and thanksgiving and patience are an increase to your light. And seek from Allah (الله) the opening of your provisions, and the goodness of His enabling grace toward you."

Then the Master said: "I charge you with six qualities. Rely upon Allah once you are resolved. Speak with a considered ruling when you are called upon. Do not fall into anger when you are wronged, and do not grow weary when you are slandered. Whoever answers your call to the good, be patient with him. And single out your own self for the best of all that you summon others to, for Allah will help you and open the way before you."

The boy said: "May Allah reward you with three rewards: an imam whose plea you have upheld and whose trust you have counseled well, and a dead man whose life you kindled by your grace. My heart is filled and my breast is swelling with light and gladness at the greatness of what you have set out. I cannot tell which of your words is the finer: the outward, which you ordered in its due sequence, or the inward, which you unfolded in its meanings."

The Master said: "Praise belongs to Allah, who made His way easy for you, drew its far reaches near, and helped you carry the weight of it. Now I bid you farewell and am drawn away from you. Is there yet any need in which one might help you carry a part of your burden?"

The boy said: "My needs rest upon three: a need I have already had fulfilled, a need for which I hope, and a need I ask you to grant me out of your kindness."

The Master said: "I have understood your three needs, and I answer you in what you have asked. As for the need already past, it is your life in knowledge after death. May Allah bless you with such a life, and bring you in it to the best of what you intended. As for the need for which you pray, it is to act upon what you know and to accept what He sets before you, and Allah will bring it about for you by His mercy. As for the need you ask of me, it is as though you wished for some increase upon what you have heard."

The boy said: "There is no greater increase than your knowing what lies within me. So grant me what you see fit."

The Master said: "As for my knowing what is within you, none knows the unseen but Allah.

And as for the increase, I will draw its matter together for you, and set you upon the foundations by which you are to work. Look upon all these things whose outward face I have named to you, and turn them into parables. Whatever among them is praiseworthy is a likeness of the truth and its people — its finer part for their finer part, its tokens pointing toward them. At the mention of parting the boy gave such a gasp that his spirit almost took flight and his soul almost left his body. Then he bent and kissed the Master's feet and wiped their dust across his cheeks, and bade him farewell, and left. The boy returned to his house with his heart torn from him in grief, unable to hold back a single word of his sorrow at parting from his elder father — the father who had been the cause of his grace — until his own family and his neighbors grew afraid for him. He passed his day and his night restless with sorrow and alarm. Then his father came in upon him, angry.

## 8. Homecoming, the Father, and the Debate with Abu Malik

His father stood over him and said: "O my son, is this how sons repay their fathers? I taught you nothing but the finest a child can be taught, and never once did you deny yourself or forsake your beliefs — until I grew old and this stranger appeared among us. You drew him in upon me and crossed over into his affair without ever consulting your parents. If what I held was true, then you have wronged me in my very self, for you kept the truth hidden from me. And if it was false, then you have disobeyed me by ruining your own soul — and whatever befalls you befalls me."

The boy answered: "O my father, I have shown you nothing but mercy and tenderness. Out of your own mercy you raised me and fed me, choosing me over kin and wealth. I have no wish to be unkind to you on a day like this. Grant me, of your bounty, one of two things."

"Name them," said his father. "What are the two?"

The boy said: "Either leave me to my courtesy and my kindness toward you — for I am your son, whom you raised from a child, and our affair rests with Allah (الله) — or grant me leave to debate with you, give me your assurance of safety from your anger, and bring your mind to bear on what I say."

His father said: "As for courtesy on the surface while the hearts differ beneath, that is a reproach standing between me and you. How long can an outward agreement hold when what lies under it is at odds? I do not want that. But your debate and your candor — those you have. So say whatever seems best to you."

The boy said: "Consider my standing at your side these forty years. Can it be anything but one of two cases? Either you are a man of knowledge who withheld that knowledge from me — and then you must not blame me for seeking my own deliverance through another; or you are ignorant — and in that you are excused before me, yet you had more need than I of a man who was a stranger to me. If I went to him before you did, it was only because you are the elder, born long before me — for I heard his words just as you heard them, and I saw what you saw."

His father's heart was rent by his son's words. He saw that there was no escape for him from either argument, and his eyes overflowed with tears. He said: "O my son, the argument that has come down upon me from you comes down upon you from me in the same measure — if you keep your affair hidden from me. So set it before me. If it is sound, I will accept it from you and count it an honor to myself; and if it is false, I will turn you back from it, in honor of your own soul and out of compassion for it."

So they debated over a span of sittings and opened their hearts to one another at length, until their affair came to rest upon guidance and right direction: acting upon the religion of Allah, safeguarding it, holding fast to it, calling others to it, and helping one another in it. Through the two of them Allah revived many of His creatures. The boy was sent out to bear the good news, with his father's blessing, and to summon others to come in. He returned to them and became a cause of their guidance, and Allah poured His blessings upon the people of that country through them and at their hands. The command of Allah was fulfilled, and the slanderer was left disappointed.

The boy's name was Salih (صالح), and his father's name was al-Bakhtari (البختري). The two of them held an honored rank among their people for the good they had done, and a high standing for the excellence of the manners they had gained. News of them spread across the land, together with news of the religion they now followed, until it reached a people who still clung to the old creed. Al-Bakhtari had long held influence among them, and they feared that his leaving their religion would cut that influence off from them. So they went out and gathered before a scholar of theirs named Abu Malik (أبو مالك), the mighty servant — the man they called, for his piety, the abundance of his forbearance, and his defense of their religion, "Kab al-Ahbar" (كعب الأحبار). Among them he was a man of standing through his knowledge, his command of speech, and his discernment of opinions.

When they came in to him they said: "O Abu Malik, do you not see what the boy has gone over to?"

"Which boy?" he asked.

"Salih ibn al-Bakhtari," they said.

"And what has he gone over to?"

They said: "We will tell you. A stranger has come to the country with a fine manner of speech and a creed he summons people to, and we do not know what it is. The boy inclined to him and went out with him without his father's leave, and was gone for a time. Then he returned speaking the stranger's words and calling to what the stranger called to. We have heard that his father has answered him and taken his side in it. What the two of them have done has grieved us, and we have held back from them for three reasons. First, the standing kindness al-Bakhtari showed us — on his father's account — together with the brotherhood in religion we shared; we dread estrangement after closeness and hate division after brotherhood. Second, if these people are upon the truth, then we have fallen short in following them and helping them in it. Third, if they have strayed into error, then we have fallen short in turning them back from it and in supporting the truth and its people. You are the head of our religion and one of our standards; we have come to inform you and to consult you, and we will not go beyond your opinion."

Abu Malik said: "Restrain yourselves, for what you say has alarmed me. You know that al-Bakhtari and his son were among those we counted people of forbearance and piety, and that they have rights over us and old favors to their credit. You have said of them what you have said, and you are, in my sight, people who do not stray from the truth — so Allah has spared you from that. This is news that has come upon a people; an argument was made plain to them, and they followed it and were satisfied by it. We cannot pronounce them in error and so wrong them, nor pronounce them guided and so wrong ourselves by imitating them, nor say they have been deceived and so refute them without knowledge — for they are nobler in our eyes than that. Rather, we must look into this news with a fair eye, by the light of justice, and with the steadfastness of the mind. It has been said:

> مَن تثبّت في الأمور لم يزل سلطانه محروزاً، ومن عجّل في أمره لم يأمن زلة قدمه

He who is deliberate in his affairs keeps his authority secure, and he who is hasty cannot keep his foot from slipping. A tear in anything else admits of mending, and what is spoiled in it can be replaced — as with a flaw in food, whose harm is easily remedied and whose cure is near at hand. But a tear in religion is gravely harmful and heavy in loss; its flaw can neither be mended nor exchanged for another — as with a flaw in the very medicine, which neither spares the one who takes it nor leaves him to himself. And we have been told concerning this:

> أفضل الذخائر كنوز الألباب واكتساب الآداب

The best of provisions is the treasure of sound minds and the acquiring of good manners, for the fruit of their outcomes is praiseworthy. Among their fruits is following the way of forbearance, and forbearance is the crown of the learned; by it noble resolve raises them to the highest glories, their excellence is made plain in every place, and honor comes to them in the ease of what they seek. We have laid up for ourselves some measure of that forbearance we borrowed, and some of the manners we gained in the days behind us, amid the narrowness of our circumstances and the hardship of the pursuit. We bore that patiently for its worth, and by it we seek help against the calamities of affairs and the accidents of the times. We must not squander it. This is the very hour of our need for it, and of putting it to use in what has come upon us in the matter of our brothers. There is no sense in leaving them and what they hold without laying it open and debating with them in it — with the forbearance of the capable and the earnest inquiry of seekers, free of any doubt in what we hold and free of accusing these people. They may have left the truth in forgetfulness — and they are nobler in our sight than that — or they may have gone out into error — and they are above such error too. Falsehood has no brilliance for which the plain truth should be abandoned. But consider the case of these people, for it cannot be free of one of three states. Either they are still upon the religion we know, and what has happened among them is only an addition of literature, sayings, poetry, and the abundant talk of people. Or they have been given an increase in the religion they already hold, and they have rejoiced in it — for knowledge is the lost property of every believer, and an increase in knowledge is better than a lack of it. Or they have been given a guidance other than what they held, through the prophethood of a prophet brought to them and calling them to it, and they have followed it upon clear proof. That is not to be denied of the command of Allah, nor will the creation ever reach the end of Allah's decree in them and His will for them, however hard they strive:

> وإن الله كل يوم هو في شأن

Indeed, every day He is engaged in some matter."

They said: "O Abu Malik, we came to you with hearts full of rage against these people, ready to brand them unbelievers, to refute their opinion, and to reckon their blood and their wealth lawful to us. But now we find our hearts humbled by your words, and by the way you have argued on their behalf and spoken in their defense."

Abu Malik said: "This argument of mine is not for them; it is for the truth, that I should not betray it in its absence. I have upheld the argument of the truth on its behalf while the one who should argue for it is away. Nor is this a defense of them; rather I have defended myself and you against uttering falsehood, and against hurling accusations into the unseen concerning what we do not encompass in knowledge. For then we would be like the tyrants of the nations gone before, who were pleased with their own doctrines in their own reckoning and rejoiced in the knowledge they possessed, until they supposed they had encompassed all that Allah had commanded, all He knew, and all He willed of His creation. So they said Allah would send no messengers after their messengers, and it never crossed their minds that beyond the knowledge they had reached there could be any doctrine at all. They ruled that Allah should cut off His proof from His creation and His deputies in His earth. They made Allah's knowledge in His servants, and what He sent down upon His prophets and messengers, no more than the reach of their own minds — so that whatever passed beyond it was, to them, error. By that opinion they went astray and led many astray. By their own ruling they made lawful what Allah had forbidden, and by their own opinion they killed the prophets of Allah. I take refuge for you in Allah that you should follow the whims of a people who went astray before you. Men of intellect may have their passions bent by every meaning and be deceived down every road — save only the road of religion, for in seeking it there is safety, and its excellence is not hidden."

They said: "O Abu Malik, we will not disobey your command, and your counsel is not lost on us. We would not obey you at the beginning of our affair only to oppose you at its end. You have pointed us to an opinion and hinted at a course of seeking. If you see fit, take us to these people, that their doctrine may be laid open to us and we may look into it. If it is right guidance we will follow it, and if it is error we will avoid it."

Abu Malik said: "This is doubt on your part about what you hold, and one who doubts neither knows his own right nor knows the wrong of another."

They said: "Then we will stand firm upon our truth and give the lie to whatever else there is, be it what it may."

Abu Malik said: "That is not how meaning is sought, nor is guidance found through calling things lies."

They said: "How, then, is the seeking to be done, if we neither deny falsehood nor affirm the truth?"

Abu Malik said: "If you already know the truth and accept it, and know falsehood and reject it, then you are not among the seekers at all. You are among those learned in prophethood and guidance — the ones who judge by revelation over the people of this world."

They said: "Then we are content with the truth we hold and have no doubt in it, and we would be excused from the labor of seeking."

Abu Malik said: "Would you not be content that they should have merit over you in a single respect, only to hand them merit over you in many? Would you refuse to stand in poverty in the dwellings of the seekers, only to stand in ignorance in the dwellings of those who sit back?"

They said: "How has this come to be among us, and in what have they been given merit over us?"

Abu Malik said: "Their merit over you is in knowing what you know, and more of what you do not know. This is a matter that has come upon a people. If you do not seek them out, they will seek you out to it; and if you will not listen to them, they will strive against you in it — and so they will gain merit over you both by being first to seek and by their striving. As for sitting back from seeking, that is nothing but ignorance, neglect, and a fault: on the one who knows, until he teaches; and on the one who is ignorant, until he learns."

They said: "O Abu Malik, you have hemmed us in with argument from every side, and every word of yours is right. Now open for us a door out of your own opinion, one we can act upon."

Abu Malik said: "To be dazzled by one's own opinion is a kind of blindness, and to neglect the seeking is error. There is no shame upon seekers in their seeking, but a seeker must know the doors of seeking, so that he seeks the truth knowing how it is sought. Whoever seeks the truth without knowing how is the quickest of all to deny it, for falsehood shows its first signs in outward display and in falling in with received opinion, while truth shows its first signs in trial and in cutting against the whims. No one lets go of agreement with his own passion, and holds fast through the first onset of trial, except a man of sound heart."

They said: "Now a door has opened for us, one in which you point toward the knowledge of seeking. Show us what that knowledge is, and what the sound heart is."

Abu Malik said: "As for the knowledge of seeking, it is that you know yourselves to be in need. The one in need seeks what he needs with the humility of a poor man before the thing he seeks. And as for the sound heart, it is the heart that does not go on denying whatever comes to it, whatever it may be, until the truth is the very thing that makes itself plain and proves its own worth, and falsehood is the very thing that lays bare its own shame. Then the heart tells falsehood apart by its intellect, and receives the truth with acceptance."

They said: "But we have been told that a man in this position, who neither confirms the truth nor refutes falsehood, is ignorant in what he does. If he does not know the truth, he cannot follow it; and he cannot know falsehood, so as to shun it."

Abu Malik answered them, "What you have been told, you have believed. But has not this man passed beyond the rank of the liars, so that he is, as it were, two ignorant men gathered into one?"

They said: "Yes. To our mind he is ignorant, not a liar."

"Then know," said Abu Malik, "that the ignorant man is the nearer to hope, for he seeks the good and pursues it. He seeks knowledge for himself; and if he does not, the learned seek it out on his behalf, because ignorance is the original condition of every creature, and there is no shame in it for one who seeks knowledge on account of his ignorance. Lying, though, is the worst of all wares. The liar is never guided by his lying, and he is never believed in what stands recorded against him."

They said: "You have made this plain to us. We have come to know the defect of lying, and so we will never deny a thing until we know it for what it is. You have also opened for us the door of the request, through reverence toward the thing sought. But tell us: this reverence and humility toward the thing sought — is it to be shown after it has shown its right, or before the statement is given?"

"Before the statement," he said, "so that by your reverence you may arrive at the statement."

They said: "And if we revere the thing sought until its content becomes clear, and then its statement turns out to be misleading, what becomes of our reverence? Has it not been thrown away?"

"No," said Abu Malik. "It was reverence toward God and humility before His command, and its reward is yours even if the one before whom you humbled yourself did not deserve it. For the seeker has discharged the duty of the request, though he has not attained the duty of the truth. He is like a man who stands before a king to ask for some gift, and in the asking has nothing open to him but reverence, flattery, and the pressing of his plea. If the king grants him something of what he asked, the king has discharged the duty of his own bounty and earns the reward of what he gave. If he grants nothing, the petitioner has still discharged the duty of asking with gentleness, and it is niggardliness that has bound the other to withhold. So it is with the seeker of knowledge. He must begin with humility, persistence, and patience under trial, until he has fulfilled the duty of the request; and after that, time will bring him what it brings."

They said: "This is the plain and manifest truth. Will you permit us, then, to go out to the people as you have counseled us? We will not reckon what you have commanded a burden."

Abu Malik said: "I would never counsel you to a thing and then hold myself back from it. Were it not for the fear of falling short by turning you aside from the path of Allah, I would have sent you on ahead and gone before you to the good, keeping that dignity for myself and preferring my own soul above every other. But we will go together. I give you my own company as an honor to you, and in thanks for your having singled me out for this good."

They said: "It is as though you had been expecting this matter before today."

He said: "This precise matter, no. But I have never ceased to expect deliverance from Allah on every side, hoping for good by every road, turning my ear toward every speaker and asking an increase from his words, whether he speaks truly or falsely."

They said: "An increase from the words of the truthful, we understand. But what do you hope to gain from the words of the false?"

"An increase of truth upon truth is light," said Abu Malik, "and to recognize falsehood for what it is increases one's certainty; and the greater the certainty, the greater the light of the heart. None are saved with Allah but the people of certainty. I am now rising to go out to the people. If you are resolved to rise with me, then let each of you look to himself. Whoever knows a sin in himself, let him repent to Allah; whoever owes a debt, let him repay it to its owner. Wash yourselves clean of every impurity, put on for the request the purest garments of resolve, and bring to the thing sought the intention of the needy — for this is what I myself am doing. Our going out is a going out to Allah. If He knows truthfulness in us, He will grant us success to His truth; and if He knows in us any mockery of the faith, He will turn us away from His signs by the slightest stumble, keeping His friend unsullied by the mockers."

They said: "Yes. We will do what you have commanded, within three days."

"No," he said.

"Two days, then?"

"No — this very day," he said. "If you will do it, do it; otherwise I go on alone."

They said:

> لا قوة إلا بالله

> There is no power except in Allah.

"We repent to Allah of every sin, we set aside every debt, and we do what you have commanded."

So they did so that same day, and they went out all together until they came to the gate of al-Bakhtari. When they had greeted him and sat down, Abu Malik said to al-Bakhtari, "O Abu Salih (أبا صالح), how is your good son, and where is he?"

Al-Bakhtari said: "Today my father is Salih, and I am the son of Salih; and Salih is with his Lord."

Abu Malik supposed from these words that Salih had died, and he said:

> إنا لله وإنا إليه راجعون

> To Allah we belong, and to Him we return.

"Has Salih died?"

Al-Bakhtari said: "Salih has not died, and he will never die. This is no turn of phrase. He abides as long as time itself abides."

Abu Malik said: "Here are three matters whose meanings have been turned about. The child has become the father; the Lord is found upon the earth, and this Lord has a servant through whom one comes to Him; and the one created to perish has become everlasting. A whiff of fragrance for the perplexed seeker — and behind this lie wonders greater still."

Al-Bakhtari said: "O Abu Malik, name your need, and be deliberate in your asking, for you stand at the very threshold of the ordeal."

"You have spoken truly," said Abu Malik. "The first of the truth is an ordeal. My need is a meeting with the righteous servant."

"There is no way to that," said al-Bakhtari. "Yet perhaps you mean my son, Salih?"

"Yes," he said.

"That is by his own leave," said al-Bakhtari.

So the old man rose in haste until he came in upon his son Salih, and told him of Abu Malik and his companions, and of what he knew of the excellence of their minds. And Salih rejoiced at it, and said, "O Allah, open the hearing of the hearts of Your servants, and guide them by Your mercy to the signs of what they seek, and expand our breasts with the light of Your hidden light."

Then he passed his hand over his face, cleared the assembly for them, and bade his father bring them forward.

When they had entered upon him, greeted him, and taken their seats — Abu Malik and those below him each being seated by introduction — the first thing Salih said was, "O Abu Malik, you have honored us with your visit and outrun us to the good by your generosity, though you had the greater right that we should visit you, to guard your affection and to discharge the duty long owed to you."

Abu Malik said: "And when were you ever wanting in virtue, O Abu al-Khair (أبا الخير)? You were forbearing toward us when we were small and generous to us when we were grown. You became for us a summoner and a warner to the good. Your root is sound, your branch is high, your work is pure. Good fortune to you."

Salih said to him: "So you come to me accusing, O Kab al-Ahbar — or merely imitating? Where is that perfect mind and that noble forbearance I have always known in you?"

Abu Malik said: "Your rank is too high for accusation, and religion stands too high for imitation."

Salih said: "Then how do you name me a warner, when the warner is a prophet, and the prophet is a proof from Allah between Him and His creation, for them and against them? How can he be a proof for you when he is not a proof against you?"

Abu Malik said: "You speak truly, and that is the obligation. We do not act here upon the ease of words. We began with you by the fairest way. I saw that truthfulness has the greater claim upon a man who has set himself as a veil for religion and a champion for the truth, and has opened its doors to seekers. We have come to know the excellence of asking, and so we sought you out and set our hope in you as the one sought. Turn now to our need."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, is not the man whose excellence you have come to know also one to whom a right becomes due from you?"

"Yes," he said, "it becomes due."

"And when you came to know the excellence of asking, did you also come to know its right?"

"And what is its right?" he asked.

Salih said to him: "To know its faces, and then to seek in each the truth that is due from you."

"And what are its faces," said Abu Malik, "and how many are they?"

Salih said: "Seeking is of three faces. There is a seeker who knows, a seeker who does not know, and a seeker who knows that he does not know. Which of the three are you?"

Abu Malik said: "I have more need to know all three than to lay claim to any. How can I lay claim to a station I do not know?"

"Then how did you claim to be a seeker at all," said Salih, "when you do not know the faces of seeking?"

"I did not know them," said Abu Malik. "Explain them to me."

Salih said: "Wealth is the treasure of the people of this world, and knowledge is the treasure of the people of the Hereafter. The seeking of wealth is of three kinds: a seeker who asks for what is already his own, a seeker who asks for what is owed him at an appointed term, and a seeker who asks for the gift of alms. So too is the seeking of the faith and the treasures of the Hereafter of three faces. The first is the seeker who is himself a knower — the divine scholar, who seeks out the dead in their ignorance to revive them by his knowledge. The second is the seeker who knows — the learned man who has attained some of the degrees of knowledge and now seeks its farthest end and limit. The third is the seeker who knows himself to be known by his own ignorance — the ignorant man who knows nothing beyond knowing that he is in need, and so he seeks out the scholars in order to learn. These are the three faces, and the descriptions of the seekers."

Abu Malik said: "Yes, these are the faces of seeking, in religion and in the world. As for me, I am the seeker who knows nothing except that he is in need. So teach me."

"Did you come to know," said Salih, "or did you come to know by sight?"

"And what is the difference between the two?" asked Abu Malik.

Salih said: "Knowledge by report is tidings; knowledge by direct sight is witnessing."

"This too," said Abu Malik, "is something one needs to know."

Salih said: "As for the way of report, it is the case of an ignorant man who does not know that he is ignorant until he meets a scholar, and the scholar informs him of his ignorance; then he knows himself ignorant and in need of knowledge by the scholar's word, and his knowledge of his own ignorance has come to him as tidings. As for knowledge of one's need and ignorance by direct sight, it is the man who already knows within his own mind that he is asking about a thing but cannot see the face of its answer. His breast is straitened by his ignorance, and he hopes for no relief except by the knowledge of what he does not know, so that his very ignorance has become, through his mind, a kind of knowing. Ignorance is want, and want is narrowness, and narrowness is a severe need that drives the creature to seek breadth. And the breadth is the breadth of knowledge, for knowledge is wide and spacious. By this I know that you are in need."

Abu Malik said: "I find within myself everything you have described. I have come to know that I am in dire need and severe want. So relieve my want out of your bounty."

Salih said: "I see you rushing to make the request before you have made the ground of it firm."

"Want hurried me on by its own narrowness, and so I hurried," said Abu Malik. "I came to know the excellence of breadth, and so I sought it."

Salih said to him: "If want has hurried you, and you have come to know the excellence of breadth, then how did you arrive at knowledge of the one you seek?"

"Your excellence rang true to me, and so I made for you," said Abu Malik. "I caught the sense of what I wanted, and so I sought you out."

Salih said to him: "And how did it come to ring true to you that it is I, and not you, who am known for this breadth, that you should seek me? How did you learn that I am the one to be sought, that you should set out for me? No scholar of the prophethood pointed you toward me, that you might have knowledge; you have witnessed from me no confirming proofs, that you might have recognition. Do you not see that you are pinning your search on the ease of mere imitation, and so wronging both wisdom and yourself? You are like a man who measures from the bottom of the measuring vessel: if he leaves it unfilled, he wrongs the one to whom the measure is owed; and if he crams it down full, he wrongs himself. So it is with one who takes his knowledge by nothing but tradition. If he acts upon it, he earns no thanks; and if he abandons it, he is called a denier. And you, to my mind, are wiser than that."

Abu Malik said: "I know well that it is as you say. Yet I had hoped that the truth would be the thing to establish itself, and that I might spare you the labor of proof. For disputation breeds contention, and contention breeds grudges, since each of two adversaries is out for his own victory over the other. And when did the truth ever take hold of a man who has thrown himself into the defense of falsehood?"

Salih said to him: "You have spoken well. The truth does indeed establish itself by its own worth and honor, and when a quarrel grows large it crowds the hearts and turns them away from what is worthier. Yet there is no setting aside the way the scholars have laid down. They have said: any seed for which the ground has not first been sweetened will not thrive, and any knowledge for which the hearts have not first been purified by proof will not grow."

Abu Malik said: "Then bring forward whatever proofs you please. The words of wisdom are like a jewel. Turn it over, and its light breaks forth; lay it aside, and its worth is still not hidden."

Salih said to him: "You speak truly — but only so long as no counterfeit of coated glass is set beside the jewel; then, indeed, the jewel is not concealed. Yet when the claims grow many, and the tyrants of the world contend over the faith until they have dressed it in what is not its own and disguised it before the seekers with a false likeness, then it becomes the seeker's duty to put the jewel to the test, until its worth is made plain and the counterfeit, along with its people, stands exposed."

Abu Malik said: "You have done justice and paid the due of the truth. As for the disguising these people practice, we have come to know it and its defects, and so we rejected it and held fast to this understanding by which we came to know you, and we named it the sound jewel of speech. We took our stand upon it, and you agreed with us in it, and together we clung to its rope. If you have found a meaning more precious in its essence than this, then, upon my life, jewels do surpass one another, rank above rank. So show me where the worth of your jewel lies, by a test that makes that worth known. As for our jewel, we have known it and made it known. Say of it, then, whatever seems right to you."

Salih answered him: "As for your essence, we were generous with it, holding it a sound jewel — until we brought it before the jewelers and the people of metals, and they tested it in the forge. They did not accept it from us as a jewel. They said it was glass in disguise, and they showed us its flaws, and so it fell outside the bounds of jewelry altogether. For a true jewel rises by its own preciousness, and by the recognition of the people of insight, who prize it and vie for it, until its price climbs to every sum. Those jewelers who know it, and any who come to know it after them, never turn it away. None accept a false stone but the people of delusion, who neither buy nor sell. So it is with knowledge: it is not knowledge until it is set before those who know the science of heaven. If they accept it, it is true knowledge; and if they do not, it is idle noise. And idle noise has no place in wisdom, nor is it accepted by the wise."

Abu Malik said: "And who are these who know the science of the heavens, after the prophets and the messengers? We know of no such man in our age, nor have the people held any covenant with such men since revelation was cut off with the books. The nations are bound now by nothing but the reports handed down. What do you say to that?"

Salih said: "Any religion held by way of narration alone is not to be called religion. For religion is one, upright, kept by God in its truth, and it admits no difference within itself. But you know well the disagreement among the narrations — how one gives the lie to another — and the religion of God is far too precious for that."

Abu Malik said: "When they differ over what is narrated, they fall back on the books. Whatever agrees with the books, they accept; whatever contradicts them, they reject as false."

Salih said to him: "But what of that which the books give only in the general, unspecified? Who, then, makes it specific for them? And what they give in detail, who joins it back into the whole for them? Who among them is trusted with what God has made lawful and forbidden? I heard you on the day of the dispute, when a distressed man came to you and said: O Abu Malik, I swore an oath of divorce and then broke it. You said to him: Some hold that she is severed from you and not lawful to you until she marries another husband, and some hold that she is still your wife and is not forbidden to you by a single pronouncement of divorce. So the man said to you: Then which of the two am I to act upon? And you told him: Act upon whichever of the two you wish. He rose from your presence not knowing whether the home he was returning to was lawful or forbidden. Is this how judgment is rendered in the religion of God? Is this how God's lawful and forbidden are settled?"

Abu Malik said: "By my life, the Sharia (الشريعة) of my age has been ruined and left in suspense, and we cling to nothing but the name of religion. What, then, is the state of religion with you?"

Salih said to him: "And what is religion, O Abu Malik?"

He said: "Command and prohibition, the lawful and the forbidden, the practices and the obligations."

Salih said to him: "And is the Sharia, in your eyes, anything other than this?"

Abu Malik said: "I know of nothing in it beyond what I have described to you."

Salih said to him: "Then you have gathered religion and Sharia onto a single ground?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said to him: "Then religion has been suspended by the suspension of the Sharia, and ruined by its ruin. What remains with you?"

Abu Malik fell silent from any answer for a time, his head bowed, turning over in his mind the matter of his religion. Then he raised his head and said: "I do not see that anything remains with me. So what is religion in your eyes?"

Salih said to him: "Religion, with me, is that whose veil is made firm against the corrupters and whose doors are opened to the seekers, so that its bearer is honored by coming first, and its seeker attains the good. By its worth deeds are ennobled, and by its justice the laws come into bloom. It is a bond stretched from heaven to earth, joined and never severed — the handhold of those who grasp firmly, the rope of those who hold fast, the ark of tranquility, the ship of deliverance, and the light of life. Its substance is generous, its sciences are guarded, its jewels are marked, and their worth is hidden from no one who looks upon them."

Abu Malik said: "If God has a religion by which men will one day be judged, with which He is pleased, for which He rewards and by which He punishes, then it must be as this description has it. And now that the excellence of the description has grown clear, how then is the One so described?"

Salih said to him: "The good comes only out of what is good, O Abu Malik. Set your light in the lamp-niche of your reason, and take hold of what is meant for you. The claims of destiny have pulled you in every direction, and the robes of delusion have dazzled you, and you have taken upon yourself, in your own affair, what none but you can carry — together with all you have shouldered of the affairs of these people who follow you in good faith. They have listened to your word, accepted your guidance, and trusted your judgment. So it falls to you to shield yourself and them from a fire you have no escape from entering. Seek, then, the way to be saved from it. You are the most favored of them in the grace of God and the most bound of them to give Him thanks, for I have known you to recognize the good and to hope for its reward. As for what you asked — that I confirm what I have called you to — it is my duty to establish it for you by whichever of the ways you wish."

Abu Malik said: "All that you have said thus far is proven and true. As for the narrations of the common people, religion is not set right by them, nor are they fit for it. Nothing remains for us but to learn what it is you call us to."

Salih said to him: "I call you to the justice of God and His oneness."

Abu Malik said: "How excellent is that to which you call. We ourselves profess this very saying, and we know its excellence above every other doctrine."

Salih said to him: "That is not where your mind has gone, O Abu Malik. The doctrine you intend is a description of justice with no knowledge of the One described, and a swerving among the names of God with no knowledge of His oneness. What I call you to is the knowledge of the justice of God and the landmarks of His unity. If you wish, I will halt you before the corruption of that saying, wherever you please."

Abu Malik said: "If the saying of the people of justice is corrupt, and their profession of oneness a swerving into denial, then there is no firm truth left on the earth — unless it be some meaning set apart from all the doctrines. So make plain to us the corruption of justice and of oneness, and where it comes from."

Salih said to him: "Justice and oneness are among the noblest of all doctrines. Oneness is too great to be corrupted, for it has no opposite; and justice cannot be corrupted, for it is the ruling that stands between God and His creation. But all these doctrines are spoiled by the corruption men have worked into the saying of justice and the rest of it. It is a bare claim by which no statement is proved, and they do not encompass in knowledge what they have said, until they surrender it back to the command of God. So defend whichever of the two you wish, and take it for your own, until I strip it from you by the argument of truth."

Abu Malik said: "I will not defend a saying I do not hold, nor one whose author was the one questioned rather than I. I do not set myself up as any man's agent, nor am I the author of these doctrines, nor a guardian over the man who advances such claims. I will defend before you nothing but my own saying. I say that God is One, that nothing is like Him, that He is just in His ruling, that He does not burden His servants with what they have no power to carry. He commanded them and forbade them, not by binding decree nor by compulsion, but His command is a granting of choice and His prohibition a warning. So the obedient did not obey through any trickery of His, and the disobedient did not disobey because He overpowered them. This is my meaning, and this is what I hold."

Salih said to him: "Did I not tell you that this doctrine is among the noblest? And yet these are names and attributes of no use unless a meaning lies behind them. It is as when you say: fire is hot. You have spoken truly — but with a truth that profits you nothing, because your saying fire is hot is a name and an attribute whose mere mention brings you nothing, if by it no food is cooked and no darkness is lit, and men are left in the dark with no meaning of fire present among them. Were names and attributes to stand in things in place of their meanings, then the mere mention of fire would burn your tongue, and the mention of food would fill you."

Abu Malik said: "As for the saying about these things, you have spoken truly, for their names and attributes are of no use unless their meaning is present. So show me that my saying about justice and oneness is names and attributes that call for the knowledge of a Named and Described One."

Salih said to him: "Your saying that God is One: now One is four letters, and we see two of them joined and two apart. Which of them is the one — or are all of them together the one? And your saying Allah is four letters: is the alif (الألف) alone the God, or are all the letters together Allah, or are they signs that point to God? Or do you say otherwise?"

Abu Malik was struck silent for a time. Then he said: "I find no way out of this except to say that they are signs pointing to God."

Salih said to him: "Which of them points to God? And by what do they point to Him — by being apart, or by being joined? Or by some standing alone while others are joined together, as in your word Allah, where the alif stands alone and the three letters are linked? Which of them, then, points to God? This one, by standing apart? Or these, by their joining?"

Abu Malik said: "I do not know what to say to you in this. Take us on to some other door."

Salih said to him: "Then the door to the knowledge of oneness has been shut before you, O Abu Malik, by whoever spoke to you in this fashion."

He said: "Yes."

Salih said to him: "Then let me ask you about your knowledge of God. Where is it from, and from whom did you take it? Is it from an attribute that pointed you to One described?"

He said: "No."

Salih said: "Then from a name that points to One named?"

He said: "Yes."

Salih said: "And what is the name that pointed you?"

He said: "Allah."

Salih said: "Is Allah a name, or the named?"

He said: "The named."

Salih said: "Then what is the name?"

He said: "Allah."

Salih said: "So Allah is a name for Allah."

He said: "Yes."

Salih said: "Then what is the difference between the name and the named? And which of the two do you worship — or is it both?"

Abu Malik was bewildered for a time, turning his answer over in his mind. Then he said: "I find no way in this except that I have looked upon all the crafts, and every one of them has a maker; and so I judged that this vast creation has a Creator who resembles nothing of His creation, just as the craftsmen do not resemble the things they have made."

Salih said to him: "O Abu Malik, you have carried us away from the first meaning, and the strait place in the meaning of oneness has driven you to seek Him by analogy and to set up likenesses for Him. But God is too exalted for that, for everything made is made from some thing, whereas God did not create things from any thing that was before them. As for these craftsmen, you know them only because you have watched some of them with your own eyes and seen how they work. So you passed judgment upon the making of what you never witnessed, judging a maker you never saw by what you observed of an action resembling the actions of the craftsmen you have seen and known, drawing the conclusion out by comparison. But when God created, He made you no witness to the creation of what He made, nor did He set up any likeness that works as He works. You have testified, by the likeness present before you, to a likeness that is absent. I have never known you, O Abu Malik, to be content with analogy in your religion — so how are you content to seek the knowledge of your Lord by measurement?

"And your saying, nothing is like Him, proves, by your own words, that He has a likeness, and that nothing is like that likeness. Had you meant true oneness, you would have said, there is nothing as He is, and you would not have said,

> لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِ شَيْءٌ

there is nothing like His likeness. Glory be to the One whom no reaching of thought can grasp and whom no soaring of the pointing hand can attain in comprehension. He is exalted, far above the description of those who are charged with duty. O Abu Malik, had you turned this quest toward one of His servants, or a king from among His angels, it would have left you helpless to comprehend what is said of how he was made. How then with his Creator? The lights of the people of the earth fall short of grasping the light of the sun, and the sun is but one of the lights of this world. So how shall the one who falls short of grasping a servant of His ever grasp the Creator? Say now what appears to you."

Abu Malik bowed his head to the ground in his bewilderment, and could find no path to speech.

Salih said to him: "O Abu Malik, raise your head and say what is present with you to say."

Abu Malik said: "Speech is gone, and there is no speech. Minds are bewildered before the answer, imaginations are struck dumb before thought, and the instrument is too weak to grasp anything beyond seeking and asking."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, this One of such greatness — do the eyes perceive Him?"

He said: "No. He is higher than that."

Salih said: "Is speech heard from Him?"

He said: "There is no speech but from a speaker, and the speaker is described by his speech, and God is too great for the attributes."

Salih said to him: "This Vast One of greatness, this High One of power — could He be unjust in His creation, so that He charges His creatures with what they cannot bear, and commands them with what they cannot grasp, and then punishes them if they fall short?"

Abu Malik said: "Whoever charges the obedient one with what he cannot bear has charged him with disobedience, and to punish him for it is wrongdoing. And God is too exalted for that."

Salih said: "O Abu Malik, this One who is thus far above injustice and above all wrongdoing — would He be content that ignorance should be the companion of His servants?"

Abu Malik said: "They learned this from God."

Salih said: "If He is higher than any sight can reach, wider than any perception can grasp, and greater than the limits of speech, then how does the one whom He forbids and commands come to know His command, so as to obey rather than disobey, and to give thanks rather than deny — unless you grant that there are causes for this knowledge set between Him and His creation? Must there not be those who deliver His justice and bear witness to His witnessing, for them and against them, chosen by His knowledge and selected out of all His creation, made messengers to them and proofs against them, so that they stand as the just ones between Him and His servants, conveying His command and His prohibition, so that obedience to them is obedience to God and the winning of His pleasure, and disobedience to them is His wrath and His punishment? Otherwise, how could the command reach God's creation at all?"

Abu Malik said: "This, to me, belongs to the very foundations of justice. Once creation stands in need of God's justice to teach them what He wills from them, by the hand of the righteous one He has appointed for it, then each will thank his benefactor, be rewarded by Him as He wills, be held to account by Him, and be punished by Him if He wills."

Salih said to him: "Is it permissible to rule with justice in any way other than this?"

He said: "It is permissible for no one but God, and God is the wisest of judges."

Salih said: "Would it then be permissible for Him to delegate His command to some of His people, setting them up justly against their own wrongdoing, and then to assign others to the very same charge, yet without guiding these later ones by a just proof as He had guided the first?"

Abu Malik said: "The judgment can only be one, because justice is one. Were the ruling split into two meanings, one of the two would inevitably fall into wrongdoing."

Salih said to him: "Has it not then been established for you that the judgment of God is one, with no difference within it, and that His way is one, unchanged in His messengers? For were it not for the succession of the messengers and the standing of command and prohibition, the truth would have no growth and creation no meaning."

He said: "Yes, this is the truth."

Salih said: "And is not that succession sent from God to His creation so that He may impose obedience upon them and make the messengers an authority between Himself and His creatures — to bear witness for those who obey them, so that their obedience is accepted, and to bear witness against those who disobey them, so that they are turned away?"

Abu Malik said: "The judgment could not have been otherwise. Were it so, He would have belied His messengers and broken His promise, and God is too generous for that. The affair of the messengers has made plain that they are of His justice, and that people are made to worship Him by obeying them. But tell me of the ranks of these just ones before God. Are they equal, or have some of them precedence over others?"

Salih said to him: "How could they stand level in rank when they are bound in obedience one to another? Were they equal in station, none of them would owe obedience to another. Rather, by His justice He has given some precedence over others, so that obedience may pass from the lesser to the greater, and from the greater to the one above him, until obedience and worship reach through them the very limit that God intends."

Abu Malik said: "Then the one who obeys the superior among them, and the superior one himself — are they equal in reward?"

He said: "Yes. This is the justice of God, which secures to every obedient one his reward. One is not to ask whether the measure of a superior or of a lesser applies."

Abu Malik said: "And is each of them the like of his fellow?"

He said: "No. For they come from the Creator bearing knowledge of the unseen and the Books He has sent down upon them, and no one can bring the like of them except from God."

He said: "Then can anyone imitate the lesser among them, when no book is sent down to him carrying revealed knowledge of the unseen?"

He said: "No one can lay claim to that station."

He said: "And how is that, when the lesser one does not display the hidden things among creation as the superior one displayed them of himself?"

Salih said to him: "If the superior one set himself apart from others by knowledge of the unseen in the revelation sent down, the lesser one likewise sets himself apart from others by knowledge of the unseen in the interpretation of that revelation. Both the sending down and the interpreting are from God, and what is with God is not attained except by revelation."

Abu Malik said: "And the lesser one too is inspired?"

He said: "Yes, and for that reason obedience to him became obedience to God."

Abu Malik said: "But if you set him level with the superior one in obedience and in revelation, why did you name him the lesser?"

Salih said: "By his obedience to the superior one and his need of him."

Abu Malik said: "And what need has he of a creature, when the revelation reaches him from the Creator?"

Salih said to him: "Even though revelation comes to him, it comes to him with the interpretation of the reports and the Books, and of what remains of what the superior ones left behind. So it was revealed to Lot (ع) by the hand of Abraham (ع), and Abraham's precedence was made plain; and so it was revealed to Ishmael and Isaac (عليهم السلام), while the goodness, the blessings, and the merit were set upon Abraham. Thus revelation from God is joined to His just ones in His earth, each according to his rank. He does not, in His justice, will to cut off His command and His prohibition and His worship from His servants after He has favored them with it, so that worship should be severed and ignorance made lawful."

Abu Malik said: "The bearer of the revelation has shown his truthfulness by the knowledge of the unseen within his Book. By what does the bearer of the interpretation prove that knowledge of the unseen is with him, so that the revelation bears witness for him and makes obedience to him alone binding, as it did for the bearer of the revelation?"

Salih said to him: "He brings, in his interpretation, knowledge from heaven and a declaration from the highest assembly, and the Book itself bears witness to it."

Abu Malik said: "Then I bear witness to what the Books have borne witness to: that every interpretation which the revelation attests is itself revelation from God. Otherwise all the Books and the messengers would have to be rejected, since their meaning is one."

Then he said to him: "If some of them give the lie to others, then all must be reckoned liars; and if some confirm others, then all must be confirmed."

Abu Malik said to him: "Such is justice, and by this the truthfulness of the one who confirms it, and his merit, are known. Obedience is made binding to the messengers of firm resolve, who are His just ones in His creation, whom He charged with delivering His message. Yet if obedience is binding by prophethood, by the sending down, and by the interpretation, then what of those who seat themselves in the councils of the prophets, resembling them though they are not of them? They have all agreed that after their own prophet there is no prophet and no warner in their time, that they themselves are the ones who uphold the command of God and His religion, and that what they say leads to God's reward or His punishment. Yet you say otherwise. How, then, did the scholars of this community fail to look into this age?"

Salih said to him: "It is not that they left it wholly unexamined, but that they did not look into it with a scrutiny that could be sound for them, and no one spoke of it to them so that they might incline to it with their minds. For it had been made to sink into their hearts that they are the more entitled to the command of God and His religion, and that there is no prophet after their prophet — just as those before them among the seducers of the vanished nations said. So every nation claimed that there is no prophet after its own prophet."

Abu Malik said: "And what was the beginning of that, and what did they intend by it?"

Salih said to him: "The originators of it were three in every age: a rebellious devil, a stubborn tyrant, and a hypocritical jurist. By this saying they sought to sever the traces of the prophets from the marks of prophethood, and to make it a possession passed among themselves. They could not accomplish it until they had made this saying sink into the hearts of the nations, addressed to every nation: that God sent none better than your prophet, that there is no prophet after him and no warner. So they whispered to them from where they did not perceive it, and drew them away from where they did not know it. Every nation clung to its own prophet by holding all who came after him to be liars, supposing that this drew them nearer to their prophet; and every nation held that every prophet after its own was a liar. Thus, by this saying, the remembrance of prophethood and the marks of the prophets were cut off from all the nations together. They did not seek what they had lost, and would not have believed had they sought it. So the seducing devils gained power over those they seduced and led them astray; the tyrants gained the means to kill the prophets and killed them; and the doubt of the evil jurists worked its way in, so that they made the falsehood fair and were destroyed and destroyed others. All of this so that the hearts of creation would not incline toward the prophets when they appeared, nor seek them out when they were lost — until despair of the prophets carried them so far that when the prophets called them openly to the truth they were called liars, and the prophets were killed among them and they felt no anger.

"This is the nation of the Magi, clinging to its rebellion, worshipping its idols, devoted to its fires, claiming that God will send no messenger after their own. And this is the nation of the Torah, clinging to its Scripture, devoted to its calf, standing at the bounds its prophets set, humbling itself before its Judge, claiming that God will raise no messenger after Moses (ع). And this is the nation of the Gospel, speaking in its parables, claiming that its prophet is its God, drawing near by its crosses, exalting its rabbis, and saying that God will send no messenger after Jesus (ع). And this is your own community, which has walked the very paths of those before it, so that whoever led the others astray led it astray as well. It took the Book from hands other than its rightful people, carried the religion outside its true way, followed its leaders into error, obeyed its masters in misguidance; its jurists bowed before the tyrants and raised up for them a religion apart from the established ways, performing the prayer outside its time and paying the alms other than as it is due. They are agreed to bear with these men, forever in need of them, certain they can do without anyone else, saying that God will send no messenger and no warner after their own. So you and men like you followed the word of the great mass, overawed by the sheer weight of consensus and the power of the sultans. You accepted it from them, dressed it in the fairest of your doctrines, adorned it with the finest of your words, and built upon it an argument of justice that seemed to leave no argument against you. Thus you followed the tyrants above you out of dread, and those below you followed you out of desire, and they all took it to be the truth.

"Now if this community must be believed in the sentence it has passed against God — that His proofs have been severed from His creation by a single strand of consensus — then the earlier nations have the better claim to be believed, for they hold three qualities that make their argument against you binding, and in which they are the more truthful, not you."

Abu Malik said: "And what are these qualities, in which they are given precedence over us, so that we must believe them if their word is true, or call them liars if their word is astray?"

Salih said to him: "Their saying is like your saying, and your saying is of a kind with theirs. Your hearts resemble one another, and yet they hold the precedence over you in the argument. The first quality: they claim they came earlier in the religion of God than you, so they have the merit of those who went before. The second: there is consensus among them, and between them and you, in affirming what they hold, so they have the merit of the truthful. The third: your own testimony on their behalf that their prophets are truthful and are messengers of God, while none of them has ever borne witness for your prophet, to neither his prophethood nor his truthfulness. So they have precedence over you by your agreement with them and their standing apart from you, and they say that the knowledge you possess is copied from their knowledge, which is the very source of your own. This is their argument against you — clear in speech and plain in justice. If the ruling of creation is to be imposed upon the Creator, then the ruling of these people is the more binding. But if the Creator is the One who rules over His creation, abrogating what He wills and confirming what He wills, with none to overturn His judgment, then by my life the saying of them all falls away. The Creator is foremost over the creation and over the command, and every day He is engaged in an affair, and His act is not to be denied, even were He to send a warner every day."

> وإنه كل يوم هو في شأن

"And truly, every day He is engaged in some affair."

Abu Malik said: "This community has wandered into a far error."

Salih said to him: "Most of those before them wandered first. For they cast the friends of God out of their rightful place, set the foolish above them in the command, and made lawful what God had forbidden — and did so on purpose, so that nothing might resemble prophethood, so that people would forget their longing for the prophets and would feel no stirring to kill them for it. Instead they diverted them with kingship and lavish gifts and led them along in the pursuit of their own whims. In this they were aided by men outwardly known for truthfulness and piety, who paraded it before the people, until the very one who used to command the community to good turned it away from the good, and the one who used to forbid it evil steered it toward evil, and those faithful to the friends of God grew few. So truth and falsehood came to alternate like light and darkness as they turned. Were it not for the remnants of God's friends and His just ones upon the earth, and the revelation that comes to them, the little truth could never have prevailed over the much falsehood. For by what else would God set the foul apart from the pure, except by His just servants? And how could God's proof reach all creation, except through them?"

Abu Malik said: "There is no way and no escape for the justice of God and His command, amid the multitude of His opponents, except by the just servants of God. Through them the proof of God against His creation is made sound."

Salih said to him: "Then, since it is established that the company of God's prophets are the just ones, is not each single one of them called just?"

He said: "Yes, he is a just one, as it is said:"

> شهد فلان وهو عدل من العدول

"So-and-so bore witness, and he was a just one among the just."

Salih said to him: "Then it is settled for you that the just one is the friend of God, and the matter has passed beyond your saying that it is a mere attribute, and has become one who bears the attribute?"

He said: "Yes, this is sound in speech."

Salih said to him: "And whoever does not reach the one so described, does the description profit him?"

He said: "The description does not profit him except by reaching the one it describes."

Salih said: "Then whoever does not know the just one of Allah and His guardian — will his own speech be just?"

At this the narrator relates that Abu Malik smiled. "You have drawn me out of my own chapter on justice and monotheism," he said, "and nothing has drawn me out of it but the truth itself, in the clearest of arguments and the gentlest of speech. After this, what position is left to prove?"

"From here, O Abu Malik," Salih answered, "the disguised glass falls away from those who trade in the colors of jewels. They could never tell its worth; but set it before one who knows, and the pretenders stand exposed, and those they deceived are exposed along with them."

Abu Malik said: "I have believed, and I will act upon it. Yet how are we to be kept safe from the misleaders of the nations, and to know the causes of Allah — even one cause among them — when we do not reach them in their own time? For in our age there are neither prophets nor messengers, as there were in the nations before us."

"Glory be to Allah, O Abu Malik," Salih replied. "You have spoken of the justice of Allah all your life; yet the moment we prove it to you in your very presence, where it becomes binding upon you, you turn back, claim you would be deceived about Allah, and lay injustice at His door."

"God forbid," Abu Malik said. "Whoever says such a thing has transgressed and lied against Allah. Where have you bound me to that?"

"By your own words," said Salih, "that the age of the prophets has passed, and that in our time there is neither prophet nor messenger. Tell me: was the sending of those earlier ranks a just act of His toward His servants, or was it in vain?"

"God forbid," he answered. "Nothing but what is just issues from Him."

"Then what has shut us out of that justice?" said Salih. "Are we not His servants as they were? They were created as we are created, and we are bound in service as they were bound. Has the obligation been lifted from us after it was laid upon them? Or has His giving run dry in our age, so that we lean only on what was granted to them? Or has the way of Allah changed in our case after it stood firm for those before us, so that we must rule that God Himself has changed? Or is justice a duty upon us while the very instrument of it is judged lawful for us to do without — though it was forbidden to those before us — when there is no substitute for the words of Allah and none to overturn His judgment? Who deserves the name of the just except the guardians of Allah? Among all that God created there is no just one save the one whom God has chosen, who became just by His command. This is why a judge is named just — for the sake of the one who appointed him — and a true word is named truthful, because of the truthfulness of the one who speaks it."

Abu Malik said: "It is indeed so, and we are permitted to say nothing of God but the truth. Yet when we followed our own opinion in speech and judgment, in the absence of the rightly guided among us, we abandoned His judgment. We supposed this was part of Allah's ruling and His way with us, as it had been with those before us in the interregna between one messenger and the next — the stretch between Abraham and the days of Moses and the Torah, and the stretch between the Torah and the days of Jesus and the Gospel. We took our stand on that same footing, working through the interregnum, holding fast to the traditions and the way."

"The religion of Allah is far too precious to Him for that," Salih answered. "Was there ever truly an interregnum after the religion of Abraham, when Allah sent after him Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Jonah, and Shuayb (شعيب), on down to Moses? He inspired them with righteous deeds, with the call to the religion of Abraham, with the establishment of prayer in its way and the giving of zakat (زكاة) as it is due, until the ruling of the Torah was sent down upon Moses. And what interregnum stood between Moses and the Gospel, when after Moses Allah raised up Joshua son of Nun, and Elias, Talut (طالوت), David, Solomon, Zechariah, and John, on down to Jesus? They upheld the ruling of the Torah, held to its religion, called to it, and recited its Psalms, until the ruling of the Gospel came down upon Jesus. So it was with those who passed before them and those who came after, and with the many generations between them whom none but Allah can number. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs; for each He struck parables, and to each He called the way to the truth. Where, then, is this interregnum, and when was it? Allah said only:

> عَلَىٰ فَتْرَةٍ مِّنَ الرُّسُلِ

"After a lull in the succession of messengers,"

and by that very word He affirmed the messengers. The lull was in the open rising of the call, once fear had come; so they called in secret and concealment. Why, then, would they be cut off from the earth? Not for the blink of an eye has the earth been emptied of the justice of Allah — either present and manifest, or fearful and veiled."

"This is a statement that cannot be refuted," said Abu Malik, "a proven word in which there is no doubt. The proof of Allah's argument, through justice and the succession of messengers, has been established and made plain. Show me, then, the meaning of their absence in our own time. What is its cause?"

"What was the cause of Moses' absence," Salih replied, "when he went out of Pharaoh's city and journeyed on in fear, watchful, until he reached the land of Shuayb?"

"He fled from Pharaoh's people," Abu Malik said, "hiding from them, because he feared for himself when they had agreed to kill him."

"And have you heard, in this law of yours, of any of the just — the just ones of Allah and the sons of His prophets — who was killed while patiently bearing the burdens of the religion?"

"Yes," Abu Malik said. "Many of them have been killed."

"Then what justice of this age do you wish to appear to you and to the people of your time?" said Salih. "One who was slain, whom Allah would raise up and send to you after his death? Or one who fled from among you, whom you would drive back to the very killing he escaped — when he has followed the way of Allah in His prophets and messengers, patient under fear and concealment, until Allah judges between him and us, and He is the best of judges?"

"There is no blame," Abu Malik said, "upon one who follows the way of the righteous by fearing for himself and fleeing from his people. The blame lies upon the wicked nations and the helpers of the powers of darkness, for killing the prophets of Allah and His just ones, and for their agreement, one and all, upon that deed."

"And what do you say of one who kills the just of Allah and His proof," Salih asked, "where does his end lie? And what of the one who helped him?"

"Both are in the Fire," Abu Malik said, "and so too anyone who raised a banner for them or carried an inkwell in their cause."

"And what do your jurists say of this?"

"They ask mercy for the slain," he said, "yet see no sin in the killer."

"And whoever obeys the killer of his own free will," said Salih, "is he not a partner with him in the blood of the slain?"

"Yes, by Allah," Abu Malik said. "He is his partner — and worse than he."

"His partnership is plain enough in his aiding him," Salih said. "But how is he worse than the killer himself?"

"Were it not for the narrations of the jurists," Abu Malik answered, "the rule of the tyrants could never have stood through the folly of the foolish."

"You have spoken the truth, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "And have you been content, for yourself, to make their kingdom your home and their land your dwelling, their armies your neighbors and their enforcers your closest friends? You are their just man: you judge for them by your learning and meet your own needs through their rule. You have prayed for them in your supplications and thanked them for whatever you sought at their hands. You have crowned their tyranny with your justice, and they grew proud; you have robed their vainglory in the garment of the sacred law, and they overreached; you have lit up their falsehood by letting your own truth grow dim. So the weak were led astray by the example you set, and with them every scholar of your kind, and they said, These are the jurists of the community; in them we have our model. You went astray by your own reasoning, and you led others astray by the reasoning of the tyrants; you imagined the one who would be held to account was someone other than yourselves, and you forgot the word of Allah, Glorified is He:

> وَلَا تَرْكَنُوا إِلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا فَتَمَسَّكُمُ النَّارُ

"And do not incline toward those who have done wrong, lest the Fire touch you.

"So you cut apart what Allah commanded to be joined, and you did it with open eyes; you spoke of the outward forms of justice while straying far from its path. You came upon His saying:

> أَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ وَأُولِي الْأَمْرِ مِنكُمْ

"Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you,

"and you took these powers of darkness for your rulers, even in their disbelief. And you forgot the saying of Allah, Exalted is He:

> قَاتِلُوا الَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُم مِّنَ الْكُفَّارِ

"Fight those of the disbelievers who are near to you.

"So you disobeyed the ones you were commanded to obey, and obeyed the ones you were commanded to fight, all the while supposing you did well. Misguidance ran its full course in you, and bare opinion carried you so far that you said Allah would never raise a messenger or a warner in your age. Is it that the goodness of your deeds has raised you beyond any need of the guardians of Allah to stand as the proof? Or that the ugliness of your deeds has cut you off from all hope that Allah might accept your repentance? Or is it justice that you should judge God unjustly, and be content, on your Messenger's behalf, with what you would never accept for yourselves were you in his place? Would any one of you be content that his son be killed, or that his house and his wealth be seized from him without right?"

"No one is content with that," Abu Malik said. "If a child of ours is killed, or a man is driven from his property, he is enraged, and his people are enraged for him, and they are not satisfied until he is satisfied."

"Then Allah and His Messenger have the greater right that you be angry for their sake," Salih said, "when His vicegerent is killed and His guardian is driven from the land."

"It grieves us," Abu Malik said, "that the guardian of Allah should be killed among us or driven from his land. But they have withdrawn from us of their own accord, and the tyrants have risen up over us in their place."

"Had you supported the friends of Allah, they would have come forth," Salih answered. "Had you forsaken His enemies, those enemies would never have prevailed. But your standing toward the guardians of Allah has settled into three ranks. There is the one who strikes and is set over them, who never tires of the hunt and is not sated even in his victory. There is the one who is pleased and gloats, biting his fingertips in spite, sparing no effort to incite others against them, never turning his gaze from them even when they are heedless. And there is the one who loathes what is done, who professes love for the guardians of Allah and yet forsakes them, having no clear sight in his faith and no trust that can be kept in confidence. For this reason the guardians of Allah have withdrawn from you in caution — for your sake, and for the sake of this vast multitude who have taken their own desires for their god, who have fallen upon the goods of this world and joined hands in a deed on a par with the killing of the prophets. They were blind to the fault in their own conduct, so they neither held themselves to account for the wrong they did nor humbled themselves to repent of it. Instead they turned away and laid the injustice of their own deeds upon Allah, and they said, Allah will send no messenger after their messenger, and no warner. So they made lawful the killing of the guardians of Allah without right. They wronged by their deeds, swerved by their words, and transgressed by their denial."

"Yes," Abu Malik said. "This is the very portrait of this nation of ours, and this is exactly what they say. Where, then, is deliverance from it, and where is the escape from the punishment of Allah?"

"To Allah," Salih said. "Take refuge in Him, and He will give you refuge. Repent to Him, and He will accept your repentance. Ask Him for the way of His guardians, and He will deliver you, through your own deeds, from the doing of those who have wronged. And so too with this multitude: if they repent to Allah, He will accept their repentance, and what would Allah then want with punishing them? Allah does not wrong them by the weight of a single atom, even when they wrong and lie; and He does not hasten them to punishment, even when they hasten to disobey Him."

"Then I seek refuge from the punishment of Allah," Abu Malik said, "by repenting to Him and seeking the means to what pleases Him. Give something to the one who seeks you and to the one who asks to be guided, and your Lord will reward you."

"If you do that," said Salih, "you will find Allah nearer to pardoning you than you are to repenting to Him, for He loves those who turn back to Him; and you will find me eager to guide you and watchful over you."

Then, at the mention of repentance, Salih's eyes filled with tears, and he broke off his speech. He heartened them, and bade them go to his father's house. And he went in to his father, the Master who had first sent him out, and took counsel with him over the affair of Abu Malik and his companions.

The Master said: "You know your own companions best. If you see good in them, guide them with your guidance; and when you warn them, do not let eagerness carry you into inflicting punishment upon them. Test them by withdrawing your attention from them, yet without harshness, and charge your elders to show them kindness and to be good to them in their season. The one who longs for good cannot stay hidden, and Allah will not fail you at your back. He will open for you, from the light of His enabling grace, that by which the affairs of His creation are set in order, and from the gates of guidance that by which He shows you the deeds of those who find their way."

So Abu Malik and his companions went on wavering in their trial until their affair was brought to completion, and they came to know their own guidance, and the guardian of Allah among them turned toward that guidance with kindness. They gave thanks to Allah for it, and they returned to their people as warners; and through them Allah guided a host of His servants to His religion.

The author said: Whatever in this account has taken the shape of a narrative was no idle telling, but a confirmation of what Allah has commanded; for in it lies the steadfastness of the messengers, the signs of the guardians, and the manners of the seekers.

Praise be to Allah, first and last. May Allah bless His Messenger, our master Muhammad, sent forth from Him to His creation as a bearer of glad tidings and a warner; and bless his guardian, the Imam (الإمام) of the God-fearing, the radiant leader of the bright-marked, the beloved of the Lord of the Worlds; and the Imams of the people of his house, whom Allah has favored and from whom He has removed every defilement, purifying them completely. Allah is sufficient for us, and how excellent a Trustee, how excellent a Master, and how excellent a Helper. There is no power and no strength except by Allah, the Most High, the Most Great.
