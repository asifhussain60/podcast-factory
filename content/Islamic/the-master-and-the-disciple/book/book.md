# The Master and the Disciple

## How to Read a Conversation Made of Doors

We have been told that a company of the believers, along with a number of those who preach the faith, came to one of the Masters among them and said: "You have set us free by reminding us of what you made us aware of, and by that awareness a duty has settled upon us that we are bound to discharge. It is a threefold duty: to give thanks for that to which you have called us, to give thanks for the knowledge you have shown us, and to give thanks for the work you have commanded us to do. So make plain to us, in each of these three, what someone who truly wishes to be thankful can actually do to render that thanks. From the bounds of religion we have already grasped what is obligatory upon us and what we are charged to perform — both what is asked of the student when he questions and what is required of the one questioned when he answers. And from the practice of the righteous and the conduct of those who seek, we have understood as much as can readily be put into words."

The Master said to them: "The matter to which I have called you is the very matter I hold in honor. May Allah stand by His servants, may He bring it to completion for them, and may He ennoble those who answer His call. For to the first of everything He has appointed a last, and to the last of everything an end, and to every end a further end whose limit is never reached. These are the houses of the hearts, and their keys are remembrance: «مفاتيحها ذكرى، وأولها بلوى، وأوسطها هدى، وآخرها تقوى». Their keys are remembrance; their beginning is affliction, their middle is guidance, and their end is piety. So whoever has sought to know the structure on which things are built, has held firm to the bounds of the obligation that lies within his reach, has set his course toward guidance by the signs of the request, and has walked that course by the best of ways —

"As for the thanks owed to the Master, it is to obey him. As for the thanks owed to the knowledge, it is to act by what it contains and to call others to it. And as for the thanks owed to the work, it is to be patient in it and to call others to it."

## 1. The Persian Who Was Dead and Revived

As for the doctrines of the righteous and the manners of the seekers: we have heard that a certain man among the people of Persia (أهل فارس) was stricken by the affliction of ignorance, even though he possessed a heart of keen memory, a sound mind, and a learning he had earned for himself. Yet ignorance held him fast where the knowledge we have spoken of was concerned, for he had been born into it and raised within it. And so he stumbled on in the fever of his thirst, taking the shimmer of the mirage for the gleam of water — until, on reaching it, he found there was nothing there at all, and found Allah waiting before him, who settled his account. Then He honored the place where he came to rest and drew back the veil from him. He found him astray and guided him, found him in need and made him rich with knowledge. By that grace he became one of the scholars of Tur (الطور) and one of those who dwell in the Bayt al-Ma'mur (البيت المعمور).

When his guidance was complete and he had come to the very end of his hope, it fell upon him as a duty to thank his Maker and to labor his way toward his Lord. Now there was a saying of his father's he had once heard, and it was to him what the soul is to the body: "The best of good deeds is to revive the dead" (إن أفضل الحسنات إحياء الأموات). So he said to himself: "I was dead, and He gave me life; ignorant, and He taught me. I was not the first to be ignorant, that I should have outrun all mankind to knowledge, nor am I the last who will ever be ignorant, that the teaching of it should end with me. Truly it is binding upon me to be among those who give thanks for this blessing — to hand this trust on to those who come after me, just as it was handed to me by those who came before. For this affair is, in its origin, from Allah; it reached me only through a long chain of causes, the first passing it to the second, and the second to the third, until it came down from the highest assembly to the creatures who are the people of this world. Then it broke through the veil, and door after door pressed it onward until it reached me. Am I, then, to be the place where it stops, its final end? Never. Those who carried it and kept faith with the trust of it have the first claim to the reward and the merit; and if their earnings are not mine to rest upon, then I am rather what their labor earned. And if their bounty does not spare me the labor, then I remain as poor in my need to seek knowledge by acting on it as I was poor in my need to seek the knowledge itself." This he said within himself, knowing that what was asked of him was the very likeness of what had been given to him, and that the end of his affair, when the account was drawn, was one with its beginning.

So he left his family and his wealth behind and went out toward his Lord, answering His call and summoning others to the good, that a share of thanksgiving might fall due to him such as the share that had fallen due from him. He set out to journey through the lands, passing among the peoples of the non-Arabs and the tribes of the Arabs, studying faces and putting his questions to them, yet finding none who answered and meeting none who sought — until at last he reached the farthest parts of the island (الجزيرة). He entered one of its cities at an hour when its people were heedless. And as he was calling the signs of Allah to mind and searching through the crowds, his eye fell upon a group of that land's people quarreling over religion with no guidance, sunk deep in the waters of their own whims. So he sat down a little apart from them, listening to their talk and reading their faces to see which among them held to the soundest path.

He said to himself: "These are the ones most fit to be revived, for three reasons. The first is that the proof has already reached them. The second is that they are a people who have a religion. And the third is that they are the nearest to right guidance, given how alert they are to religion, how they attend to it together and seek after it together — and one who seeks a thing is close to finding it."

When they had finished the discussion they were engaged in, they turned to him and said: "Who are you, young man, and where are you from?"

He said: "I am a servant of Allah (عبد الله), and one of those who dwell in His sanctuary (حرم)."

They said: "Then what is your need, and what is your work?"

## 2. A Stranger in the City

He answered them: "My purpose has already slipped past me, and I am now busy seeking it."

They said: "Did anything of what we said please you?"

He said: "All of it is strange — to anyone capable of wonder."

They said: "But wonder is of two kinds: one praiseworthy and one blameworthy."

He said: "Yes, I have come to know that. And words, too, are of two kinds: praiseworthy and blameworthy."

They said: "And which kind, in your view, is the praiseworthy?"

He said: "The kind that is true."

They said: "And what is the true?"

He said: "That which takes its origin from Allah and calls back to Allah."

They said: "And what is the blameworthy?"

He said: "That which springs from caprice and calls to something other than guidance."

They said: "You have spoken truly. Have you nothing of your own to let us hear?"

He said: "I have nothing to say that is my own. I am only a follower of what is rightly said."

They said: "Then let us hear from you whatever may show us your way. Your bearing has grown sweet to us and your fellowship dear, and we hope you may be a door of mercy that Allah has opened for us."

He said: "Very well." So he composed himself to speak, and the people grew silent before him, and he turned his face toward them.

Then he said: "He who created all things by His power and set them in perfect order, and who decreed by His generosity and so bestowed guidance — He it is who opened up the wide pastures of the way, making their surface like scattered chaff and their depths dark and rich, their paths never tangled and their tracks never worn away. Were it otherwise, imaginings would lose themselves in the search for Him and minds would fall short of reaching Him. He is not so manifest in His being that any but His own could lay hold of Him, nor so exposed that the goal of His path could be turned aside. Rather He is generous and yet veiled: His knowledge is held back because of its great worth, and His secrets are kept safe for those who are fit to bear them. Their glory is the plumage of guidance, and their fullest ornament is the garment of piety. They are marked with the light of lights — a trust laid up in the breasts of the chosen and the righteous, keepers of the Word, noble scribes,

a trust which they render up and a bound which they never overstep. It is the law of the Judge and the bounty of the Most Merciful. He ennobled it and set it deep within, He shielded it and kept it hidden, He gave it its full due and crowned it with His covenants. So the aspirations of the thinkers cannot reach it, and the contrivances of the scheming cannot lay hold of it. Its outward face is a remembrance and a proof for those who are guided, and its inner reality is a sure and abundant draught, whose sky bends low to those who gaze up and whose ground lies level for those who search, and whose falling stars keep their appointed places for the seekers. It has watering places for those who journey toward it, and signs whose wonders come one after another, pointing through their likenesses back to Him. Its course is a bestowing of favor, and its way an unfolding in detail. Its rulings are binding obligations, and its practice a whole body of law. Truthfulness has ennobled its teachings and justice has made its yield wholesome, so that He has made it, for every good, a leader and a road, and made it, for its own inner meaning, a shield and a guide. Then, through their ignorance and their need, He drove minds to seek out knowledge and sufficiency. Blessed is He who made the night and the day follow one upon the other, for whoever would remember or would give thanks. And may Allah send His blessings upon the one He chose from among His servants and made a warner to all the worlds."

Then he said: "Fear Allah with the fear of one who truly dreads Him, and dread Him with the dread of one who still hopes in Him. Seek knowledge, and you will come to know; act, and you will be shown mercy."

Then his eyes brimmed over with tears, and he broke off his words and took his leave of the people weeping, and rose and turned back toward his mount. The people were overcome with weeping at the warmth of his speech and the depth of his counsel, and they longed to rise and go with him. But good manners held them back from pressing in upon him all at once, for they did not know whether that would be welcome to him, and so they returned to their dwellings. Yet one did follow after him from among the company — a youth, the youngest of them in years and the most even-tempered of them all. Discipline had brought his mind to its full growth, and reflection had set his heart alight, and upon all this there weighed within him everything he had just heard.

## 3. The Boy at the Door — Limits and Conditions

The youth kept the Master's company, with his permission, until the Master reached the end of his journey. The boy's father was a great sheikh among the Arabs, the noblest of them in lineage and the most influential of them over the people of religion. When they arrived, the Master went in and brought the boy in with him. He called for food, and once they had finished and settled into conversation, the boy spoke.

"O wise one," he said, "you have spoken and I have listened; you have preached and I have taken it in. You have adorned the religion with the finest of ornaments, made it desirable with the best of longing and the fullest of provision, and summoned to it by the noblest of its trusts. Minds have been drawn toward your questioning through the openings your words have made in the heart. Such excellence could come only from a man whose inward reality, the batin, has been perfected into that of a teacher, and whose outward bearing, the zahir, has grown wide with forbearance — for he is a wellspring of wisdom from a firm root, whose blessings rise over the worlds, and the very branches of whose branches bear fruit for those who seek. I hope that you are one of those blessed branches, for I see in you a noble mind, a sound heart, and a perfect intellect. You have said that minds are driven by their ignorance to seek knowledge and capacity, and my own mind is among the neediest and most destitute of them. Is there, then, a way to life for me?"

The Master said: "He who created His creatures by His power did not create them in vain, nor fashion them scholars from the very start, nor does He abandon them in the wastes of ignorance, nor is He content that folly should be their culture or the garments of ignorance their dress. As an exit from all of this He has appointed for them the very faculty by which He composed their frame and completed their creation. He filled their hearing, their eyes, and their hearts with lights, as a mark of the honor He gives them and as a proof standing against them. Then He placed the treasures of His wisdom and the tokens of His will within doctrines that please Him, doctrines carried by His elect from among His creation. He charged them to hold to that religion and to call others to it, and He laid upon all mankind the duty of answering His call through the faculty we have named, so that God might set apart the corrupt from the good. Thus the seeker, by his seeking, is brought to see, and the man heedless of his own backwardness is exposed by that very heedlessness. After His Messengers, no argument remains for anyone against God, and after the evidence has been given, no excuse remains for going astray."

The boy said: "That no excuse remains has struck fear into me, and that the evidence stands has made me long for it. I beg you for a proof, and I will be your lowly servant."

The Master said: "God did not create men as scholars. He created them as small children, ignorant, knowing nothing. There is no greatness except after smallness, and no scholar except after ignorance. Smallness is the prelude to greatness, and ignorance a guide toward knowledge. In the same way, knowledge cannot be carried unless a lesser knowledge is brought in before it — one quicker than it to reach the heart and smoother for the mind to grasp — so that its first principles may take hold in the imagination, the limbs may be humbled to its obligations, the ribs may shiver at its depth, and the mind may bear its purpose. This is the way of God with His servants. It does not befit us to break from the community on our own opinion, nor, under our own guidance, to follow anything other than that way."

The boy said: "I am too small for what I desire, so raise me up. I am ignorant of what I seek, so teach me. I am bound by your favor, so have mercy on me. You yourself were once in this very state, and God drove the blessing to you at the hands of one whose testing of you was severe and whose thanks then fell due from you. I know well the plight of a man who suffers the narrowness of ignorance, and the need of one whose every resource has failed him."

The scholar said: "The state you describe is indeed as you say, and I am zealous that you should be free of it. But my mercy toward you is not to bring your hope nearer while your labor still lags behind. Every claim is accepted only when it is truthful; knowledge brings no benefit until it is acted upon, and action brings no benefit until it is accepted. The truthfulness of speech is its justice, and the proof of knowledge lies in the open roads of deeds and in the documented likenesses — in all that the far ends of the earth have held, and over which night and day have differed. Do not chase after an argument idly, do not cast one away idly, and do not accept one by mere imitation, or the first doubt to rise against it will tear it out of your heart. Do not deny an argument once its proof has stood, or you will become a partisan of falsehood; and do not give up inquiring into it and understanding it, or your judgment will be left barren. Think well of me without reserve, strive in your questioning without suspicion, seek the religion as one like you ought to seek it, and ask God's help in your affair. God does not let the reward of the seeker go to waste, nor deny recompense to anyone He sees seeking the good."

The boy said: "You have dealt justly with yourself, in fitting and effective deed, and you have pointed to the truth in the finest of words. I ask God's help, and I ask you about this matter to which you call: what is it, whose is it, and to whom does it belong?"

The scholar said: "It is the religion of God, which He has accepted, and which can be accepted from Him alone and from no other. He delivered it first to the elect among His Messengers, and then to all His creation at large. Upon the one specially favored with it lie thanksgiving for it, action by it, and the call to it. Upon the one deprived of it lies the seeking of it until he comes to know, and obedience to the one who guides him."

The boy said: "This favor that God has singled out for His friends — are all such favors equal in worth, so that men are equal in their excellence, or do some surpass others?"

The scholar said: "Have we not already said that every knowledge has before it a lesser knowledge that taught it, and that every favor has its bearers, so that they stand at graded ranks before their Lord, each charged according to what he has gained of knowledge? Then to every possessor of merit his merit is given, in justice from God toward His creation and in mercy to His servants."

The boy said: "This to which you call me — is it that very favor, or is it something other than it?"

The scholar said: "It is not identical with it in its full reality, nor is it wholly other than it. Rather it is drawn from it, as a single scoop of water is drawn from the breadth of the Euphrates. I call you to what God has accepted for His servants and honored on the day He perfected it — mighty and guarded, generous and hidden. He ennobled it with His reward, an honor for the one who upholds it, and He made its penalty a disgrace for the one who squanders it. Its outward form is figures, and its inward reality is knowledge. Its wisdom is a light, and its justice is plain. This is what I call you to."

The boy said: "What has made you more entitled to this matter than others, and more deserving of it than the rest, when you claim that it is from God for all creation alike — so that everyone lays claim to it and denies it to his neighbor? What has made you more worthy of the name of truth than they, when this matter came to you only by the same road it came to them?"

The scholar said: "There is no scholar who speaks a word of truth but that he has drawn it from the very origins from which we too have drawn, and in this they meet us, acknowledging it from us and among themselves. What set us apart from them, then, was that they abandoned those principles while we held fast to them: we obeyed where they disobeyed, we preserved what we were entrusted with where they squandered it, and we upheld what we were charged with where they neglected it. So they agree with us in acknowledgment and differ from us in deed. Their acknowledgment is a witness for us against them, and their squandering is the mark of their poverty toward us and their need of what is in our hands. They are like brothers who inherited great wealth from their father and divided it equally among themselves. Some squandered their share until they fell into poverty, while others kept theirs, guarded it, and traded with it, so that the wealth in their hands multiplied. Then the poor among the brothers turned to the rich, to serve them and to seek their bounty. So too are these who wasted the way of their Messengers: their ignorance drove them to seek knowledge from the people of the noble origins. The proof that we are more deserving of the truth than they is their need of us and our sufficiency without them. So it is that our scholar has become the one who answers their ignorant, our clothed one covers their naked, and our rich one feeds their hungry. Thus the truth is bound to the man of merit, the credit belongs to every possessor of favor, and thanks are owed to every doer of kindness."

The boy said: "As for your superiority over them, that has become clear; and as for the argument of truth, it stands established against every one of the common people who squanders it. But what is your argument against me, when I already acknowledge what you have said, uphold what you have described, deny what you have denied, and know the truth and its worth, and know falsehood and its people?"

The scholar said: "My argument against you is the same as my argument against them, for you have parted from them in outward description while agreeing with them in the inner meaning."

The boy said: "How can that be, when I acknowledge the truth to its rightful owner while they deny it?"

The scholar said: "If you have parted from them by acknowledging where they denied, you have agreed with them by withholding where they withheld. For what does acknowledgment profit without a turning back to act, and what harm does denial do when the reality is already present?"

The boy said: "Then I turn back to the people of truth by their right, if you make them known to me — or to an agent who can establish it on their behalf. Show me which of the two you are able to do."

The scholar said: "Do you not see that you have been driven to need the establishing of the rightful owner of the truth, or of his agent, just as the common people were driven to need the establishing of the truth itself? Deeds have set you apart from them, and ignorance has joined you to them."

The boy said: "You speak truly. Make it clear to me."

The scholar said: "Have you not acknowledged that my argument stands against you and against the common people?"

He said: "Yes."

The scholar said: "Then can anyone establish for himself a truth that belongs to another?"

He said: "No one is able to do that."

The scholar said: "Then the argument has been established against you and against the common people. Nothing remains but submission, and turning to the one whom you cannot do without and who can do without you."

The boy said: "As for my poverty, I acknowledge it. As for the One who is rich while I am in need, I seek Him — so have mercy on me."

The scholar said: "Have you ever seen a man whom poverty drove to a man of bounty attain any of that bounty except by earning it or by favor?"

The boy said: "Yes. None of it is attained except by earning or by favor."

The scholar said: "And do the people of favor gain any share of what belongs to the people of earning?"

He said: "No."

The scholar said: "Then seek this matter by work, and you will attain it by desert. Do not seek it by flattery, for then you would attain it by favor, and so be left deprived."

The boy said: "You are the key to goodness and its guide. Show me the more meaningful of the two paths and the nearer of them to guidance. The greatness of my need has bound my captivity fast to you."

The scholar said: "O my son, there is nothing beyond your reach, and no reproach against you. What you have hoped for from me shall reach you. But it has limits not to be overstepped, and conditions not to be forgotten."

The boy said: "Set whatever limit you love, and lay down whatever condition you see fit. I hope you will find me patient under your testing and grateful for your kindness."

The scholar said: "The first of the limits is to uphold the outward meaning of the Book and its laws, and to act upon what you know of it. For whoever acts for God by what he knows, God guides to what he does not know."

The boy said: "But the Books are many, and all of them are from God. For every Book there is a scholar among creation who cleaves to it, and they are all agreed upon guarding it and upon the religion within it."

The scholar said: "We have come back to our first words, and we are made to need the settling of what should have needed no settling."

The boy said: "How so?"

The scholar said: "From your saying that the Books are many, that all are from God, and that each has its own scholar. Do you then suppose that the words of God and His Books cancel one another — that the first of them gives the lie to the last, and the last to the first?"

The boy said: "I do not suppose that. But what is the proof of it?"

The scholar said: "Had creation been able to do all that is in the first Book, it would have led them to the second; and had they done what is in the second, it would have led them to the third, until the work reaches its end in the last of them, since the last is worthier than what came before it. For though all of them are from God, the later is nearer to the present age and clearer in its way, being an abrogation of what preceded it, with nothing coming after it to abrogate it in turn."

The boy said: "You speak truly, and you have shown me the knowledge of truth and the sign of truthfulness. I hold to the limits you have laid upon me. So what are your conditions for me?"

The scholar said: "My conditions upon you are five: do not fail me if I entrust you; do not conceal anything from me if I ask you; do not seek me out until I answer you; do not ask me for anything until I begin with you; and do not speak of my affair to your father."

The boy said: "I will do all that you have named, and it is easy for me beside the greatness of what I hope for from you. But how am I to manage my father? He is one of the heaviest trials laid upon me and the hardest I have to bear. Will you permit me to withdraw myself from him?"

The scholar said: "To withdraw from him would be a signpost pointing others toward you. Rather stay with him, be dutiful to him, satisfy him with some measure of outward agreement, keep your secret from him, and veil your affair. God will suffice us against his harm and the harm of others."

The narrator said: Then the scholar and the boy took leave of one another, and they stayed apart for a long stretch of their days. The boy remained perplexed in his affair. He knew nothing but the law at which he had been made to halt; he did not know where the scholar dwelt; and he could not seek him out, bound by the condition laid upon him, even as the absence stretched on. All the while the scholar was watching his patience and his endurance of what had been laid upon him. At last, on a quiet night when creation had grown still and the spies had grown heedless, when they had come together in their assembly, the boy felt that his need was drawing near and that the chance to be alone with his companion had come. He fell down before him in prostration, and when he raised his head, the scholar spoke.

"I saw you prostrate in thanksgiving," said the scholar. "Thanksgiving before the deed breeds suspicion, and thanksgiving cut short after the deeds makes the deeds themselves vain."

The boy said: "All your words are truthful, and all your works are mastery. My thanks are only for the favor that has already come from you, and by thanking I hoped for an increase from you."

The scholar said: "Thanksgiving deserves that it should bring increase to the thankful. Your right is now due, so turn to me with your heart, bring your understanding present, and listen to what is laid before you."

The boy said: "I have brought the instrument of understanding present and taken up the equipment of hearing. Say what seems good to you."

The scholar said: "The religion has a key that unlocks it and locks it, like that which distinguishes unlawful union from lawful marriage."

The boy said: "This key you have named is great in the sight of God, since it distinguishes the lawful from the unlawful, and truth from falsehood. So what is it?"

The scholar said: "It is the covenant of God, which affirms His rights, gathers up His obligations, and makes Paradise binding for His friends — the rope of God upon His earth and the safe haven among His servants. I will recite it to you, and I will bind you by it."

The boy said: "Yes, take from me whatever you love. I do not spurn your judgment, and I do not overstep your way."

The narrator said: Then the scholar set about reciting the covenant to the boy, delivering it to him slowly and binding him fast by it, while the boy could not master himself for awe, and his tears ran down from the intensity of the lesson, until he reached the end of the covenant. Then the boy praised God and glorified Him, and gave thanks for what had come to him, and he knew with certainty that, by their acceptance of his pledge, he had entered the Party of God (Hizb Allah) and the company of His friends. Then he fell silent from his praising, and the scholar began to expound and explain.

## 4. How the World Was Made

The scholar began with the first of the causes of the manifest order and the origin of its creation. God, he said, is the Creator of all things and the One who composes them; He created them and brought them into being. He is not the first of every first created thing, as though He had taken some earlier creation as a proof of what came after — rather He is the First of all firsts and the Inheritor of every thing. He began the creation of what He created out of light, and from that light three words branched. The first of them is the will. From the will branched the command, and from the command branched the utterance, whereby He says to whatever He wills "Be," and it is. So the first creation is a will, then a command, then an utterance.

These are three words, and from them is derived "كن," which is two letters, and "فيكون," which is five letters, making seven letters in all; and from these seven letters seven things branch. Out of the light He created air, the first of them: single, without consort, unique among things, mingling with them and encompassing them, yet encompassed by none of them. It is therefore the proof of the One of whom it is said "ليس كمثله شيء" — there is nothing like Him. Then, out of those three words, He created water, darkness, and light, the light of the heavens and the earth. From the water He created smoke, from the darkness He created the condensing of the heat, and from the light He created fire. These are three things branching from three things before them, and each became a consort to its origin, since it was created from it. The seventh, the air, holds sway over them all. This was the beginning of creation and the beginning of things.

When creation had been set upon seven principles, and the course of forms had been laid down by likeness and analogy, He created out of the smoke seven heavens and their signs, and out of the condensing of the heat He created seven earths and their sustenances. These are the seven branches drawn from those seven origins.

Then we turn to consider the signs of the heaven and the sustenances of the earth, asking what they are and what bears witness to them. In this we come back to the three words and their letters, from which the seven were created, namely "a will to command by an utterance." That is twelve letters. Its signs in the heaven are the twelve constellations, and its witnesses are seven kingdoms; its signs upon the earth are twelve islands, and its witnesses are seventeen seas. These are the signs of the heavens and the earth, which people pass by while they turn away from them.

Then the heavens and the earth were closed up in darkness by the black heat, which belongs to the dominion of the earth. Then He pierced them with His lights, and the light and the darkness, the two opposed realities, were mingled together. He drew them apart by night and day as two signs. He made the night and its sign a way of darkness and apportioned it, and He made the day and its sign a way of light and apportioned it. So the night grew dim with its darkness, and its sign is the moon and the stars, while the day shone with its light, and its sign is the seeing rays of the sun. Then out of the two of them the days were brought forth, passing in turn between night and day, and they are seven days. Their signs are that each day has twelve hours and each night twelve hours.

When the creation of things had been completed upon these principles, He made them pairs, male and female. I do not mean male and female in the bodily sense, but in the sense of the superior and the subordinate. Then the way of pairing went forth in creation, and out of their substances He created every living thing — humankind and beasts and birds and creeping things. Then this way extended into speech, and speech too became pairs: qualities and things qualified, names and meanings. So glory be to Him who created all the pairs and made them outward signs of His command, likenesses of His religion, and proofs of His power, pointing by means of things to their Originator and by means of the creation to its Maker.

Then He opened it with His light, began it with His command, created it by His knowledge, and sealed it, from among His names, with the letters of "بسم الله الرحمن الرحيم." When He had perfected the whole of it by His making and composed it in pairs by His measuring, He chose for Himself, out of the choicest of His will and the depths of His unseen, a great knowledge and a clear light, and He made the two of them a religion by which God is served and by which one draws near to Him. Then He honored it with the sacred laws and the books, and gave signs of it through parables and customs. Then He set up for it, from among the best of His creation, banners and proofs and rulers, and made them the houses of His prophethood, the treasuries of His knowledge and wisdom, the interpreters of His revelation, and His successors in His earth, guides upon the path of salvation in both the outward and the inward. Among them are the imams and the speakers; among them are the proofs and the chiefs; among them are the summoners to the good and the learned. In their stations they are like the stars of heaven, for by them works are raised up and in them likenesses reach their end. Their imam is like the great sun among the lights, veiled by its own coverings from sight, and it is his outward form and his likeness. The proof of their imam, and his gate, is like the shining moon, which is his outward form and his likeness. Their summoners are like the radiant stars, which are their outward forms and their likenesses.

All the pairs I have described to you are outward and inward, likeness and thing represented. The outward of the seven heavens are likenesses, and their inward are the seven speakers, the messengers endowed with firm resolve. The seven earths are outwardly likenesses, and their inward are the imams of guidance. For each speaker among them there are twelve chiefs, and their likeness from the seven heavens is the twelve constellations. Likewise, each imam among the imams of guidance has twelve proofs by whom he is upheld before his people, and their likeness in the earth is the twelve islands, which are the dwellings of the chiefs, on every island a proof. Every proof has summoners, many and few, for He increases in creation what He wills, so that the proof may be conveyed — the proof of God to His creation — that people may have no argument against God after the messengers.

Through these causes, the causes of God that I have described to you, the proof of God reaches His creation, and His justice appears among them outwardly and inwardly. The inward is the religion of God, to which the friends of God (رض) have bound themselves, and the outward is the sacred laws of the religion and their likenesses. So the religion became to the laws a soul and a spirit, and the laws became to the religion a body and a guide. The body cannot stand except by the soul, for the soul is its life, and the soul cannot stand except by the body, for the body is its instrument. In the same way the outward things — the sacred laws and the rest — cannot stand except by the inward religion, because it is their light and their meaning and the spirit of life within them; and the inward cannot stand except by the outward, because the outward is its body and its proof. The outward is the knowledge of this world, and it is seen only through it; the inward is the knowledge of the hereafter, and it is seen only through it. There is not a single letter of the letters of the inward, nor a single friend of the friends, but that its witness in the outward is many, on account of the abundance of the likenesses and the breadth of the sacred laws.

Speech upon this grows long and multiplies, and the more its answer increases from the words of wisdom, the more its rightness is hidden, and its end causes its beginning to be forgotten, on account of its preciousness and the purity of its essence. For the light of wisdom overshadows one part of itself with another, just as the light of the sun overshadows the light of the moon and the stars and makes it faint. In the same way the words of wisdom adorn the tongue of the wise man.

## 5. The World, the Hereafter, and the Speech of Parables

The boy said: This world, in which so vast a creation was placed, holds a great many people, the first of them and the last, and they disagree about it. Some have given themselves over to the world and chased after it, seeing no worth in anything but piling it higher. Others reproach it and live as ascetics within it, yet without doing away with it altogether — even though what it forbids is much and only a little of it is truly forbidden. I have heard from you no word or account concerning it. What do you say of it?

The scholar said: As for this world, no one will ever truly reproach it, neither the scholar nor the ignorant man. The ignorant man knows its worth by the very fact that he is ignorant of what lies beyond it, and the scholar knows its worth through the worth of its meaning. None ever reproaches this world but an ignorant man. And how could it be right for the creation to reproach the work of the Creator? Say rather that the world stands, for anyone who cares to see, upon seven things that branch from seven origins: heaven, earth, darkness, light, vessels, prosperity, and misery. These are seven qualities, and from each of them seven further qualities branch out.

The heavens are seven. The earths are seven.

The light has seven days that turn upon seven nights. Saturday is for slumber and rest. Sunday is for the limits and the sunna. Monday is for the mathani, in the great meaning, al-Kabir. Tuesday has three houses: the apparent, the inward, and the inward of the inward. Wednesday is for the four sacred ones. Thursday is the fifth of the causes of God. And Friday is the greatest law of God (namus).

The darkness is the seven nights, a rest for the seven days, so that every day has its night.

The vessels are seven: camels, horses, mules, donkeys, elephants, oxen, and ships. By these seven vessels the regions of the earth are crossed. Camels are for the sands, horses for the plains, mules for the mountains, donkeys for nearby places, elephants for marshes and wasteland, oxen for salt flats and thickets, and ships for the seas. So they are seven mounts for seven domains.

The blessings of this world rest upon seven: air, water, light, darkness, food, clothing, and marriage. Air is the space between sky and earth, in which the winds arise and the breaths pass to and fro, and it is the life of the animal. Water is the life of every living thing. Light is the very stuff of sight. Darkness makes the plants grow. Food sustains the body. Clothing is a shield that veils what is shameful. Marriage is the seed of reproduction and the cause of offspring. Some of these are the keys of the good, and some are the mines of blessings. Which of them is blameworthy, when every one of them is among the gifts of God and His blessings, for which His servants are bound to give thanks?

Their opposites in misery are likewise seven: heat, cold, hunger, thirst, fear, sickness, and death. These are seven that must be borne with patience, once their meaning is known.

The boy said: These are the attributes of this world, and I do not doubt that any part of it is other than what the Creator has made and composed. So what do the scholars mean when they censure the world, and what do the ignorant mean when they praise it? I see that the ignorant have arrived at the meaning, even in their ignorance. And if that is so, the rightness of their case would force the scholars into error, since the scholars contradict the people of ignorance at every turn.

The scholar said: The scholars did not err in censuring this world, but no creature grasps what they mean by it. And the ignorant did not perceive the meaning of what they praised in the world, for they were ignorant of its meaning. Whoever is ignorant of a thing is hostile to it.

The boy said: Show me the truth of what the scholars say, so that I may rightly believe them. And mark out the speech of the people of ignorance by what lays bare its falsehood, since the outward appearance points the other way. My chest has grown weary of this ignorance, and I hope for no relief except through knowledge of it.

The scholar said: This world, with all that I have described to you of it, is an outward that stands for an inward. It has no subsistence except through that inward, for the inward is to it as the soul is to the body. Whoever knows the soul, the body is made wholesome for him. Whoever does not know the soul and acknowledges only the body dwells upon a corpse, and the corpse is forbidden. From here the scholars forbade this world, because it is dead. They forbade the dead thing that God has forbidden, until one comes to it from where God wills. And the people of ignorance are shown to lie in their praise of this world, because they did not know its inner meaning, nor what God intended by it. They supposed that God created the world for some meaning other than its own, but God did not create things in vain. Had the world and all within it been created for the world alone, He would have set in it a creation that fades away, with no consequence and no meaning. Yet every act without consequence is play, and every speech without meaning is falsehood, and God is far too exalted for that.

The boy said: The truthfulness of the scholars is established, their virtue and excellence are proven, and the people of ignorance stand exposed in their falsehood, by the argument that has now appeared. There remains for us the knowledge of the inward of the world and what is in it. And I see that the distress I complained of to you has been lifted.

The scholar said: What is the reward of the one who brought you out of the distress of poverty into the breadth of ease?

He said: Thanks and praise.

The scholar said: And what is the reward of the one who brought you out of the narrowness of ignorance into the breadth of knowledge?

He said: Obedience and works.

The scholar said: This world and all that resembles it are an outward that stands for the hereafter and what is in it, for the hereafter is its soul and its life. Whoever labors in this world for the world, with no knowledge of the hereafter, his labor is misguidance, for it has neither meaning nor consequence. But whoever labors in this world for the hereafter, believing in it, his labor has meaning and consequence, and his labor is thanked.

The boy said: You have spoken truly. So make plain to me the features of this world and what is in it, and set the parables against the things they signify from within.

The scholar said: God, blessed and exalted, has created nothing among the things of this world — no beast upon the earth, no bird that flies with its wings, nothing moist and nothing dry among the fixed inanimate things such as mountains, stones, trees, and the minerals of gold, silver, and gems, nor anything at all, great or small — except that it is a parable struck. Do you not see that the atom, for all its smallness and the subtlety of its meaning, has limits: before it and behind it, to its right and to its left, above it and below it? Those are six limits, and the seventh is itself. So too is everything God has created, from the ant and the mosquito up to what is above them of heaven and earth and all that lies between them, great and small: each has six limits, and the thing itself is the seventh. This is a proof pointing to the six origins from which things were created, while the air that encompasses them is the seventh, master over them all. If your breast is wide enough for these questions and for looking into the parables, matching each thing with its counterpart among the inward pairs, then ask about whatever appears to you.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the heaven, in its height above all things and the breadth of its regions, so that it has gathered all things within it?

The scholar said: Its likeness is the speaking Imam (al-Imam al-Natiq), who is exalted above all creation by his virtue and the height of his degrees, and who has gathered within himself all the rulings — the rulings of the Shari'a — by the breadth of his knowledge.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the constellations and the rest of the stars?

The scholar said: They are the likeness of the twelve chiefs (nuqaba), the chiefs of the speaking Imam. And its stars are his summoners (du'at), who call to the good by the permission of their Lord.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the earth and its breadth, which was made a resting place and a bed for this creation?

The scholar said: It is the likeness of the gate (bab) of the speaking Imam during his lifetime, and of his successor (wasi) after his death. He is a recourse for the people and a place of settling for the one who seeks guidance, and he is a knower of the parables and of the inner interpretation (ta'wil).

The boy said: What is the likeness of the twelve islands in the earth?

The scholar said: They are the likenesses of the twelve arguments (hujaj), the arguments of the successors.

The boy said: What is the likeness of its rivers and its springs?

The scholar said: The likeness of those who call to the good (du'at).

The boy said: What is the likeness of the seven kingdoms of the heavens and their orbits?

The scholar said: Those are causes between God and the speakers (nutaqa), and they belong to Him. They are the seven and the two orbits, and those are the greatest causes of God.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the seven seas?

The scholar said: They are causes between God and the imams of the speaker, beneath those first causes.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the salt water and the fresh water, and their difference upon the earth?

The scholar said: The salt water is the likeness of the knowledge of the outward, in which there is no summons (da'wa) and no summoners, just as salt water yields neither crop nor anything else. The fresh water is the likeness of the knowledge of the inward, whether as springs within the earth or as water flowing over it. So too is knowledge: the outward, by which the summoners run their course, and the inward, which is kept hidden.

The boy said: How did water come to be a likeness of knowledge?

The scholar said: Because water is the life of every living thing, and likewise knowledge is the life of every knower.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the air that surrounds all things?

The scholar said: The air is too great to be set as a likeness for any thing it might signify.

The boy said: Is it greater still, or are the heaven and the earth as great as it, so that they too may be its likenesses?

The scholar said: Glory be to God — rather it is greater than both. The breadth of the earth within the atmosphere is like a signet ring in an open desert. The breadth of the atmosphere within the sky is like a signet ring in an open desert. And the breadth of the sky and its regions within the breadth of the air is like an egg in an open desert. What thing to be signified could take in all of this, so as to be a proof and a likeness of it?

The boy said: Then what is its meaning?

The scholar said: It is a proof of the One who has nothing like Him, glory be to Him and exalted above what they associate with Him.

The boy said: But did not the Creator say, "فَلَا تَضْرِبُوا لِلَّهِ الْأَمْثَالَ" (So do not strike parables for God)?

The scholar said: And did He not also say, "وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى" (And to God belongs the highest similitude)?

The boy said: Glory be to Him who has set enigmas in His Book so that one may come to it by its own door — and you are one of its gates. Make plain to us how this is.

The scholar said: Everything has a meaning, and the meaning of the parables is this: the outward is evidence of the inward. He, blessed and exalted, has forbidden that He be measured against anything of His creation, such that one should say, "This is the likeness of God," as one says, "This is the likeness of the Imam," or "This is the likeness of the hujja," or "This is the likeness of the da'i." Their likenesses became signs pointing to them and to their ranks. So it is not fitting, given His greatness, that the Creator be measured against anything of His creation by any word or deed, and it is not said, "This is the likeness of God." Rather, the parables are taken according to their meanings, for their meanings carry signifiers: the small among them points to the great, the great points to the highest meanings, and the highest points to the Most High, the Highest. That is why He said, "وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى" (And to God belongs the highest similitude) — meaning the highest proof, for there is no proof of anything higher than the proof of God. And that is the air: highest above the heaven and the earth, encompassing all things while nothing among them encompasses it. It is the highest similitude, and it is the proof of the One who has nothing like Him. He did not say that there is nothing like Him — for indeed there is nothing like Him — but rather He meant that no proof of Him is like other proofs. To Him belongs the highest similitude, and there is no proof like His proof among the proofs of any other, among those of His creation whom we have described. So blessed be God, Lord of the mighty Throne.

The boy said: The meaning has grown vast and the reach has widened. Was there, before the air, some thing between it and God, given the greatness of what you have described in it?

The scholar said: Yes.

The boy said: What is it?

The scholar said: The time.

The boy said: And what is the time?

The scholar said: It is the time of the will by which He created what He willed and commanded what He wished.

The boy said: You have plunged me into the depths of the seas of the highest assembly. Return me to the sciences of this world and their likenesses, that I may seek help through them in my affair and be made strong by them to bear what I am meant to bear. For I have feared for myself and grieved for my mind, lest I be led astray into what I cannot fathom.

The scholar said: You rose high by your question, and so the answer rose with you to the highest of the outward meanings, and there your judgment was lost and your mind bewildered. How then would it be if I disclosed to you its inward? You and I would be as Moses and al-Khidr, the Knowing One.

The boy said: Does this inward have an inward more inward still?

The scholar said: By my life, the inward has an inward that is loftier in station, wider in power, and more complete in guidance, and it is the utmost of proofs to the road of salvation.

The boy said: Then I see here three layers of knowledge: the outward, the inward, and the inward of the inward. Is there a proof of this?

The scholar said: And where would the proof be, if not for this and for that to which it points?

The boy said: Then show me a likeness of these three layers.

The scholar said: I will show you from the Book of God, exalted is He, and from the argument of the intellect, and from what the whole body of the parables points to.

The boy said: I did not think there was any teaching beyond what I had already reached. Truly the affair of God is far greater than what the imaginings of the pretenders can rise to.

The scholar said: As for the Book of God, exalted is He, His word: "يَا بَنِي آدَمَ قَدْ أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْكُمْ لِبَاسًا يُوَارِي سَوْآتِكُمْ وَرِيشًا وَلِبَاسُ التَّقْوَى ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ" (O children of Adam, We have sent down upon you a garment that covers your shameful parts, and adornment, and the garment of godliness — that is better). This is three: the outward, the inward, and the inward of the inward.

Al-Ghulam said: You have spoken truly.

The scholar said: As for the argument of the intellect: a thing is known only by a name, an attribute, or a meaning. The meaning is what the thing is in itself, the name is what it is called, and the attribute is what points to it. Likewise the religion of God has an outward name and an inward proof that points to it, and it stands only by the two together. Nothing is pointed to except by a noun or an attribute, as in the saying of one who says, "The moon has risen." The meaning is the disk, "moon" is its name, and "has risen" is its attribute. Were he to say "has risen" and then fall silent, it would carry no meaning. And were he to say "moon" and then fall silent, it would carry no meaning — until, joining the name and the attribute, he says, "The moon has risen," and then the meaning is made plain and the speech is sound. Just so, if you say "outward," it calls for an answer, that one ask, "Outward of what?" And if you say "inward," it calls for an answer, that one ask, "Inward of what?" But when you say, "the outward of the religion and its inward," the speech is understood, and it becomes a religion having an outward and an inward, just like the thing that has a name and an attribute, as He said: "وَذَرُوا ظَاهِرَ الْإِثْمِ وَبَاطِنَهُ" (And forsake the outward of sin and its inward). So sin became a thing having an outward and an inward. Such too is the greater religion of God, whose laws are called its outward and whose inward is its inward, and it subsists in itself. To the knowledge of its meaning there are many parables that point, such as the hidden egg, which has an outward that shields it, an inward that is the white within, and an inward of its inward — the yellow yolk, which is its essence and its meaning.

The boy said: Are not the world and the hereafter a pair, the soul and the body a pair, and the knowledge of the outward and the inward a pair — and are not the pairs of this kind, as you have described, many?

The scholar said: You have spoken truly. But they are pairs — so of what are they the likeness?

The boy said: They are pairs that point to the religion of God.

The scholar said: You have spoken truly. The pair is an outward name and an inward attribute, pointing to the knowledge of God and to the religion of God, and that is the inward of the inward. Likewise the creation was created in three stations. The first is the creation of the angels, the second the creation of human beings, and the third the creation of the beasts. The outward of knowledge is the limit of the beasts, and whoever knows only that, with no inward, is at the level of the beasts. The knowledge of the inward is the knowledge of the children of Adam and their limit, and whoever knows it is a believer and is at the level of human beings. And the knowledge of the inward of the inward is the knowledge of the angels. Whoever knows it is spiritual in knowledge and bodily in frame — a prophet sent, whom God appoints as His deputy in His earth and by whom He establishes the argument over His creation. He is the veil of the angels, the cipher of revelation and its interpreter to the children of Adam. With him are the keys of the gardens, which none enters except the one who obeys him, and with him is the passage from the Fire, which none enters except the one who disobeys him. And the people are but two men: a divine scholar, and one who learns.

## 6. Three Layers of Knowledge

Of these two men, the scholar went on, the first has been taught the very purpose of knowledge, and the spirit of certainty has quickened his soul, so that he is a scholar by what he knows and a master by what he does. The second is a learner who seeks knowledge for the sake of his own deliverance. The rest of the people are savages and a rabble who know nothing; they follow every false guide, deceived by his error and seduced by his ignorance. They imagine they are doing well, yet they have been cut off from the true examples that came before them, and they will not escape what Allah has promised, for the disbelievers shall have the like of it.

The boy said: So this is what virtue is, and this is what knowledge and its unfolding are. Blessed are the guardians of God, and blessed are those who follow in benevolence. Will I reach this great station, which stands above every station beneath it?

The scholar said: O my son, this is a station that God has honored above all other stations and degrees, so that you can reach it by virtuous work alone. If your soul does its work, and you seek the station with honest certainty and a heart both pure and awake, I have hope that you will attain from it what the guardians of God, the chosen and the righteous, have attained.

The boy said: And who may hope to reach the like of this station, to which God has raised His guardians?

The scholar said: Their God is your God. Between God and anyone else there is no kinship; nothing brings the creation near to Him but piety and righteous deeds. The knowledge by which Allah has raised His guardians — you stand at its beginning, and if you act upon it you will reach its end. It is a debt binding upon God to bring you to the ranks the righteous among them attained, and to deprive you of nothing He gave them.

The boy said: Can you help me to that, then, by some inner meaning, as your bounty first came to me?

The scholar said: This is a station in which nothing avails but the work of its own people.

The boy said: How can that be, when you were able to help me before?

The scholar said: The sower is able to make the earth fertile, to sow it and to water it, but he cannot bring forth the plant and its sheath. A man is able to bring forth his seed whenever he wills, but he cannot fashion from it what he wills. So it grieves me, my son, that you should seek my help in a thing and I be unable to help you to it. May Allah expand your breast and light your heart with guidance. Hold fast to the fear of God, and do what is best, for Allah does not waste the reward of the one who does his work well. You are upon the path of salvation, the road of guidance, the way of the people of piety. So walk your road, whose beginning you already stand upon, and cling to the rope you have been called to, until you are led by a rope from God to the rope of God, for that is the goal of every seeker.

The boy said: Is not the rope of Allah the Imam to whom you have called me?

The scholar said: He is that in what is outwardly apparent. He is your rope and the most trustworthy handhold, your hujja, and the door of your Imam.

The boy said: Then what is the rope of Allah, and what is the rope that comes from God?

The scholar said: That is the utmost of your guidance and the furthest reach of all the worlds.

The boy said: There is no power and no strength save in Allah, لا حول ولا قوة إلا بالله. He who has no guide has drowned in the depths of ignorance, just as he who has no ship has drowned in the seas of this world.

The scholar said: And what are the hawl and the quwwa which you have said cannot be except by Allah?

The boy said: We mean by it that I have no device to draw a benefit to myself, and no power to ward off from myself a harm I loathe, except by Allah.

The scholar said: Then why do you say, there is no hawl except in Allah, rather than, there is no hila — no stratagem — except in Allah?

The boy said: It is narrated to us in our sciences that the hawl is the year, from the saying of Allah, حولين كاملين, two complete years, meaning two years. And it is narrated to us, as the common people also relate, that the quwwa is mules, from the saying of Allah, وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْكُنُوزِ مَا إِنَّ مَفَاتِحَهُ لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ, and We gave him of treasures whose very keys would weigh down a band of men endowed with strength. They said the band was forty men and the strength was mules, and that the keys of his treasures were the load of forty mules.

The scholar said: So it is as though, by saying there is no power and no strength save in Allah, you meant there is no year and no mule save by Allah. Is this, in your view, the interpretation of your words and their meaning?

The boy said: I have no saying other than this, and it is a feeble one that resembles nothing of the speech of wisdom. Indeed I suspect that the meaning within it is other than this — something stronger in the utterance and more firmly fixed in the mind. Is there for these two words an inner interpretation other than this?

The scholar said: The creation has never uttered a word of truth without its having a root in the inner, nor a word of falsehood without its having once had a meaning; for when speech is turned away from its meaning it becomes an absurdity, and the absurdity, displaced from its place, is altered, and truth, once altered, becomes falsehood. Rather, it is a speech by which what comes after is grasped through analogy and forms, just as what came before was grasped through likeness and parable.

The boy said: You have spoken truly. So what is the meaning of these two words and their like?

The scholar said: As for the hawl, he is the speaking Imam of Allah and His truthful Caliph, the master of the twelve captains, just as the hawl, which is the year, is completed only by twelve months. The Imam was named hawl because he turns the speech of the Creator, in the subtlety of its essence, into the speech of the children of Adam, until it is understood and becomes apparent speech. In this way the words of wisdom take on a body and a frame, while the speech of the Creator carries within them the spirit of life and the light of salvation. The words of wisdom are ennobled above all other speech by the nobility of the Creator's speech that is their inner. So the Imam was named the hawl for this turning of speech, and he turns it only by the leave of Allah. And the quwwa is his hujja and his door, whom God has strengthened to bear the heavy weight of the utterance. He receives it from the Imam whole and parcels it out to the summoners in detail, each according to his station; so the hujja was named quwwa, and it is strengthened only by God. It is for this that the one who speaks says, there is no hawl and no quwwa save in Allah, meaning there is no Imam and no hujja save by Allah, and from Allah come their light and their support. The band, the ones endowed with strength, are the twelve hujjas. So too Joseph (ع) said to his father, يَا أَبَتِ إِنِّي رَأَيْتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ رَأَيْتُهُمْ لِي سَاجِدِينَ, O my father, I saw eleven planets and the sun and the moon; I saw them prostrating to me. By the sun he meant the Imam, by the moon his hujja and his door, and by the planets the completing of the twelve captains. And likewise the brothers of Joseph said, لَيُوسُفُ وَأَخُوهُ أَحَبُّ إِلَى أَبِينَا مِنَّا وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ, truly Joseph and his brother are dearer to our father than we are, though we are a band — meaning the twelve captains, and asking that a man from among them be chosen over the rest. In this, poverty of judgment concerning Joseph (ع) entered upon them, and the risings of envy toward him appeared in them when God favored him. Yet the command of God was fulfilled in Joseph, and the end of their affair was remorse and repentance before him.

The boy said: Praise be to God, who cleansed them of defilement and returned them to the resort of repentance and the ways of turning back. So what is the meaning of the king's saying, إِنِّي أَرَى سَبْعَ بَقَرَاتٍ سِمَانٍ يَأْكُلُهُنَّ سَبْعٌ عِجَافٌ وَسَبْعَ سُنْبُلَاتٍ خُضْرٍ وَأُخَرَ يَابِسَاتٍ, I see seven fat cows being devoured by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others withered? What are they, and what is the vision seen in sleep?

The scholar said: As for the vision, it is like the outward work a man performs; it is as the mirage which, when he reaches it, he finds to be nothing. So too is the work of the outward without the inner: nothing of it is accepted, however much it may be. And so is the vision in sleep: nothing is realized from what is seen, however often it is seen. The king said, in the common people's telling, I see seven things turning upon seven. As for the seven fat cows, they are the causes of God that stand between Him and His creation. As for the seven lean ones, they are the seven speakers, driven by need to what comes from God at the hands of His seven causes. The seven green ears are causes between God and the legatees. And the seven withered ones are the legatees, driven to what comes from God at the hands of these seven causes. Blessed, then, be Allah, Lord of the worlds.

The boy said: And what is the likeness of this among us?

The scholar said: That lies in the foundation of Islam and its offspring, like a grain that put forth seven ears — meaning that from his loins came seven imams.

The boy said: How, then, does God forbid this world, saying, فَلَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا, so let not the life of this world deceive you?

The scholar said: God has spoken truly. He did not say, let not this world deceive you; rather He said, let not the life of this world deceive you. Life carries four meanings: a life apparent in this world, whose end is annihilation, and the life of the Hereafter, whose end is permanence. He said: do not work for the perishing life, but work for the abiding life. This is His saying, يَا لَيْتَنِي قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِي, would that I had sent something ahead for my life — meaning, would that in the perishing life one had sent something ahead for the abiding life. And the two lives, in another sense, are a life through the outward knowledge and a life through the inward knowledge. The outward knowledge is the life of this world, and it is the lower knowledge; the inward knowledge is the life of the Hereafter. For this reason He said, لَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا, let not the life of this world deceive you — meaning, let not the outward of knowledge, and the acting upon it alone, deceive you. For He accepts from you nothing but the inner established together with the outward, and He does not accept the outward without the inner.

The boy said: Consider one who knows the inward knowledge but does not know the outward and does not act upon it. What is his standing among the people of religion?

The scholar said: A wretched standing. His inner will not rise up nor be made sound if he has abandoned a thing he was commanded to do and made a shield of it for his inner, like the outward of fruits — for when the rind is peeled from them before their harvest they are spoiled and cannot be set right afterward; and like the body, for when its limbs are severed the soul is not sustained in it a single hour. Such is the outward of religion: when its obligations are neglected, its inner cannot stand for its owner. Moreover, his leaving of the outward, without leave from the One who imposed it upon him, does not escape one of two things. If he left it out of incapacity, then he is the more incapable in the inner; and if he left it deliberately, out of ability, then he is disobedient to the One who commanded him. The disobedient is a transgressor, and the transgressor does not dwell beside the guardians of God; rather he is their enemy and they are his, because he has severed what God commanded to be joined.

The boy said: And one who knows the outward knowledge and acts upon it but does not know the inner — what is his standing among the people of religion?

The scholar said: An evil standing, for he is like a body that has been formed but into which no soul has been breathed; he is numbered among the dead, and the dead are the abodes of the disbelievers. As one who denies the signs of God, he is an enemy to religion and to its people.

The boy said: So I see that the outward is not made sound except by the inner, and the inner does not stand except by the outward; that this world is lawful only to the one who has known the Hereafter, which is its true life and its inner; and likewise that religion is not made whole for its people until they establish both its outward and its inner.

The scholar said: Yes, this is the meaning, and upon this the work is to be done. For the fulfilling of all that God has commanded is a claim upon His good pleasure, and the abandoning of any of what God has commanded is an exposing of oneself to His wrath.

The boy said: Is not the one who knows the outward together with the inner and establishes them both a believer?

The scholar said: Yes, he is a believer in truth.

The boy said: And is it not so that the one who knows and then works for the Hereafter finds this world made wholesome for him, its good things lawful, and the handling of its gains permitted?

The scholar said: Yes. This world is made wholesome for none but them; they are the most deserving of it and the most entitled to it.

The boy said: Then why do I see the people of this description — who are the scholars and the folk of this affair — having cast off the world and all that is in it, until the ignorant among the people suppose it is forbidden to them? They turn away from this world; they look upon it only with a sidelong glance, and their souls are heavy toward it. And I see their colors gone sallow without illness, their bodies weak, their skins clinging to their bones, as though they do not eat food — even while they eat.

The narrator said: When the scholar heard the mention of his brethren and their description, he was choked with weeping and his eyes overflowed with tears, so that for a time he could not speak from the intensity of it. And the boy wept at his weeping, until the scholar feared that some of their enemies might prevail against them. So he held himself firm and his heart grew settled within him, and then the scholar turned toward the boy.

The scholar said: O my son, the people you have described in this way have looked upon themselves by the light of the true life and busied themselves in their affair with the certainty of salvation, and they have not dealt harshly with themselves. O my son, this world is seen by wealth and children, and the Hereafter is seen by knowledge and certainty. O my son, the faith of these people in the unseen is sound; they held the promise and the warning true, so they yearned toward what they were promised and sought it, and the fear of the threat filled them with dread, so they strove; they feared the stupor of death, so they hastened to good deeds. Their bodies grew weak from the persistence of fasting, and their colors turned sallow from the length of their vigils. They do not sleep when the sleepers sleep, out of dread, nor do they eat their sustenance except grudgingly. The longing of contemplation has turned them from the pleasure of ease, and the sweetness of remembrance has held them back from the respite of sleep. Their souls have been drawn away from the appetites, so this world was not made pleasant for them; they remember death often, so life was not made sweet to them; and they have beheld the bounties of the Hereafter with certainty, so they see this world only as a passing fragment. Thus they cast off the world in turning away from it, without holding it forbidden.

The boy said: This is the sound account, and it was in this sense that the scholars disdained this world, while the people are heedless of them, supposing that they have forbidden to themselves what God has made lawful for them. Now describe to me the delusion of the people of the world in their world, and their heedlessness of the Hereafter.

The scholar said: If those enamored of this world would only reflect upon their own creation and the turning of their states — from weakness to strength and from strength to weakness, from ease to ruin, and from life to death — and if they would then look upon those who passed before them of the vanished generations, among whom were the good and the wicked, and how all went down into one plain of earth, and how they saw no reward for the good in this world nor any punishment for the wicked in this world, they would know that they were created for other than this world. But earning weighed heavy upon them, and they would not carry upon themselves the burden of the seeking. So they seized the fleeting thing of this lower life and were content with the ease of this world in place of the Hereafter. This is their habit and this is their meaning. They have no argument before God and no honor before His guardians, and there has already come to them of the tidings that wherein is deterrence.

## 7. The Five Shares and the Long Road to the Shaykh

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has guided us to His religion, for we could never have found guidance except by His favor. Tell me now about the beginning of this creation and how it is put together. Is it a single creation, or many different ones? For I see so much difference among people in their opinions and their passions."

The scholar said: "As for the creation of bodies, it is various, as you can see in the diversity of tongues and colors. That very variety is a proof of God's power, made plain through His signs, so that His creatures may know one another: the child knows his father and the father his son; the man knows his wife and no other, and the woman knows her husband and no other. So God created them in justice. But as for the creation of the instrument—by which I mean the senses, both what is visible of them and what is hidden—that is a single creation, and what lies hidden within will tell you the very same thing as what is shown without. Have you ever seen a scholar with three eyes, so that you might point to some increase in him, or an ignorant man with a single eye, so that you might point to some deficiency in him—until someone claimed that the scholar sees by his surplus and the ignorant man fails to see for his lack? Have you seen any increase in the outward instrument of one man over another? You see their creation as one and the same in the visible instrument, and the meaning of its composition is identical in them all. From this we have come to know that the inner senses follow the very same pattern. It is justice. God would not have been just in what is visible and unjust in what is hidden. He did not make two hearts for the scholar within one cavity, nor did He deprive the ignorant man of anything. The instrument is one, and its composition belongs to the One who gathered it together. By this very instrument—which He gave them, and which they failed to use—God has made His case against them. The Almighty said:

لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ أَعْيُنٌ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ آذَانٌ لَا يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا

They have hearts wherewith they do not understand, and they have eyes wherewith they do not see, and they have ears wherewith they do not hear.

Do you suppose, then, that God, in His generosity and bounty, has charged them with what they have no power to bear and carry? Never. He is fairer than that."

The boy said: "I see it no other way, and I say nothing else. But I ask you about the difference of passions. Where did it come to them from, when the instrument is whole and the origin of the composition is one and the same?"

The scholar said: "As for the origin, it is one. They all descend from Adam, and Adam is from dust, whom his Lord created and into whom He breathed of His Spirit. But they strayed from the Sunna in their marrying; they bred along a path that was not the religion, and they grew up under no guidance. And I do not doubt that among them are children of unlawful union who lay claim to fathers not their own. When the roots were corrupted, the branches turned foul and strange, and the road back to the truth grew hard for them—together with the arrogance that entered them against obedience, the sloth that held them back from worship, and the envy they carried toward the friends of God. So they did not keep themselves within the bounds of the lawful, apart from the forbidden."

The boy said: "If the origin is one and the instrument is one, where does the difference of passions arise from, when their aim in seeking is also one—since every man seeks what is good for himself?"

The scholar said: "By my life, their aim in seeking is indeed one, but they differ in striking the mark, like archers aiming at a target. They agree in shooting at it with resolve and with the instrument, yet for all that they differ in hitting it. So too the hearts within the breasts are gathered upon the minds, and the minds are gathered upon safeguarding the bodies and securing their dignity. When the call of truth appeared, they parted ways. Some judged that answering the Messengers and humbling themselves before them was part of their own dignity and the way to guard themselves against folly. Others judged that denying the Messengers and raising themselves above obedience to them was part of their own dignity and the way to guard themselves against the narrowness of worship. In both, the origin was one: to seek ease for the body and dignity for the soul. So one company chose what the Messengers had promised them of the reward of God—though in this world it was precious and scant—choosing it for its permanence; and the others chose from the bliss of this world—though it perishes—choosing it because it is present, because it is easily sought, and because its reward is swift. The very hearts by which the first sought the bliss of the Hereafter were the like of those by which the others sought the bliss of this world. Each man looks to himself, and each supposes himself an adviser to his own soul. So they came together in the seeking and parted in the striking. That is from the justice of God, His generosity, and the breadth of His giving. To every seeker is given what he sought: whoever desires the reward of this world is given from it, and whoever desires the reward of the Hereafter is given from it."

The boy said: "Yet the people of this world are ranked in classes within it. Some are rich enough to have no need to labor, and these are kings by the abundance of their wealth. Others are poor and in need of money, and these serve the first in order to receive from them. Then the bliss of this world gathers them all on one side. Are they all alike in an evil end, or is there for some of them a portion different from the rest?"

The scholar said: "It is from the justice of God that He joins them one to another and does not part them from their companions and those they loved. They are equal in His punishment, just as they were joined together in their disobedience to Him."

The boy said: "And what of this religion, which God has honored and perfected, which He has accepted as a religion for Himself and laid upon His servants? Will He accept it from some of them and not the rest, so that they stand in ranks like the people of this world, or will He accept it only from one who brings it whole?"

The scholar said: "If the people of this world have their classes within it, then the people of religion have their degrees within it. In justice, the Creator is to be worshipped to the full measure of what He wills, according to the greatness of His sovereignty and the greatness of their need of Him. Yet His mercy widened toward them. He knew that among them are the weak, and so He accepted from them whatever came easily of sincere striving in work and of exertion in obedience, whether little or much. Then He charged each soul with knowledge according to its strength and its capacity to act, and He gave every possessor of merit his due, so that they stand at degrees before their Lord. Then, by His justice, He appointed an end for their obedience: that He should join them to those among His servants who hold the religion whole and whose works are excellent. The Almighty said:

وَمَنْ يُطِعِ اللهَ وَالرَّسُولَ فَأُولَئِكَ مَعَ الَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ اللهُ عَلَيْهِمْ مِنَ النَّبِيِّينَ وَالصِّدِّيقِينَ وَالشُّهَدَاءِ وَالصَّالِحِينَ وَحَسُنَ أُولَئِكَ رَفِيقًا ذَلِكَ الْفَضْلُ مِنَ اللهِ

And whoever obeys God and the Messenger, those are with the ones upon whom God has bestowed favor, of the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous. What excellent companions they are! That is the bounty from God.

This is by their exertion in obedience, not because their deeds equal the deeds of the righteous. It is the righteous who reach this rank, yet these attain it only as a mercy from God, even though they themselves are weak and their works are slight."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has done this for them. Is it then to be feared that anyone should be punished for something other than disobedience, even when his work is little?"

The scholar said: "God is more just than that."

The boy said: "Is there hope, then, that anyone should enter the Garden without works—such as the insane and the little children?"

The scholar said: "That falls under the grace of God, His generosity, and the breadth of His mercy."

The boy said: "What, then, is the limit of exertion in obedience and the measure of sincerity in work, such that God is bound to accept a man's excuse for all that lies beyond it?"

The scholar said: "The full reach of his knowledge, and the utmost of his strength."

The boy said: "Then describe to me the duty of knowledge and the duty of action, and the goal of each, so that I may test myself. If I am able to reach the goal in seeking the pleasure of my Lord, I shall press on; and if not, I shall halt at the limit of my strength, and God is the first to excuse me for what I cannot do. I am certain of the justice of God and safe from any wrong on His part, but I fear the evil within my own soul."

The scholar said: "Do not fear where you have made yourself safe, and do not feel safe where you ought still to fear. Strive for the sake of God with your person and your wealth, for God does not accept what He hates, nor does He hate what pleases Him. He has hated disbelief for His creation and has been pleased with thankfulness from His servants and with justice in His judgment; He accepts nothing else. Truthfulness and faithfulness are the two gardens of His pleasure, and none is saved with Him except the people of truthfulness and faithfulness."

The boy said: "Then make plain to me the goal of my work within myself, and what God has made binding upon me in it."

The scholar said: "Obedience to God ennobles its people, even when their origins are humble, and good work is the crown of those who do it. As for what is binding upon you in your own person, it is to perform every obligation and to shun every forbidden thing, for that is the very substance of religion and the purity of the body. The sum of what makes God pleased with you and accepting of you is this: that you bring to others what you would love to have brought to yourself, and withhold from them what you would love to have withheld from yourself. In that is justice, and God is pleased with justice."

The boy said: "Yes, this is the binding truth, and whoever does not act by it has no share with God. Is there, then, any latitude in what you have mentioned when necessity comes?"

The scholar said: "God has made this broad for His servants, out of His compassion and mercy. He has said:

فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ

Whoever is compelled, without willful desire and without transgression, there is no sin upon him."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has laid no hardship upon His servants in religion. I am resolved to hold fast to what He has commanded, and I seek His help in doing as He has ordered me. But what is the duty regarding my wealth, which you joined to my own person when you said, Strive with your person and your wealth?"

The scholar said: "As for your wealth, it is all that God has granted you of every kind of gain. Take it only from what is lawful, and spend it only in its rightful place."

The boy said: "And what is the lawful source it is taken from, and what is the rightful place it is spent in?"

The scholar said: "Its lawful source is the wholesome earnings in which you have wronged no one and which you have not tainted with falsehood. As for its rightful due, that is its zakat: look upon all that has come into your hands, whether little or much, and bring out a fifth of it—the finest of it and the dearest to you—and give it to the one whose claim upon you is binding. In this way your wealth is purified by alms, just as your body is purified by work. Work is a shield, and zakat is a purification, and he who purifies himself has prospered."

The boy said: "I shall do so, and I repent to God for what has gone before. Then what shall I do with the remainder of my wealth?"

The scholar said: "You shall spend upon yourself and your household in kindness. If your bounty then exceeds your need, share it with the destitute among your brethren and extend your favor to everyone who turns to you in want, for the man who has no bounty of his own stands in need of the favor of another. Be swift to every good and foremost in every act of bounty. This is the goal of the work, if you are patient in it."

The boy said: "Yes—patience, and then more patience. How could a man in great need fail to be patient toward one who has no need of his labor? Everyone in want is patient, and everyone who has been favored is grateful. Here is all my wealth before you, in five shares: one share is its zakat; one share is expiation for what I have squandered; one share is thanksgiving to God for what I have come to know; one share is for the poor among my brethren; and one share you shall accept by your grace, that it may be provision for you. Take them all to yourself, and God will send their like in their place."

The narrator said: The scholar's heart was stirred for the boy when he saw how the jewel within him had come clear, how the ordeal had brought forth a praiseworthy result, how his merit now stood plain and his work was made pure.

The scholar said, while his tears ran down: "O my son, for the like of you let the scholars travel the long road; for the like of you let the earth stand firm and the heaven be held up; for the like of you let the bonds of kinship be made plain, and let the rain come down from the openings of the clouds. So rejoice, my son, in what God has given you and in the breast He has opened to Islam. Take the glad tidings of a light from your Lord and a near opening, and receive the gift of God to you and the favors of His grace upon you with abundant work and noble thanks. As for my own share of this wealth, I have no need of it. I came out from wealth, and it is from the temptation of wealth that I fled. It would not be right, my son, that you should return me to what I have detested. As for your brothers' share, keep it for them; when they ask you for something, pass it to them by your own hand, so that they may know your kindness and give thanks for your goodness. As for the share of expiation, hold it with you until you know your fault. As for the share of thanksgiving, it is a trust kept for God until you reach your maturity and rise to the utmost of what you have come to know. As for the share of zakat, that too is a trust held for the one to whom it belongs. Take them all to yourself until you meet him, for he is the ruler over you and on your behalf."

The boy said: "And who is this noble one with you, whose judgment you do not overstep and whose command you do not transgress?"

The scholar said: "He is the one whose right God has made binding upon me and upon every believer, the one in whose hand are the keys of the gardens and the landmarks of the kingdom, the one whose palms are opened

with the light of Sinai. He is the cause behind the signs, the one whose knowledge calls to the loftiest of ends. Through him your light is completed, through him Allah perfects your affair, and at his hand you are blessed."

The boy said: "Such kindness as this—to whom does it belong, and who are you yourself?"

The scholar said: "I am his son and one of his servants in good works, seeking his pleasure and shunning his displeasure."

The boy said: "I think I am far from his good pleasure, and yet close enough that, had I not come to know of him, I would have an excuse. Do I have an excuse, given what I now know?"

The scholar said: "An excuse is granted only before one has been brought within reach of such knowledge."

The boy said: "Shall I speak, or shall I hold back?"

The scholar said: "Say what seems best to you."

The boy said: "Should you not, at this very moment, take me by the hand to the door of his chamber until I enter, and to the house of light until I draw from it? This is part of the full measure of truthfulness that is binding upon you."

The scholar said: "This is indeed your due—but only with his permission and by his decision."

Then the scholar took leave of the boy and departed. The boy wished to press provisions upon him, but the scholar declined to accept them. He went on until he came to his own father, and there he described the boy and made it plain that the boy was now of the company of the people of goodness and among the mines of excellence. The father marveled at this, and said: "Make haste to me with this boy, for I hope that he will be a door of mercy that Allah has opened for the people of his age."

The scholar lingered only a little, then returned to the boy, and found him in a state of body other than the one he had left him in.

The scholar said: "What is this I see in you, so unlike the state I left you in?"

The boy said: "And what is it you have found changed in me, may my father and mother be your ransom?"

The scholar said: "Weakness of body, and a yellowing of your color."

The boy said: "That is from a desolation that came over me after your departure."

The scholar said: "What is this desolation, and what is the cause of this weakness?"

The boy said: "Your absence made me desolate, and the dread of what might come to pass before I met you again weakened me."

The scholar said: "Allah has known what is in your heart, and so has made the trial light upon you, and has cast mercy for you into the heart of the one who has charge of you. So take up your provisions for the journey, for I carry high hope for you, and I will bring you to the door of your goal. There is no power except by Allah."

At this the boy was so overcome with joy and awe that his soul nearly departed his body, until Allah, knowing his condition, took hold of him and returned it to him. When he revived, he said: "Praise be to Allah, who has never withdrawn His compassion from the believers." He gave great thanks to his father, and came up to kiss his palms and his feet in service. Then he rose at once, took up the traveling kit, and the two set out until they reached the greater scholar. They sought leave and were admitted; they entered, gave their greetings, and sat down by his permission. When they were settled in the assembly, the boy said to his teacher: "Teach me how to speak and how to ask for what I need."

The teacher said to him: "It is not lawful for me to instruct you in anything while you are in this place, out of reverence for its master. You are in the hands of one who knows your need and knows what you intend."

So the boy held his peace. They remained with the master for a time, until the boy's guidance was completed, he reached the utmost of his hope, and came to know what was his and what was owed of him. Then he prayed, and the two withdrew. They had spoken no word in the assembly save greetings of peace and well-being; the boy had turned yellow from the intensity of his awe and could not bring himself to speak. The master, the lord of the house, gave orders that the two be honored and that whatever they required be provided, and he brought them on to his own dwelling. When he had led them in, he greeted them and showed his gladness in them.

He said to the scholar—for between them was brotherhood and an old companionship in learning: "Brother, is this the boy we used to hear about?"

He said: "Yes. This is the one whose remembrance grew sweet in the hearts, so that his mention rose upon the tongues of the believers."

The boy said: "Has the report of me truly reached the friends of Allah, until they recite my mention in their gatherings?"

The scholar said: "Yes, my brother. So it is with you."

The boy said: "Favors have encompassed me from every side, and hands of kindness have overtaken me from every road. I do not know whom to thank, nor whom to repay."

Then they all sat and conversed through their night. When morning came, they sought leave to enter upon the Shaykh. When they were admitted and had finished their greetings, the Shaykh gave them leave to sit, and they sat. Then the Shaykh began his address.

He said: "Praise be to Allah, who has parted the darkness of hearts with His light and opened with His justice what was closed against the seeker, and who has made remembrance easy by His fashioning, as a gentleness to His servants and a mercy from Him to those who long for Him. He has not burdened creation with the full measure of what is His due, nor has He willed for them the denial of His blessings; rather He has charged them, out of His justice, with only what they are not too weak to bear. So the matter was set balanced between two extremes, and for this it is named justice. He has spread out His hands with generosity in kindness and bounty; He has disappointed no one who called upon Him, and turned away no one who kept silent. His blessings are overflowing and His favors are pouring—neither reserved for the one who gives thanks for them because of the abundance of His reward, nor withheld from the one who is ungrateful for them because of the greatness of His punishment. He has kept His blessings above any stinting, and made His proofs too firm to be denied; He is wider than existence in His all-encompassing, and beyond all denial in the evidence for Him. He is present without limit, known without being fully described. Glory be to Him: how gentle is He, for all His greatness, toward His creation; how merciful, for all His might, toward His servants; how just, for all His power, in His ruling; how near, for all His loftiness, to His people; and how far, for all His nearness, from every illusion. So worship Him and give thanks to Him. To Him you shall return."

Then he said: "Now, beyond this: it belongs to the honor of minds that they be put to use in seeking. Among the fruits of seeking is finding what truly is, and among the marks of what truly is, is its sweetness. Every sweet, fresh spring is a drink whose surface is plain and whose depth is veiled; the one who pursues it never wearies of the thought of it, and creation never reaches the end of it. Its landmarks are a truth, and the truth of the truth is an obligation, and from this it is called a truth, because its obligation is made real. Whoever withholds from a truth its due has done wrong. Is it not the due of thought that the hearts pay their alms through good manners? And the due of good manners is the seeking of knowledge; and the due of knowledge is that it be acted upon; and the due of acting is that it be made pure through obedience to those charged with authority; and the due of obedience is that it be brought to perfection in hardship and in ease alike, in good fortune and in trial the same. None receive this save those who are patient, and none receive it save the possessor of a great share."

Then he turned his face toward the boy and said: "O boy, I have honored you as an estranged friend who has come to me, and I greet you as a visitor who has sought me out. So what is your name?"

He said: "Ubayd Allah, son of Abd Allah."

The Shaykh said: "That is your description, and your news has already been brought to us."

The boy said: "I am free, son of Abd Allah."

The Shaykh said: "And who freed you from a master, that you became free?"

The boy said: "This world had possession of me, and I have handed it back to the One who called it into being."

The Shaykh said: "I understand. If one is owned and possesses nothing of his own, is it permitted for him to grant you your freedom?"

The boy said: "It is not permitted."

The Shaykh said: "Then what is your name?"

At this the boy fell silent, at a loss for the answer.

The Shaykh said: "O boy, how can a thing be known when it has no name?"

The boy said: "Then I am one newly born to you. Name me."

The Shaykh said: "That waits upon the completion of seven days."

The boy said: "And why is it deferred to the completion of seven days?"

The Shaykh said: "For the dignity of the newborn."

The boy said: "And if the newborn dies before the seven days are complete?"

The Shaykh said: "Nothing harms him. He is named after that."

The boy said: "And this name you will name me by—is it mine?"

The Shaykh said: "Would you then be as an object of worship, even though you have been born?"

The boy said: "How, then, is this to be spoken of?"

The Shaykh said: "The name is yours, and you belong to the name. So it does not come forth except within your bound, and it goes with you for your appointed span."

At this the boy rose and hurried to obey him, and out of the beauty of his manners he did not answer back. He went out, and his father went out with him, until they returned to their lodging. The owner of the house greeted them and said: "How stands our brother in the matter of his need?"

The scholar said to him: "A promise has been given. The one who awaits it is thirsty, and the term is near."

So they remained until the seventh day, then sought leave of the Shaykh. When he had granted it, he ordered the boy to wash and to put on his purest garments. As the boy took up this preparation, his heart sensed that his need would be granted on that very day, and that this preparation he had been commanded was a sign of a good to come after it. When he had done what was commanded, they all went out. The boy went out saying, "A day of good fortune, and a guardian of good fortune," repeating it again and again; and nothing drove him to this saying but the abundance of his joy and the intensity of his longing to know what would draw him near to the pleasure of Allah. When they came in upon the Shaykh, they found that he had taken up the implements of his work. He greeted them with peace, and they returned the greeting.

Then he bade the boy draw close to him, and he drew close; and he brought him near, and he came near. And he began to speak of matters that no fancy had ever reached, that no pen had ever traced, that had never occurred to the heart of any human being—matters not to be mentioned in the assemblies of preachers, nor set forth openly in the gathered books, for the greatness of their worth, and unveiled to none but their own people. And when he had discharged what was owed and brought the boy out of the wildernesses of confusion that had led him astray, to the very end of guidance, his path was made pure and his draught made sweet, and he came to know his Lord. So he released his consecration by magnifying the sanctities of Allah, held fast to the firm rope, circled the ancient House, spent his rites in remembrance, and completed his pilgrimage by the greatest sign.

Then he and his father continued to come and go to the Shaykh, studying what had been given him. The boy bore patiently with the constancy of it; he never wearied of asking, for the nobility of it, and was never sated with the answering, for its sweetness. They stayed in that state for a season of time, until the Shaykh knew that the root had been watered and the branch had risen high.

Then the boy said to the Shaykh, in thanks to Allah and to him: "Praise be to Allah, who has made you worthy of this station, so that through you He gives life to religion and death to ignorance, and has made you a place of return for the people and a sanctuary. You are the gate of the people of heaven and the ascent of the people of the earth. From the light of your might the lights have appeared, and from the pouring of your hand the rivers have flowed. May you remain so, until the one who is pleased with you contents you."

The Shaykh said: "As for you, may Allah bless you in what He has given you, and aid you by His enabling grace to guard what has come to you, and employ you rightly in His will, and smooth your way with the light of His guidance, and open your breast with the keys of wisdom, and ennoble your affair with the path of the call, and perfect His favor upon you with the marks of an answered prayer, and bring you, in obedience to Him, to the best of your hope of His good pleasure. I bid you farewell, my son, and grant you leave to depart. Grievous to me is our parting, but three matters have driven us to it. The first is that we are setting out on a journey to another land. The second is your separation from your family for all this length of time, together with the hardship you took on in going out without your father's leave, for in him there is a heavy grief and a great anger that nothing will dispel but your return—though I have hope for your father, if you are gentle with him. And the third is the duty owed for the right of this grace that has reached you, for it is binding upon you to worship by it and to call others to it."

The boy said: "Your parting is a sorrow to me, and your loss is a want in me; yet your counsel is the more rightly guiding, your obedience the more binding, your judgment the more sound. So charge me with what you see fit, and dismiss me when you will, and be kind to me by letting my father go with me to my land, that I may lean upon him, act through him, and entrust all my affairs to him."

The Shaykh said: "That I shall do. And I enjoin upon you the fear of Allah who created you, the safeguarding of the trust you have labored to bear, and the care of your father, who reared you when you were small and toiled long in your affairs, with his own person and with his wealth. Do not clothe him in the garments of scorn, do not set him upon the mounts of ruin, do not leave his name unspoken, do not turn away from his remembrance, and do not deal falsely with him in your city; for at the very outset there is a sting."

The boy said: "May Allah reward you, on behalf of the Master of every grace, with the thanks of every grateful one; and may the Lord of abundant bounty repay you for your long shepherding of me, when hands are too weak to repay you. I will keep your charge. Do not cut off my share of the blessing of your supplication."

Then they rose together, clasped hands, and embraced, and each of them said his farewell to his companion, unable to contain himself for the weight of the parting, able to speak only by a gesture. Then they parted, and went their separate ways.

So the scholar and the boy traveled on until they had drawn near to the boy's city, where his father was. There the scholar said to him: "O my son, I have understood the will of the Shaykh, and you have not. Guidance lies only in his words. This is your city, and we have reached its edge. Sit with us here, apart from the road, for I wish to remind you of some of my concerns and to charge you with what you should do."

When they had sat down, the scholar said: "O my son, I know your father and his enmity toward the people of this matter. To that has now been added your going out with me and your absence from him, without his leave and without his trust. I wish to honor your father's view by concealing some of these affairs, so that you may return to him and he may be at ease, and remain near us. And I hope, if you are gentle with him, that he will be closer to what you desire than anyone else. Your absence has worn him down, and his solitude has become a terror to him.

"Know, my son, that Allah has opened to you the least of your aspirations, and this before you had supposed you would reach it save after a long stretch of time. Allah has been good to you, so be good to yourself, and honor the one through whom Allah has honored you. We have thought well of you, and our hope in you has been great. So preserve the trust your father entrusted to you, and stand firm in your affair. Piety and pious caution are the guardians of your religion and your work; thanksgiving and patience are an increase in your light. Seek from Allah the opening of your provisions and the goodness of His granting you success."

Then the scholar said: "I charge you with six qualities: trust in Allah when you are resolved; speak with a ruling when you are called upon; do not grow angry when you are wronged; do not weary when you are slandered; whoever answers your call to good, be patient with him; and single out your own self for the best of what you summon others to—for Allah will help you and will open the way before you."

The boy said: "May Allah reward you with three goods: an imam whose proof you have upheld; a trust in which you have counseled faithfully; and a dead man whom you graced with life. My heart is filled and my breast brims over with light and gladness at the greatness of what you have laid open. I do not know which of your words is the more excellent: the outward, which you arranged in its sequence, or the inward, which you expounded in its meanings."

The scholar said: "Praise be to Allah, who has made His way easy for you, brought its far reaches near to you, and helped you to bear its weight. I am now bidding you farewell and turning away from you. Is there any need still upon you?"

The boy said: "The pillars of my needs are three: a need already fulfilled, a need I pray for, and a need I ask you to grant of your grace."

The scholar said: "I have understood your three needs, and I answer you concerning what you ask. As for the need that has already passed, it is your life in knowledge after death; may Allah bless it for you as a life, and bring you in it to the best of what you intended. As for the need for which you pray, it is to act upon what you have come to know, and to have it accepted when you present it; may Allah bring that about for you by His mercy. As for the need you ask of me, it is as though you desired an increase upon what you have already heard."

The boy said: "There is no increase greater than your knowing what is within me, so grant of your grace what you see fit."

The scholar said: "As for my knowing what is within you, none knows the unseen but Allah; yet the believer sees by the light of Allah. As for the increase, I will make its matter plain to you and set you upon the foundations by which you are to work. You are to look upon all those things whose outward form I have set out for you, and read them as parables. Whatever among them is praiseworthy is a likeness of the truth and its people—the most excellent of the likenesses belonging to the most excellent of them, and their tokens pointing to them. And whatever among the parables was blameworthy, I turned it upon the enemies of the truth and its people—the great of the likenesses to the great of them and the small to the small, and their tokens pointing to them. These are the foundations of the knowledge upon which the work turns; if you preserve them, you will have no need of any others. And I charge you with the fear of the One who created you when you were nothing, with keeping His covenant, and with fulfilling the right He has laid upon you and upon all His creation."

When he had finished his discourse, delivered the boy to his safety, and completed his nurture, he said: "O my son, take care of yourself, for I have concerns besides you, and this is the parting between me and you."

At the mention of parting the boy gave such a gasp that his spirit almost took flight and his soul almost left his body. Then he bent to kiss the scholar's feet and wiped their dust upon his cheeks, and bade him farewell and departed. The boy returned to his house, his heart carried away from him in grief, unable to hold back a single word of his sorrow at parting from his elder father—the father who had been the cause of his grace—until his family and his neighbors grew afraid for him. He passed his day and his night restless with sorrow and alarm. Then his father came in upon him, angry.

## 8. Homecoming, the Father, and the Debate with Abu Malik

The father's anger did not spare him. "O my son," he said, "is this how sons repay their fathers? I taught you nothing but the finest a father can teach a child. You never betrayed your own soul nor renounced your beliefs — until the day I had grown old and this stranger came. You drew him in upon me, and you went over to his cause with him without ever consulting your parents. If what I held was true, then you have wronged me in my very self, for you hid the truth from me. And if it was false, then you have disobeyed me unto your own ruin, and whatever has overtaken him has thereby overtaken me."

The boy answered gently. "O my father, I have shown you nothing but mercy and compassion. You raised me as a mercy from you, you nourished me, and you chose me over kin and wealth. I have no wish to be unkind to you on this day of ours. Rather I ask that you grant me, out of your bounty, one of two things."

"Name them," said the Shaykh. "What are they?"

"Either you let me keep to my courtesy and my kindness toward you — for I am your son, whom you raised when I was small, and our affair rests in the hands of God — or you give me leave to debate with you, and your assurance that I am safe from your anger, so that I may draw upon your mind."

The Shaykh weighed the two. "As for courtesy on the surface while our hearts differ beneath, that is a reproach that would always stand between us. How long can outward agreement hold when what lies under it is at odds? I do not want that. But your debate and your candor you shall have. So say what you think best."

The boy said, "Consider my standing with you these forty years. Can it be anything but one of two cases? Either you are a scholar, and you withheld from me the teaching I was owed — and if so, do not blame me for seeking my own salvation through another, since you denied it to me. Or you are ignorant, and then you are excused in my eyes; but in that case you needed the stranger even more than I did. If I reached him before you, it is only because of your age and the lateness of your birth, for I have heard his words just as you have heard them, and I have seen what you have seen."

The boy's words tore at the Shaykh's heart. He saw there was no escape from either horn of the argument, and his eyes brimmed with tears. "O my son," he said, "the argument you have laid against me falls upon you too, if you keep your affair hidden from me. So tell it to me. If it is true, I will accept it from you, and count that a dignity to myself; and if it is false, I will turn you back from it, as a dignity to you and out of compassion for you."

So they debated from time to time and opened their hearts to one another at length, until both were guided to the right path — living by the religion of God, upholding it, holding fast to it, calling others to it, and helping one another in it. Through the two of them God revived many of His creatures. The son went out into the world to bring the good news of his father's guidance and to call others to the teaching, and he returned and became a means of guidance for them. God blessed the people of that land through them and by their hands. God's command was fulfilled, and slander was put to shame.

The boy's name was Salih, and his father's was al-Bakhtari. The two were honored above their people and raised to high standing for the excellence of the manners they had gained. News of them spread through the land, and with it news of their religion, until it reached a group who still clung to the old creed. Al-Bakhtari had long held influence among them, and they feared that his leaving their religion would cut that influence off. So they went out and gathered before one of their scholars, a man named Abu Malik, Abd al-Jabbar, the mighty servant — called among them, for his gravity, the abundance of his rulings, and his defense of his religion, "the heel of the inks." He was their authority by his knowledge, his command of speech, and his discernment of opinions.

When they came in to him they said, "O Abu Malik, do you not see what the boy has gone over to?"

"Which boy?" he asked.

"Salih ibn al-Bakhtari."

"And what has he gone over to?"

"We tell you that a stranger came to the country with a doctrine and a creed he calls people to, and we do not know what it is. The boy went to him and went out with him without his father's leave, and was gone a while. Then he came back speaking the stranger's words and calling to what the stranger called to. And we are told his father has now agreed with him and taken his side. This grieves us, and we have held back from them for three reasons. First: al-Bakhtari's household was as our own, for the bond of religion we shared, and we dread estrangement after intimacy and hate division after brotherhood. Second: if these people are upon the truth, then we have failed to follow them and to help them in it. Third: if they have gone astray, then we have failed to turn them back and to stand up for the truth and its people. You are the head of our religion and one of our standards, and we have come to inform you and to seek your counsel, and we will not go against your opinion."

Abu Malik said, "Be gentle in your judgment, for what you say has given me pause. You know that al-Bakhtari and his son were among those we counted as men of gravity and piety, and that they have claims upon us and old favors to their credit. You have said of them what you have said, and you are people in whom I trust the truth above all else, so that God has spared you from stooping to anything less. This is news that has come upon a people; an argument has been shown to them, and they have followed it and been satisfied by it. We cannot rule them astray and so wrong them, nor rule them guided and so wrong ourselves by imitating them, nor call them deceived and refute them without knowledge of their case, for they are nobler in our eyes than that. What we must do is look at this news with a fair eye, in the light of justice and with a steadfast mind. It has been said: he who is steadfast in his affairs keeps his standing secure, and he who is hasty cannot keep his foot from slipping.

"A breach in most things can be repaired, and what is spoiled in them can be replaced, as with a flaw in food, whose harm is easily remedied and whose cure is near at hand. But a breach in religion is gravely damaging and greatly ruinous. Its flaw cannot be mended nor traded for another, as with a flaw in a medicine, which gives its taker no reprieve and leaves him no way to save himself. We have been taught that the best of provisions are the treasures of sound minds and the gaining of manners, whose fruits are praiseworthy; and among those fruits is forbearance. Forbearance is the crown of the learned. By it noble resolve lifts them to the highest glories, their merit is made plain in every place, and they win the honor of gain with ease of asking. For ourselves we have laid up a measure of that forbearance we once borrowed, and something of the manners we gained in days now past, amid narrow means and hard striving. We bore that for its worth, and we call upon it against the blows of events and the accidents of the age. We cannot let it go to waste. This is the very hour of our need for it, of putting it to use in what has come upon us in the matter of our brothers.

"There is no sense in leaving them and their affair unexamined, without laying it open and debating it with them — with the forbearance of the capable and the earnestness of seekers, free of any doubt in what we hold and free of any accusation against these people. They may have turned from the truth through forgetfulness, and they are nobler than that; or they may have strayed into error, and that is beneath them. Falsehood has no cleverness left to it once the truth stands plain. So reflect with me on the affair of these people, for it cannot fall outside one of three cases. Either they are upon the very religion we know, and what has happened among them is only some addition of manners, reports, and verses — and the speech of men is much; or an increase has come to them upon the religion they already hold, and they have rejoiced in it, for knowledge is the lost property of every believer, and an increase of knowledge is better than a lack of it; or a guidance has come to them other than what they had, through the prophecy of a prophet brought to them and calling them to it, which they have followed upon clear proof. And this is not to be denied of the command of God. Creation will never reach the limit of God's decree over them or His will for them, however hard they strive, for God is at work every day."

They said, "O Abu Malik, we came to you with hearts full of rage against these people, ready to declare them unbelievers, to refute their opinion, and to make lawful to ourselves their blood and their wealth. And now we find our hearts humbled by your words, and by the way you have risen in argument on their behalf and spoken in their defense."

Abu Malik said, "This argument of mine is not for them. It is for the truth, that I may not betray it in its absence. I have risen for the argument of the truth when the one who would argue for it is not here. Nor is it a defense of them; I have defended myself and you against speaking falsehood and hurling accusations into the unseen about what we do not encompass in knowledge. For otherwise we would be like the tyrants of the nations before us, who were pleased with their own ways of judgment and rejoiced in the knowledge they held, until they imagined they had encompassed all that God commanded and taught and willed for His creation. They said God would send no messengers after their messengers, and they never supposed that beyond the knowledge they had reached there lay any further way. So they ruled that God should cut off His argument from His creation and His deputies in His earth. They made the knowledge of God in His servants, and what He sent His prophets and messengers with, no greater than the reach of their own minds; and whatever passed beyond that was, in their eyes, misguidance. By that opinion they went astray and led many astray. They made lawful by their own ruling what God had forbidden, and by their own opinion they killed the prophets of God. I beg you, by God, do not follow the whims of a people who went astray before you. People of intellect may be swayed by whim toward every notion and lured away from every path — save the path of religion, for in seeking it there is safety, and its merit is not hidden."

They said, "O Abu Malik, we will not go against your command, nor is your counsel hidden from us. We will not obey you at the start of our affair only to disobey you at its end. You have pointed to a course and hinted at a search. If you see fit, take us to these people, that their doctrine may be laid open to us and we may look into it. If it is sound guidance, we will follow it; and if it is error, we will avoid it."

Abu Malik said, "This is doubt on your part about what you yourselves hold, and one who doubts neither knows his own right nor the falsehood of another."

They said, "Then we hold firm to our truth and reject whatever else there may be, whatever it be."

Abu Malik said, "That is not how meaning is sought, nor is guidance found by rejection."

They said, "How then is the search to be made, if we neither reject falsehood nor affirm the truth?"

Abu Malik said, "If you already know the truth and accept it, and know falsehood and reject it, then you are not among the seekers at all. You are among those already learned in prophecy and guidance, who judge by revelation over the people of the world."

They said, "Then we are content with the truth we hold and have no doubt in it, and we ask to be excused from the labor of seeking."

Abu Malik said, "Are you not content that they should have merit over you in one respect, that you would grant them merit over you in many? If you will not consent to stand in need among the ranks of the seekers, will you then sit in ignorance among those who hold back?"

They said, "How has this come upon us, and in what have they been given merit over us?"

Abu Malik said, "Their merit over you is that they know what you know and, beyond it, what you do not. This is a matter that has come upon a people. If you do not seek them out, they will seek you out to it; and if you will not listen to them, they will strive against you for it. So they will gain merit over you on both counts — by being first, and by their striving. As for holding back from the search, that is ignorance entire, and negligence, and a fault: a fault in the learned until he teaches, and in the ignorant until he learns."

They said, "O Abu Malik, you have caught us with proof on every side, and your every word is right. Now open for us a door in your counsel by which we may act."

Abu Malik said, "To be dazzled by one's own opinion is blindness, and to neglect the search is error; yet there is no shame in the search for those who seek. But the seeker must know the doors of seeking before he can rightly seek the truth by that knowledge. Whoever seeks the truth without knowing how to seek is the quicker to deny it, for falsehood shows its first signs in display and in agreement with prevailing opinion, while the truth shows its first signs in hardship and in opposition to whims. And none forsakes the agreement of his whim and bears patiently the onset of hardship except one whose heart is sound."

They said, "Here a door has opened for us, in which you point us toward the knowledge of seeking. Show us, then: what is that knowledge, and what is the sound heart?"

Abu Malik said, "As for the knowledge of seeking, it is that you know yourselves to be in need; and the needy one seeks his need in the humility of want before what he seeks. As for the sound heart, it is a heart that does not persist in denying whatever is brought to it, whatever it be, until the truth is what makes itself plain and shows its merit, and falsehood is what lays bare its own defect. Only then does such a heart discern falsehood by its reason and accept the truth by acceptance."

They said, "But we have been taught that whoever stands in this position — who neither verifies the truth nor refutes falsehood — is ignorant in his conduct, for he knows neither the truth, that he might follow it, nor falsehood, that he might avoid it."

Abu Malik said, "What you have related, you have believed. But has such a one not passed beyond the bounds of the liars and become, as it were, two ignorant men in one?"

They said, "Yes. With us he is ignorant, not a liar."

Abu Malik said, "The ignorant man is nearer to the good and to seeking it. He seeks knowledge for himself; and if he does not, the learned seek it out for him, because ignorance is the original condition of creation, and there is no shame in it for one who seeks knowledge on account of his ignorance. But lying is the worst of wares. The liar is not guided by his lying, and he is never believed in what stands recorded against him."

They said, "You have made us certain of it. We have come to know the defect of lying, and so we will never lie about a thing until we know it for what it is. And you have taught us the door of the request, which is reverence in one's need toward what is sought. So is this reverence and humility toward what is sought to be shown after it has made its right plain, or before the statement is given?"

Abu Malik said, "Rather before the statement, so that by your reverence you may grasp the statement itself."

They said, "But if we revere what is sought until it becomes clear what it holds, and then its statement proves misleading — what then is the worth of our reverence? Has it not been wasted?"

Abu Malik said, "No. Rather it was reverence toward God and humility before His command, and its reward is yours even if the one before whom you humbled yourself did not deserve it. For the seeker has discharged the duty of the request, even if the duty of the truth was not discharged for him. His case is like that of one who stands before some king to ask a favor, and who can ask only with reverence, flattery, and insistent complaint. If the king grants him something of what he asked, the king has met the duty of grace and earns the reward of what he gave. And if he grants nothing, the petitioner has still discharged the duty of asking gently, while stinginess has bound the withholder to his refusal. So too the seeker of knowledge: he must begin with humility, persistence, and patience under trial until he has discharged the duty of seeking, and then time will bring him afterward whatever it brings."

They said, "This is the manifest, evident truth. Will you permit us, then, to go out to the people as you have counseled? And we will not treat what you have commanded as a burden."

Abu Malik said, "I would not counsel you to anything while begrudging myself the same. Were it not for the fear of falling short by turning you away from the path of God, I would have counseled you to hold back while I went ahead of you to the good, keeping that honor for myself and preferring my own soul above every other. But we go together. I comfort you with my own presence, as an honor to you and in thanks for the goodness you have singled me out to share."

They said, "It is as though you were expecting this affair before today."

He said, "This affair in particular, no. But I am always expecting deliverance from God from every side, hoping for good by every road, inclining my ear toward every speaker and seeking an increase from his words, whether he be right or wrong."

They said, "An increase from the words of the truthful, that we understand. But what do you hope to gain from the words of the false?"

Abu Malik said, "An increase of truth upon truth is light, and to recognize falsehood in the false is an increase in certainty; and the greater the certainty, the greater the light of the heart. None are saved with God except the people of certainty. I am rising now to go to the people. If you are resolved to rise with me, then look to yourselves. Whoever knows a sin in himself, let him repent to God; whoever bears a debt, let him repay it to its owner. Wash yourselves clean of every defilement, put on for the request the purest garments of resolve, and bring to what is sought the intention of the needy — for I myself am doing this. Our going out is a going out to God. If He knows truthfulness in us, He will grant us success to His truth; and if He knows in us any mockery of the faith, He will turn us from His signs by the smallest disgrace, keeping His friend pure of the mockers."

They said, "Yes. We will do what you have commanded, within three days."

He said, "No."

They said, "Within two days, then?"

He said, "No — only within this our day. If you do it, well; and if not, then I go on alone."

They said, "Then we repent to God from every sin, we discharge every debt, and we do what you have commanded. There is no power except in God."

So they did this from that very day, and they all went out together until they came to the gate of al-Bakhtari. When they had greeted him and were seated, Abu Malik said to al-Bakhtari, "O Abu Salih, how is your son Salih, and where is he?"

Al-Bakhtari said, "Today Salih is my father, and I am the son of Salih; and Salih is with his Lord."

Abu Malik supposed that Salih had died, taking his father's words to mean that he was with his Lord, and he said, "إنا لله وإنا إليه راجعون. Has Salih died?"

Al-Bakhtari said, "Salih has not died and will never die. He abides as long as time abides."

Abu Malik said, "Here are three matters whose meanings have been turned about. The child has become the father; the Lord is found in the earth, and the Lord has a servant to whom one is brought through Him; and the one created for perishing has become everlasting. A whiff of perfume for perplexed seekers — and behind this lie further wonders."

Al-Bakhtari said, "O Abu Malik, come to your need, and be steady in your asking, for you are at the very beginning of the trial."

Abu Malik said, "You have spoken truly. The beginning of the truth is a trial. My need is to meet the righteous servant."

Al-Bakhtari said, "There is no way to that. But perhaps you mean my son, Salih?"

He said, "Yes."

He said, "That is by his leave."

So the old man rose in haste until he came in upon his son Salih and told him of Abu Malik and his companions, and Salih was glad of it for what he knew of the excellence of their minds. He said, "O God, open the hearing of Your servants' hearts, guide them by Your mercy to the signs of what is sought, and expand our breasts with the light of Your veiled light." Then he wiped his hand over his face, cleared the assembly for them, and gave his father the word to bring them in.

When they had come in upon him, greeted him, and taken their seats — Abu Malik and those below him seated by introduction, and Salih drawing Abu Malik near to himself — the first thing Salih said was, "O Abu Malik, you have honored us with your visit and outstripped us to the good by your generosity, though you had the better right that we should visit you, to keep your affection and to discharge the duty long owed to you."

Abu Malik said, "And when were you ever without excellence, O Abu al-Khair? You were forbearing with us when you were small and generous to us when you had grown, and you became among us a caller and a warner to the good. Your root is wholesome, your branch is high, your work is pure. So joy be to you."

Salih said to him, "Have you come to me as one making an accusation, O Ka'b al-Ahbar, or as one merely imitating? Where is that perfect mind and noble forbearance I have always known in you?"

Abu Malik said to him, "Your rank is too high for accusation, and religion stands too high for imitation."

Salih said to him, "Then how do you name me a warner, when the warner is a prophet, and the prophet is a proof from God between Him and His creation, for them and against them? How can one be a proof for you who is not also a proof against you?"

Abu Malik said, "You have spoken truly. This is the obligation. In this matter we do not act on the ease of a phrase. But we began with you in the fairest way. I saw that truthfulness was more fitting for one who has set himself as a veil for the religion and a champion for the truth, and has opened its doors to seekers. We have come to know the excellence of seeking, so we sought you and hoped for you as the one sought. Come, then, to our need."

Salih said, "O Abu Malik, is not the one whose excellence you have come to know also one whose right becomes binding upon you?"

He said, "Yes, it becomes binding."

He said, "And when you came to know the excellence of seeking, did you come to know its right?"

He said, "And what is its right?"

Salih said to him, "To know its faces, so that from among them you may aim at the one in which the right becomes due to you."

Abu Malik said, "And what are its faces, and how many are they?"

Salih said to him, "Seeking has three faces: a seeker who knows, a seeker who is coming to know, and a seeker who knows only that he does not know. Which of the three are you?"

Abu Malik said, "I have more need to know all three than anything else. How could I claim a station I do not know?"

Salih said to him, "Then how did you claim to be a seeker at all, when you do not know the faces of seeking?"

Abu Malik said, "I did not know them. So make them plain to me."

Salih said, "Wealth is the treasure of the people of this world, and knowledge the treasure of the people of the Hereafter. The seeking of wealth has three faces: a seeker who asks for what he already owns, a seeker who asks for what is owed to him at a named term, and a seeker who asks for the bounty of alms. So too the seeking of religion and the treasures of the Hereafter has three faces. The first is the seeker who already knows, and he is the divine scholar, the `alim rabbani`, who seeks out the dead in their ignorance in order to revive them by his knowledge. The second is the seeker who is coming to know, and he is the learner who has grasped some of the degrees of knowledge and seeks its farthest end and limit. The third is the seeker who is only becoming acquainted with himself, and he is the ignorant man made known by his ignorance, who knows nothing beyond knowing that he is in need, and so he seeks the scholars that he may learn. These are the three faces, and the descriptions of the seekers."

Abu Malik said, "Yes, these are the faces of seeking, in religion and in the world. And I am the seeker who is only becoming acquainted with himself, who knows nothing yet has come to know that he is in need. So teach me."

He said, "Did you come to know it, or did you come to see it?"

Abu Malik said, "And what is the difference between the two?"

Salih said, "Knowledge is report; direct acquaintance is eyewitness sight."

Abu Malik said, "This too is among the things one needs to know."

Salih said, "As for the way of report, it is the case of an ignorant man who does not know that he is ignorant until he meets a scholar, and the scholar tells him of his ignorance; then he knows, by that scholar's word, that he is an ignorant man in need of knowledge, and his knowledge of his own ignorance has become a matter of report. As for acquaintance with one's need and ignorance by direct sight, it is the man who knows in his own mind that he is asking about something and does not know the face of its answer; his breast is straitened by that ignorance, and he hopes for no relief except by knowing what he was ignorant of, so his ignorance has itself become a knowing by his own mind. Ignorance is want, and want is narrowness, and narrowness is a severe need that drives the creature to seek breadth; and the breadth is the breadth of knowledge, for knowledge is wide and spacious. From this I have come to know that you are in need."

Abu Malik said, "I find within myself everything you have described. I have come to know that I am needy, of severe want. So relieve my want by your excellence."

Salih said, "I see you hastening in the request before it is firmly settled."

Abu Malik said, "Want hastened me by its narrowness, so I hastened; and I came to know the excellence of breadth, so I sought."

Salih said to him, "If want has hastened you and you have come to know the excellence of breadth, then how will you come to knowledge of the one you seek?"

Abu Malik said, "Your excellence was proven sound to me, so I made for you; and the meaning came near, so I sought you."

Salih said to him, "From where was it proven to you that I, and not you, am the one known for breadth, so that you sought me? And from where did you come to know that I am the one sought, so that you made for me? No scholar versed in prophethood pointed you to me, that you might know; nor did you see in me the confirmations of the proofs, that you might recognize me. Do you not see that in your seeking you are reaching for the ease of imitation, and so you wrong wisdom and you wrong yourself — like one who measures with the underside of the measure? If he does not strike it level, he wrongs his creditor, and if he strikes it too full, he wrongs himself. So it is with one who takes knowledge by imitation: if he acts on it, he is not thanked, and if he leaves it, it is said that he has disbelieved. And you, in my judgment, are wiser than this."

Abu Malik said, "I truly know that it is as you say. But I hoped that the truth would be that which establishes itself, and that I might spare you the labor of the proof; for disputation breeds contention, and contention breeds rancor, since each adversary seeks his own victory over his fellow. When will he ever reach the truth who persists in defending the false?"

Salih said to him, "How excellent is what you have said. The truth does indeed establish itself by its own excellence and honor, and disputation, when it grows great, only occupies hearts away from what is worthier than it. But there is no forsaking what the scholars have laid down as a way, for they have said: no crop whose ground has not first been made wholesome for it grows pure, and no knowledge for which the hearts have not been purified by proof increases."

Abu Malik said, "Then recite whatever proofs you wish. For the speech of wisdom is like the jewel: if you turn it about, its light glints; and if you set it aside, its worth is not hidden."

Salih said to him, "You have spoken truly — yet that is so only when there is no counterfeit of coated glass set beside the jewel; then the jewel stands under no concealment. But when claims grow many, and the tyrants of the world contend over religion until they have clothed it in what is not itself and coated it with a likeness before the seekers, then the seeker is obliged to put the jewel to the test, until its worth is made plain and the counterfeit and its people are exposed."

Abu Malik said, "You have dealt justly and discharged the duty of the truth. As for this people's counterfeiting, we have come to know it and its defects, so we rejected it and held fast to this view by which we have come to know you, and we said it is the sound jewel of speech. We stood by it, and you agreed with us upon it, and we all clung together to its rope. If you have found a meaning more precious than it as a jewel, then by my life, jewels do surpass one another. So show me the worth of your jewel by a test through which its worth is known. As for our jewel, we have known it and you have known it, so say of it whatever appears to you."

Salih said to him, "As for your jewel, with us it was noble, of the rank of the sound jewel — until we presented it to the jewelers and the people of the mines, and they judged it counterfeit and would not accept it from us as a jewel, and said, this is coated glass. They laid its defects bare for us, and it fell from the bounds of the jewel. For the jewel is raised only by its preciousness, by the discernment of the people of insight into its worth, and by their vying over it, so that its price climbs to every height. And once the jewelers have cast it down, who else can know it, or who will accept it after that but the people of delusion, who neither sell nor buy? So too is knowledge: it is not knowledge until it is presented to those who know the knowledge of heaven. If they accept it, it is sound knowledge; and if they do not accept it, it is idle prattle, and idle prattle has no place in wisdom and is not accepted by the wise."

Abu Malik said, "And who are these that know the knowledge of heaven after the prophets and the messengers? For we know of no one of them in this age of ours, and the people have had no acquaintance with such since revelation by the scriptures was cut off; and the nations follow nothing now but transmitted reports. So what do you say of this?"

Salih answered, "Any religion that stands only upon narration is not called a religion, for religion is what is valued by God in its own right and admits no difference within it, whereas the narrations, as you well know, are full of disagreement, each denying the other. And the religion of God is dearer than that."

"Yet when they differ in the narration," said Abu Malik, "they refer back to the books. Whatever agrees with the books they accept, and whatever contradicts them they reject as false."

"But what is found in the books only in general terms," Salih replied, "who then will make it specific for them? And what is found there already specified, who will connect it back for them? Who among them is trusted concerning what God has made lawful and what He has forbidden? I heard you myself on the day of the quarrel, when a distressed man came to you and said, O Abu Malik, I swore an oath of divorce and then broke it. You said to him, Some hold that she is now cut off from you and not lawful to you until she marries another husband, and some hold that she is still your wife and is not made forbidden to you by a single pronouncement of divorce. The man asked you, Then which of the two shall I act upon? and you told him, Act upon whichever of the two you wish. So he rose and left you not knowing whether the home he returns to is lawful or forbidden. Is this how judgment is rendered in the religion of God? Is this how His lawful and His forbidden are determined?"

Abu Malik said, "By my life, the Sharia of this age has been ruined, corrupted, and left idle, and we hold now to nothing but the name of religion. So what is the state of religion with you?"

"And what is religion, O Abu Malik?" Salih asked.

"Command and prohibition," he said, "the lawful and the forbidden, the Sunnah and the obligations."

"And in your view," said Salih, "is Sharia anything other than this?"

"I know of nothing beyond what I have described to you."

"Then you have gathered religion and Sharia onto a single ground?"

"Yes."

"Then religion has been left idle by the suspension of the Sharia," said Salih, "and corrupted by its corruption. What is left with you?"

At this Abu Malik fell silent a while, his head bowed, turning over in his mind the matter of his religion. Then he raised his head and said, "I do not see that anything is left with me. So what religion do you hold?"

Salih said, "Religion, with me, is that whose veil is made firm against the corruptors and whose doors are opened wide to the seekers, so that the one who brings it is ennobled by his precedence and the one who seeks it attains the good; through its excellence works are honored, and through its justice the sacred laws flourish. It is a cause joining heaven to earth, connected and unbroken — the handhold of those who take hold and the rope of those who cling fast, the ark of tranquility, the ship of salvation, and the light of life. Its content is generous, its sciences are safeguarded, its jewels are marked with a sign, and their worth is hidden from no one who looks upon them."

"If God has a religion by which men are to be judged one day," said Abu Malik, "with which He is pleased, and by which He rewards and punishes, then it must be in this sense you describe. And now the excellence of the attribute has become plain — so how then is the One who is thus described?"

"The good comes only from what is good, O Abu Malik," Salih said. "Set your light in the lamp-niche of your mind and grasp what is intended for you. The claims of destiny have pulled you into their contest, the robes of vanity have dazzled you, and you have taken upon yourself a burden that no one else can bear for you, together with the affairs of those who follow you in good faith, who have listened to your speech, accepted your guidance, and trusted your judgment. It is your duty to guard yourself and them from a fire you cannot escape passing near, so seek out the road to deliverance from it. You are the most favored of them in the grace of God and the most bound to give thanks for it, for I have known you to recognize the good and to hope for its reward. As for what you asked — the confirming of that to which I have called you — it is my obligation to establish it for you from whatever aspect you wish."

"All that you have said so far is sound and firmly true," said Abu Malik. "As for the narrations of the common people, religion is not set right by them, nor are they fit for it. There remains for us only to know that to which you call us."

"I call you," said Salih, "to the justice of God and His oneness."

"How excellent is that to which you call," Abu Malik replied. "Indeed, this is the very doctrine we profess, and we know its excellence above all other schools of thought."

"That is not where your thought has gone, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "The doctrine you mean is a description of justice without knowledge of the One so described, and a use of the names of God without knowledge of His oneness — and this I refuse. What I call you to is the knowledge of God's justice and the true landmarks of His unity. If you wish, I will hold you back from the corruption of that saying however you like."

"If the doctrine of the people of justice is corrupt and their profession of oneness is denial," said Abu Malik, "then there is no firm truth left upon the earth, unless the meaning lies outside all the doctrines altogether. So show us the corruption of justice and of oneness, and from where it comes."

"Justice and oneness are among the noblest of all doctrines," said Salih. "Oneness is too great to be corrupted, for there is nothing set against oneness, and justice cannot be corrupted, for it is the judgment that stands between God and His creation. But the doctrines are all spoiled by the way men have corrupted the saying of justice and the rest, for it becomes a mere claim by which no statement is proven, and they do not encompass the knowledge of what they say until they return to the command of God. So take up whichever of the two you wish and defend it, until I strip it away from you by the argument of truth."

"I will not defend a saying I do not hold," said Abu Malik, "nor one whose author never asked this of me, who never appointed me his agent, nor am I a guardian over the masters of these doctrines. I will defend before you only my own saying. I say that God is One, nothing is like Him, just in His judgment, who does not burden His servants with what they have no power to carry. He commanded them and forbade them, yet He did not command by compulsion nor forbid by force; rather His command is a giving of choice and His prohibition is a warning, so the obedient did not obey under coercion, and the disobedient did not disobey as one overpowered. This is my meaning and what I profess."

"Did I not tell you," said Salih, "that this doctrine is among the noblest? But these are names and attributes that profit nothing unless their meaning is present behind them. It is like your saying, fire is hot. You have spoken truly, yet the truth does not profit you, for your saying fire is hot is only the name of an attribute, and to utter it profits nothing if no food is cooked by it and no darkness is lit by it; those who speak it remain in the dark without the meaning of fire being present. Were names and attributes to stand in things in the place of meanings, then the mere mention of fire would burn your tongue, and the mention of food would leave you full."

"As for what you say of these things, you have spoken truly," said Abu Malik, "for their names and attributes profit nothing unless their meaning is present. So show me that my words about justice and oneness are names and attributes that require the knowledge of a named and described reality."

"You say that God is One," said Salih, "and One is four letters. We see two letters joined and two letters apart. So which of them is one, or are they all together one? And you say Allah, and that too is written in letters. Is the alif alone the name, or are all the letters together Allah, or are they signs pointing to God, or do you say something other than this?"

Abu Malik was struck silent a while. Then he said, "I find no way out of this except that they are signs pointing to God."

"Which of them points to God?" Salih asked. "And by what do they point to Him — by their being apart or by their being joined? Or singly and together, as when you say Allah, where the alif stands alone and the three letters are connected. Which then indicates God — this one standing alone, or these joined together?"

"I do not know what to say to you about this," said Abu Malik. "Take us on to some other door."

"Then the door to the knowledge of oneness has been shut in your face, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "Whoever taught it to you in this fashion has closed it against you. Is it not so?"

"Yes," he said.

"Then I ask you about your knowledge of God," said Salih. "From where does it come, and from whom did you take it? Was it from an attribute that led you to One described?"

"No."

"Was it from a name that led you to One named?"

"Yes."

"And what is the name that led you?"

"Allah."

"Is Allah a name," asked Salih, "or the One named?"

"The One named."

"Then what is the name?"

"Allah."

"So Allah is a name for Allah?"

"Yes."

"Then what is the difference between the name and the One named?" asked Salih. "And which of the two do you worship — or both?"

Abu Malik was perplexed a while, turning over his answer in his mind. Then he said, "I find no way through this except that I have seen that every craft has a maker, and so I judged that this vast creation has a Creator who resembles nothing of what He has made, just as craftsmen do not resemble the things they fashion."

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "you have strayed from the first meaning. The narrowness you met in the meaning of oneness has driven you to seek Him by analogy and to set up likenesses for Him, and God is dearer than that. For everything made is made from some prior thing, whereas God did not create things from anything that existed before them. As for these craftsmen, you know them because you have watched some of them with your own eyes and seen how they work; so you passed judgment upon a making whose maker was hidden from you, on the strength of an action resembling it that you witnessed among the craftsmen you have seen and known, reasoning by comparison. But when God created, He did not make you a witness to the creation of what He fashioned, nor did He have any likeness that fashions as He fashions. Yet you have testified from a present likeness to an absent one. I never knew you, O Abu Malik, to be content with analogy in your religion — so how can you be content to seek the knowledge of your Lord by measurement? Your very words, nothing is like Him, prove out of your own mouth that He has a likeness, and that nothing is like that likeness. Had you meant true oneness, you would have said, He is not like anything, and you would not have said, nothing is like Him. Glory be to the One whom no reach of aspiring thought can grasp, and whom no soaring of the pointing mind can attain by perception, exalted far above the description of those who are charged with duty. O Abu Malik, had you turned this search upon one of His servants, or a king among His angels, you would be left unable to comprehend what is said of that one and how he was made — so how then of his Creator? The lights of the people of the earth fall short of perceiving the light of the sun, and the sun is but one of the lights of this world. How then shall one who falls short before a creature of His perceive the Creator Himself? Say now what seems right to you."

Abu Malik cast his eyes to the ground, bewildered, finding no path to speech.

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "raise your head and say whatever comes to you."

"Speech is gone, and there is nothing to say," said Abu Malik. "Minds are confounded before the answer, the imaginings are struck dumb before thought, and the faculty is too weak; by it one can reach nothing but the seeking and the asking."

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "the One who is of such greatness — do the eyes perceive Him?"

"No, He is higher than that."

"Is speech heard from Him?"

"There is no speech except from a speaker," said Abu Malik, "and the speaker is described by speech, and God is too great for such attributes."

"This Vast One of greatness, this Exalted One of power," said Salih, "would He be unjust in His creation, charging them with what they cannot bear and commanding them with what they cannot grasp, and then punishing them if they fail to do it?"

"Whoever charges the obedient servant with what he has no power to bear," said Abu Malik, "has laid an act of disobedience upon him, and to punish him for it would be a wrong, and God is dearer than that."

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "the One who is thus removed from injustice and thus exalted above wrongdoing — would He be content that ignorance should be a familiar companion to His servants?"

"Ignorance is a sure denial of thanksgiving and a persistence in unbelief," said Abu Malik, "and God does not love the unbelievers."

"Were they then created as knowers," asked Salih, "already aware of what is wanted of them?"

"No," he said. "He created them ignorant, knowing nothing at all."

"Then from where do they come by the knowledge of what He wants of them," asked Salih, "so that it is made binding upon them to please Him, and so that they may be guarded from His wrath?"

"They learned this from God," said Abu Malik.

Salih answered, "If He is higher than sight can reach, wider than perception can grasp, and greater than the limits of speech, then how does the one whom He commands and forbids come to know what is wanted of him — so as to obey Him rather than disobey, and thank Him rather than disbelieve — unless you accept that there are causes for this knowledge set between Him and His creation? Are there those who deliver His justice and bear witness to His witnessing, for them and against them — men whom He has chosen by His knowledge and selected out of all His creation, whom He has made messengers to them and proofs against them, so that they stand as the just ones between Him and His servants, conveying His command and His prohibition? Obedience to them is then obedience to God and the winning of His pleasure, and disobedience to them is His wrath and His punishment. Otherwise, how could the command come from God to His creation at all?"

"This, for me," said Abu Malik, "is among the foundations of justice. Once creation stands in need of God's justice to teach them what He wants of them, and He teaches them through the hand of the righteous one He has appointed for that, then each shall thank his benefactor, be rewarded as God wills, be reproached, and be punished if God wills."

Salih said to him, "Is it permissible to rule with justice in any way other than this?"

"It is permissible for none but God," he said, "and God is the wisest of judges."

"Would it be permissible for Him," Salih went on, "to entrust the command to some of His creatures, setting them up in justice against their own wrongdoing, and then to lay the very same charge upon others, yet not guide these others to it by a just guide as He guided the first?"

"The judgment can only be one," said Abu Malik, "because justice is one. Were the ruling split into two meanings, one of the two could not escape injustice."

Salih said to him, "Has it not been established for you, then, that the judgment of God is one, with no difference in it, and that His way is one, unchanged in His messengers? Were it not for the unbroken succession of the messengers and the firm standing of command and prohibition, the truth would have no growth and creation no meaning."

"Yes," he said, "this is the truth."

"Is not their succession from God to His creation," said Salih, "so that He may make obedience binding upon them and set them as an authority between Himself and His creatures — to bear witness for whoever obeys them, so that his obedience is accepted, and to bear witness against whoever disobeys them, so that such a one is turned away?"

"The judgment could be nothing else," said Abu Malik, "for otherwise He would have given the lie to His messengers and broken His promise, and God is too generous for that. The affair of the messengers has made plain that they are of His justice, and that creation worships Him by obeying them. But tell me of the ranks of these just ones with God. Are they equal, or have some a merit over others?"

Salih said to him, "How could they stand level in rank when they are bound to obey one another? Were they equal in rank, none of them would obey another. Rather, by His justice He has preferred some above others, so that obedience passes from the lesser to the greater, and from the greater to one greater still, until obedience and worship reach the utmost of what God wills."

"Then the one who obeys the greater among them, and the greater one himself," asked Abu Malik, "are they equal in reward?"

"Yes," he said. "This belongs to the justice of God, which grants every obedient one his reward as a due, and no one is questioned as to the limit of the greater or the lesser."

"Is any one of the excellent ones," asked Abu Malik, "matched by his opposite?"

"No," he said, "for they come from the Creator bearing knowledge of the unseen in the Books He has revealed to them, and no one can bring the like of these except from God."

"Then can anyone match the lesser among them," he asked, "if no book is sent down to him carrying revealed knowledge of the unseen?"

"No one can lay claim to that station," he said.

"And how is that," he asked, "when they did not distinguish themselves from creation by the unseen, as the excellent one distinguished himself?"

Salih said to him, "If the excellent one distinguished himself from others by knowledge of the unseen in the revelation, then the lesser likewise distinguished himself from others by knowledge of the unseen in the interpretation. Both the revelation and its interpretation are from God, and what is with God is attained only through inspiration."

"And the lesser is also inspired?" asked Abu Malik.

"Yes," he said, "and for that reason obedience to him became obedience to God."

"If you set him level with the excellent one in obedience and in inspiration," asked Abu Malik, "why do you call him the lesser?"

"By his obedience to the excellent one," said Salih, "and his need of him."

"And what need has he of a creature," asked Abu Malik, "when the inspiration comes to him from the Creator?"

Salih said to him, "Though the inspiration comes to him, it comes bearing the interpretation of the reports and the Books and the rest of what the excellent ones left behind — as it was revealed to Lot at the hand of Abraham, so that Abraham's excellence was made plain, and as it was revealed to Ishmael and to Isaac, so that the goods, the blessings, and the merit were set down to Abraham. The inspiration from God is joined to His just ones in His land, each according to his rank. He does not, in His justice, will to cut off His command and His prohibition and His worship from His servants after He has favored them with it, so that worship would be severed and ignorance made lawful."

"The bearer of the revelation," said Abu Malik, "has shown his truthfulness by the knowledge of the unseen in his book. By what does the bearer of the interpretation show that he too holds knowledge of the unseen, so that the revelation bears witness for him and makes obedience to him binding as it does for the bearer of the revelation?"

Salih said to him, "In his interpretation he brings knowledge from heaven and a declaration from the highest assembly, and the Book bears witness to it."

"Then I bear witness," said Abu Malik, "to what the Books bear witness to: that every interpretation the revelation testifies to is a revelation from God. Otherwise all the Books and the messengers would have to be given the lie, since their meaning is one and the same." And he added, "If some of them give the lie to others, all must be reckoned liars; and if some confirm others, all must be confirmed.

"Such is justice," Abu Malik said, "and by it the truthfulness of those who hold to it is known, and their merit. Obedience is binding to those confirmed among the messengers who are His justice within His creation, whom He charged to convey His message. Yet if obedience is binding through prophethood, revelation, and interpretation, then what of those who sit in the seats of the prophets, resembling them though they are not of them? They have all agreed that after their prophet there is no prophet and no warner in their age, that they themselves are the ones who uphold the command of God and His religion, and that what they say leads to God's reward or His punishment. You say otherwise. So how have the scholars of this community failed to look into this matter?"

Salih said to him, "They have not wholly left it, but they have not looked into it with a sound looking, and no one has spoken to them of it so that they might incline their minds toward it, for it has been made to soak into their hearts that they are the worthiest of God's command and His religion, and that there is no prophet after their prophet — just as the seducers of the nations before them said. So every nation has claimed that there is no prophet after its own prophet."

"What was the beginning of that," asked Abu Malik, "and what did they intend by it?"

Salih said to him, "Its beginning was three in every age: a rebellious devil, a stubborn tyrant, and a wrangling jurist. By this saying they sought to cut the marks of the prophets off from the special gifts of prophethood, and to make it a possession passed among themselves. They could not do so until they had steeped the hearts of the nations in the saying, put to every nation, that God sent none better than your prophet, that there is no prophet after him and no warner. So they whispered to them from where they did not perceive, and turned them aside from where they did not know. Each nation clung to its own prophet by giving the lie to whoever came after him, supposing that this drew them nearer to their prophet. Each nation claimed that every prophet after its own was a liar. Thus the mention of prophethood and the marks of the prophets ceased among all the nations by that saying, and they did not seek what they had lost, nor would they have believed had they sought. So the seducing devils gained power over the seduced and seduced them, the tyrants gained the power to kill the prophets and killed them, and doubt crept in from the evil jurists, who dressed it up finely, and were destroyed and destroyed others. All of this was so that the hearts of creation should not incline to the prophets when they appeared, nor seek them when they were lost. Despair of the prophets grew upon them, until the prophets called to the truth openly and were given the lie, and were slain among them without stirring their anger.

"Here is the nation of the Magi, clinging to its tyranny, worshipping its idols, keeping to its fires, saying by their own claim that God will send no messenger after their messenger. Here is the nation of the Torah, clinging to its scripture, bent over its calf, standing by the bounds of its prophets, humble before its Judge, saying by their claim that God will raise no messenger after Moses. Here is the nation of the Gospel, speaking by its likening, claiming that its prophet is its God, drawing near by its crosses, exalting its rabbis, saying that God will send no messenger after Jesus. And here is your community, walking the paths of those before it, seduced by what seduced them. It inherited the Book from other than its rightful people, walked with the religion outside its true way, followed its leaders in error, obeyed its masters in straying, and its jurists bowed before the tyrants, so that they set up for the people a religion without the established practice — performing the prayer at other than its time and paying the alms into other than its due. They are patient with these masters, persistent in their need of them, certain of their independence from all others, saying that God will send no messenger and no warner after their Messenger. So you and your like have followed the saying of the great multitude, dazzled by the abundance of consensus and the force of the sultans, and you accepted it from them, and sweetened it with the fairness of your schools, and adorned it with the fineness of your words, and by it you set up an argument of justice where no argument had been raised against you. So you followed those above you among the tyrants for the awe of them, and those below you followed you out of desire, all supposing it to be the truth.

"Now if this community must be believed in what it has decreed against God — that His proofs are cut off from His creation by a single strand of consensus — then the earlier nations have the better claim to be believed, by three qualities which oblige an argument for them against you, and in which they are more entitled to truth than you."

"And what are those qualities," asked Abu Malik, "in which they have merit over us, so that they must be believed if their word is true, or given the lie if their word is crooked?"

Salih said to him, "Their word is like your word, and your word is of the same kind as theirs. Your hearts resemble one another, and theirs is the merit over you in the argument. As for the first quality, they say they are earlier to the religion of God than you, so theirs is the merit of those who came first. The second is that the consensus, both theirs and yours, confirms what is with them, so theirs is the merit of the truthful. The third is your own testimony that their prophets are truthful and are the messengers of God, while none of them has borne witness for your prophet, neither to prophethood nor to truthfulness. So theirs is the merit over you by your agreement with them and their standing apart from you; and they say that the knowledge you hold is copied from their knowledge, and that theirs is the root of yours. This is their argument against you, clear in word and plain in justice. If the ruling of creatures is binding upon the Creator, then the ruling of these is the more binding. But if the Creator is the ruler over His creation, abrogating what He wills and confirming what He wills, with none to overturn His ruling, then by my life the saying of them all falls away, and the Creator has the first claim over creation and over the command. Every day He is engaged in some affair, and His act is not to be denied, even were He to send a warner every day."

"This community," said Abu Malik, "has folded itself upon a far-reaching error."

Salih said to him, "Most of those before them went astray also. They cast the friends of God out of their right and let the foolish overcome them in their affair, and they made lawful what God had forbidden — deliberately, so as not to resemble prophethood, lest people should long for the prophets and then not kill them. But they distracted them with kingship and largesse and led them to follow their whims. In this they were helped by men known outwardly for truthfulness and piety, who made a show of it before the people, until the very ones who had once commanded the community to good turned it from the good, and the ones who had forbidden it evil pointed it toward the evil. The people of faithfulness to the friends of God grew few, and truth and falsehood became alternating fortunes, like light and darkness in their difference. Were it not for the remnants of the friends of God and His just ones in the earth, and the inspiration that comes to them, the little of the truth would not prevail over the much of the falsehood. Otherwise, by what would God distinguish the corrupt from the good, except by His just ones? And how would the argument of God reach all creation, except through them?"

"There is no way," said Abu Malik, "and no escape for the justice of God and His command, amid the multitude of adversaries, except by the just ones of God. By them the argument of God to His creation is made sound."

Salih said to him, "If it is established that the company of the prophets of God are the just ones, is not each single one of them called a just one?"

"Yes," he said. "He is a just one, as it is said, so-and-so bore witness, and he is a just one among the just."

Salih said to him, "Then it has been established for you that the just one is the friend of God, and this has passed out of what you called merely a description, and become a thing described."

"Yes," he said, "this is sound as a saying."

Salih said to him, "And one who does not reach the thing described — does the description profit him?"

"The description does not profit him," he said, "except by reaching the thing described."

Salih pressed the point further. "Then whoever does not know the just one of Allah and His guardian — will his speech itself be just?"

At this the narrator tells us that Abu Malik smiled. "You have brought me out of my own chapter on justice and monotheism," he said, "and what has brought me out of it is nothing but the truth itself, argued in the clearest of proofs and spoken in the kindest of words. After this, what chapter is left for me to prove?"

"From here, O Abu Malik," Salih answered, "the camouflaged glass falls away from those who trade in the colors of jewels. They never recognized its worth; but once it is set before one who knows, the essentialists stand exposed, and those they deceived stand exposed with them."

Abu Malik said, "I have believed, and I have acted upon it. But how are we to be safe from the seducers of the nations, and how are we to know the causes by which Allah reaches His servants, or even one such cause among them, when we do not live in their time? In our age there are neither prophets nor messengers, as there were among the nations before us."

"Glory be to Allah, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "For a long stretch of your life you have kept affirming the justice of Allah. Yet the moment we press that justice upon you, in your own presence, where it becomes binding on you, you turn back. You begin to say that you would only be deceived about Allah, and you attribute injustice to Him."

"God forbid," Abu Malik protested. "I say no such thing. Whoever says it has transgressed and lied against Allah. Where have you found me committed to that?"

"From your own words," Salih replied. "You say the age of the prophets has passed, and that in our time there is neither prophet nor messenger. So tell me: when Allah sent those earlier ranks of messengers, was that sending a matter of His justice among His servants, or was it in vain?"

"May Allah forbid that it be anything but justice from Him," he said.

"Then what has shut us out of that justice?" Salih asked. "Are we not His servants as they were? They were created as we are created, and we are bound in His service as they were bound. Has the obligation been lifted from us after it was made binding upon them? Has His giving run dry in our time, so that we cling to Him only by what was granted to them? Has the Sunnah of Allah changed in our case after standing firm in those before us, so that we should now rule that God Himself has changed? Or is justice a duty owed to us and His judgment binding upon us, exactly as it was His way with those before us, so that there is no altering the words of Allah and none to overturn His decree? And who deserves the name just except the guardians of Allah? Justice is not found among any that Allah created except in the one He Himself chose, who became just by His command. That is why a judge is called just, for the sake of the One who appointed him, and a true word is called truthful because of the truthfulness of the one who utters it."

"It can only be as you say," Abu Malik admitted. "We are not permitted to say anything about Allah except the truth. Yet if we followed our own opinion in what we said, and judged by it in the absence of the rightly guided among us, we would have abandoned His judgment. This is part of Allah's ruling and His Sunnah with us, just as it was with those before us during the interregna between one messenger and the next. There was the period between the religion of Abraham and the time of Moses and the Torah, and the period between the Torah and the time of Jesus and the Gospel. We have stood upon that same footing, working through the interregnum, holding fast to the traditions and the Sunnah."

"The religion of Allah is far too precious to Him for that," Salih answered. "Was there truly any interregnum after the religion of Abraham, when Allah sent after him Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Jonah, and Shu'ayb, leading on to Moses? He inspired them to do good, to call to the religion of Abraham, to establish prayer according to its Sunnah and to give zakat according to its obligation, until at last the ruling of the Torah was sent down to Moses. And what interregnum stood between Moses and the Gospel, when after Moses Allah sent Joshua son of Nun, and Elias, Talut, David, Solomon, Zechariah, and John, leading on to Jesus? They upheld the ruling of the Torah, holding to its religion, calling others to it, reciting its Psalms, until the ruling of the Gospel came down to Jesus. So it was with those who passed before them and those who came after, and with the many generations between them whose full number none but Allah knows. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs; for each He set forth parables, and each of them called to the truth. Where, then, is this interregnum, and when did it ever occur? Allah said only, After a succession of messengers, and by that He affirmed the messengers themselves. What came after was the rising of the call once fear had settled in, so that they prayed in secret and concealment. Why then should you imagine they were ever cut off from the earth? The earth is never emptied of the justice of Allah for the blink of an eye. He is either openly present, or hidden and veiled in fear."

"This is a statement that cannot be refuted," Abu Malik said, "a settled word in which there is no doubt. As for the standing of Allah's argument through justice and the succession of the messengers, that is now established and made plain. So show me the meaning of their absence in our own time. What is its cause?"

"What was the cause of Moses' own absence," Salih asked in turn, "when he went out of Pharaoh's city and passed on in fear, watching and waiting until he reached the land of Shu'ayb?"

"He fled from Pharaoh's people, hiding himself from them," Abu Malik said, "because he feared for his life once they had agreed to kill him."

"Have you heard in this law of yours," Salih continued, "of any among the just, the just ones of Allah and the sons of His prophets, who was killed while patiently bearing the burdens of the religion?"

"Yes," he said. "Many of them have been killed."

"Then which of the just ones of Allah do you expect to appear to you and to the people of your age? One who was killed, whom Allah sends back to you from death? Or one who fled from you, whom you would drive back to the very killing he escaped, though he has followed Allah's own way in His prophets and messengers, patient under fear and in concealment, until Allah judges between him and us — and He is the best of judges?"

"There is no blame," Abu Malik said, "upon one who follows the Sunnah of the righteous by fearing for his life and fleeing from his people. The blame lies upon the wicked nations and the helpers of the powers of darkness, for killing the prophets of Allah and His just ones, and for their unanimous agreement upon that crime."

"And what do you say of the one who kills the just one of Allah and His proof?" Salih asked. "Where is his end, and where the end of the one who helped him?"

"Both are in the Fire," Abu Malik answered, "and so too is anyone who raised a banner for them or so much as carried an inkpot in their service."

"What do your jurists say about this?"

"They call for mercy upon the slain," he said, "yet they see no disobedience in the one who killed him."

"And whoever obeys the killer of his own accord," Salih pressed, "is he not a partner with him in the blood of the slain?"

"Yes, by Allah," said Abu Malik. "He is his partner, and even more wicked than he."

"His partnership is plain enough through the help he gave," Salih said. "But how has he become worse than the killer himself?"

"Were it not for the narrations of the jurists," Abu Malik answered, "the rule of the tyrants could never have been established through the foolish."

"You have spoken the truth, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "And now I lay this upon you yourself. You have accepted their kingdom as your home, their land as your resting place, their soldiers as your neighbors, and their enforcers as your intimate companions. You serve as their just man, judging on their behalf by your learning and settling your own needs through their power. You have prayed for them when you had a request, and thanked them for what you took from them. You set the crown of your justice upon their tyranny, and they grew proud. You clothed their falsehood in the robe of the sacred law, and they went to further excess. You lit up their falsehood with the idleness of your truth. So the weak were led astray by your conduct — and by every scholar like you — and they said, These are the jurists of the community; in them we have our example. They went astray through you, and you went astray through the tyrants, and you supposed that the one called to account was someone other than yourself. You forgot the words of Allah Almighty, And do not incline toward those who have done wrong, lest the Fire touch you. So you severed what Allah commanded to be joined, and you saw yourselves doing it. You spoke of the outward forms of justice while you were lost to its very path. You came upon His saying, Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you, and you made these powers of darkness the ones in authority over you, even when they had disbelieved. And you forgot His saying, Fight those of the disbelievers who are near to you. So you disobeyed the ones you were commanded to obey, and obeyed the ones you were commanded to fight, all the while imagining you were doing good. Your error ran on, and your opinion carried you so far, that at last you said Allah will send neither messenger nor warner in your age. Is it that the goodness of your deeds has freed you from any need of the guardians of Allah and their proof? Or that the foulness of your deeds has cut off your hope of Allah's accepting repentance? Or is it justice that you should pass judgment on God without justice, and be content on your Messenger's behalf with what none of you would accept for himself in his place? Would any one of you accept that his own son be killed, or that his house and his wealth be seized from him without right?"

"No one would accept that," Abu Malik said. "If a child of ours were killed, or a man among us driven from his property, he would rage, and his people would rage with him, and they would not rest until he was satisfied."

"Then Allah and His Messenger," said Salih, "have a greater right that you should be angry for their sake, when His vicegerent is killed and His guardian is driven out of the land."

"It is bitter to us indeed," Abu Malik replied, "that the guardian of Allah should be killed among us or driven from his land. But they have deliberately withdrawn themselves from us, and the tyrants have risen up over us in their place."

"Had you supported the friends of Allah, they would have appeared," Salih said. "Had you forsaken His enemies, they could never have prevailed. But your standing toward the guardians of Allah has fallen into three ranks. There is the assailant given power over them, who never tires of the hunt and is never satisfied even when he has struck. There is the one who looks on with malicious pleasure, biting his fingertips in spite, sparing no effort to incite others against them and never turning his gaze from them even in their heedlessness. And there is the one who hates what is done to them and holds in his heart a love for the guardians of Allah, yet forsakes them all the same, having no clear insight into faith and no trustworthiness in keeping a secret. For the sake of all this the guardians of Allah have withdrawn themselves from you by Taqiyya, out of caution before you and before this great mass of people who have taken their own desires as their god, fallen greedily upon the goods of this world, and joined together in the killing of the prophets. They were blind to the fault in what they did, so they never held themselves to account for the wrong they committed, and were never humbled into repentance for their deeds. Instead they turned the injustice of their own actions back upon Allah and ascribed it to Him, saying, Allah will send no messenger after these messengers, and no warner. So they made lawful the killing of the guardians of Allah without right. They wronged by their deeds, they turned aside by their words, and they overstepped by their denial."

"Yes," said Abu Malik, "this is the very description of this nation of ours, and this is exactly what they say. So where is the salvation from it, and where the escape from the punishment of Allah?"

"To Allah," Salih answered. "Seek refuge in Him, and He will grant you refuge. Repent to Him, and He will accept your repentance. Ask Him for the way of His guardians, and by your works He will deliver you from the deeds of those who did wrong. So it is with this mass of people as well: were they to repent to Allah, He would accept their repentance, and then what use would He have for punishing them? For Allah does not wrong them by the weight of an atom, even when they are unjust and lie, and He does not hasten their punishment, even when they hasten to disobey."

"Then I take refuge from the punishment of Allah," Abu Malik said, "by repenting to Him and by seeking the means to what pleases Him. Show your favor, then, to the one who seeks from you and asks to be guided. May your Lord reward you."

"If you do that," Salih said, "you will find Allah nearer to pardoning you than you are to your own repentance, for He loves those who turn to Him in repentance; and you will find me eager to guide you and watchful over you."

Then Salih's eyes filled with tears at the mention of repentance, and he broke off his speech. He gave them heart, and bade them go on to his father's house. He himself went in to his father, the scholar who had first sent him out, and consulted him about the affair of Abu Malik and his companions.

The scholar said, "You know your companions best. If you see good in them, guide them with your guidance. And when you warn them, do not let eagerness carry you into inflicting ta'zir upon them. Test them by leaving them to themselves for a while, without harshness, and instruct your elders to treat them well and act kindly toward them in due season. The one who truly desires good cannot stay hidden. Allah will not abandon what you leave behind you. He will open for you, from the light of His enabling grace, that by which the affairs of His creation are ordered, and from the gates of guidance that which will let you see the works of those rightly guided."

So Abu Malik and his companions remained in their trial, wavering, until their affair was settled and they came to know their own guidance, and the guardian of Allah turned toward them in their guidance with tenderness. They gave thanks to Allah for that, and returned to their people as warners. And through them Allah guided a company of His servants to His religion.

The author said: Whatever in this account took the shape of a story was no idle tale invented, but a confirmation of what Allah has commanded, for in it lies the steadfastness of the messengers, the signs of the guardians, and the manners of the seekers.

Praise be to Allah, first and last. May Allah bless His Messenger, our master Muhammad, sent forth from Him to His creation as a bearer of good news and a warner; and His guardian, the Imam of the God-fearing, the radiant leader of the bright-marked, the beloved of the Lord of the Worlds; and the Imams of the people of his house, upon whom Allah has bestowed His favor, from whom He has removed every impurity and whom He has purified completely. Allah is sufficient for us, and how excellent a Guardian; how excellent a Master and how excellent a Helper. There is no power and no strength except by Allah, the Most High, the Most Great.
