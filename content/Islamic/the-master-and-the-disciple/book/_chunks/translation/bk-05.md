The boy said: This world, in which this great creation was set, holds many people, both the first and the last, and they differ concerning it. Some have entrusted themselves to the world and pursued it, seeing no merit except in multiplying it. Others reproach it and practice asceticism within it, yet without dispensing with it entirely, even though what is forbidden of it is much while only a little is truly forbidden. I have heard from you no mention or report concerning it. What do you say of it?

The scholar said: As for this world, no one will ever truly reproach it, neither the scholar nor the ignorant. For the ignorant man knows its virtue by his very ignorance of what lies beyond it, and the scholar knows its virtue through the virtue of its meaning. No one ever reproaches this world except an ignorant man. And how can it be permissible for the creation to reproach the action of the Creator? Say rather that the world is set up, for the one who would see, upon seven things branching from seven origins: heaven, earth, darkness, light, vessels, prosperity, and misery. These are seven qualities, and from each of them branch seven further qualities.

The heavens are seven. The earths are seven.

The light has seven days that turn upon seven nights. Saturday is for slumber and rest. Sunday is for the limits and the sunna. Monday is for the mathānī, in the great meaning, al-Kabīr. Tuesday has three houses: the apparent, the inward, and the inward of the inward. Wednesday is for the four sacred ones. Thursday is the fifth of the causes of God. And Friday is the greatest law of God (nāmūs).

The darkness is the seven nights, a paradise for the seven days, so that each day has its night.

The vessels are seven: camels, horses, mules, donkeys, elephants, oxen, and ships. By these seven vessels the regions of the earth are traversed. Camels are for the sands, horses for the plains, mules for the mountains, donkeys for near destinations, elephants for marshes and wasteland, oxen for salt flats and thickets, and ships for the seas. So they are seven mounts for seven domains.

The blessings of this world rest upon seven: air, water, light, darkness, food, clothing, and marriage. Air is the space between sky and earth, in which the winds arise and the breaths pass back and forth, and it is the life of the animal. Water is the life of every living thing. Light is the substance of sight. Darkness is the increase of vegetation. Food is the sustenance of the body. Clothing is a shield that veils what is shameful. Marriage is the seed of reproduction and the cause of offspring. Some of these are the keys of the good, and some are the mines of blessings. Which of them is blameworthy, when all of them are from the gifts of God and His blessings, for which His servants are bound to give thanks?

Their opposites in misery are also seven: heat, cold, hunger, thirst, fear, sickness, and death. These are seven that must be borne with patience, once their meaning is known.

The boy said: These are the attributes of this world, and I do not doubt that anything of it is other than what the Creator has made and composed. So what do the scholars mean by censuring the world, and what do the ignorant mean by praising it? I see that the ignorant have arrived at the meaning, even in their ignorance. And if that is so, the rightness of their case would oblige the scholars to be in error, since the scholars contradict the people of ignorance in every sense.

The scholar said: The scholars did not err in censuring this world, but no creature knows what they mean by it. And the ignorant did not perceive the meaning of what they praised in the world, for they were ignorant of its meaning. Whoever is ignorant of a thing is hostile to it.

The boy said: Show me the truth of what the scholars say, so that I may rightly believe them. And distinguish the speech of the people of ignorance by what exposes their falsehood, since the outward appearance points the other way. My chest has grown weary of this ignorance, and I hope for no relief except through knowledge of it.

The scholar said: This world, and all that I have described to you of it, is an outward standing for an inward. It has no subsistence except through that inward, for the inward is to it as the soul is to the body. Whoever knows the soul, the body is made wholesome for him. Whoever does not know the soul and acknowledges only the body dwells upon a corpse, and the corpse is forbidden. From here the scholars forbade this world, because it is dead. They forbade the dead thing that God has forbidden, until one comes to it from where God wills. And the people of ignorance are shown to lie in their praise of this world, because they did not know its inner meaning, nor what God intended by it. They supposed that God created the world for some meaning other than its own, but God did not create things in vain. Had the world and all within it been created only for the world, He would have set in it a creation that fades away, having no consequence and no meaning. Yet every action without consequence is play, and every speech without meaning is falsehood, and God is far too exalted for that.

The boy said: The truthfulness of the scholars is established, their virtue and excellence are proven, and the people of ignorance are exposed in their falsehood, by what has appeared of the argument. There remains for us the knowledge of the inward of the world and what is in it. And I see that the distress I complained of to you has been lifted.

The scholar said: What is the reward of the one who brought you out of the distress of poverty into the breadth of ease?

He said: Thanks and praise.

The scholar said: And what is the reward of the one who brought you out of the narrowness of ignorance into the breadth of knowledge?

He said: Obedience and works.

The scholar said: This world and all its like are an outward standing for the hereafter and what is in it, for the hereafter is its soul and its life. Whoever labors in this world for the world, with no knowledge of the hereafter, his labor is misguidance, for it has neither meaning nor consequence. But whoever labors in this world for the hereafter, believing in it, his labor has meaning and consequence, and his labor is thanked.

The boy said: You have spoken truly. So make plain to me the features of this world and what is in it, and set the parables against the things they signify from within.

The scholar said: God, blessed and exalted, has not created anything among the things of this world, no beast upon the earth, no bird that flies with its wings, nothing moist and nothing dry among the fixed inanimate things such as mountains, stones, trees, and minerals of gold, silver, and gems, nor anything at all, great or small, except that it is a parable struck. Do you not see that the atom, for all its smallness and the subtlety of its meaning, has limits: before it and behind it, to its right and to its left, above it and below it? Those are six limits, and the seventh is itself. So too is everything God has created, from the ant and the mosquito up to what is above them of heaven and earth and all that lies between them, great and small: each has six limits, and the thing itself is the seventh. This is a proof pointing to the six origins from which things were created, while the air encompassing them is the seventh, master over them all. If your breast is wide enough for these questions and for looking into the parables, matching each thing with its counterpart among the inward pairs, then ask about whatever appears to you.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the heaven, in its height above things and the breadth of its regions, so that it has gathered all things within it?

The scholar said: Its likeness is the speaking Imam (al-Imām al-Nāṭiq), who is exalted above all creation by his virtue and the height of his degrees, and who has gathered within himself all the rulings, the rulings of the Shariʿa, by the breadth of his knowledge.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the constellations and the rest of the stars?

The scholar said: They are the likeness of the twelve chiefs (nuqabāʾ), the chiefs of the speaking Imam. And its stars are his summoners (duʿāt), who call to the good by the permission of their Lord.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the earth and its breadth, which was made a resting place and a bed for this creation?

The scholar said: It is the likeness of the gate (bāb) of the speaking Imam in his lifetime, and of his successor (waṣī) after his death. He is a recourse for the people and a place of settling for the one who seeks guidance, and he is a knower of the parables and of the inner interpretation (taʾwīl).

The boy said: What is the likeness of the twelve islands in the earth?

The scholar said: They are the likenesses of the twelve arguments (ḥujaj), the arguments of the successors.

The boy said: What is the likeness of its rivers and its springs?

The scholar said: The likeness of those who call to the good (duʿāt).

The boy said: What is the likeness of the seven kingdoms of the heavens and their orbits?

The scholar said: Those are causes between God and the speakers (nuṭaqāʾ), and they belong to Him. They are the seven and the two orbits, and those are the greatest causes of God.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the seven seas?

The scholar said: They are causes between God and the imams of the speaker, beneath those first causes.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the salt water and the fresh water, and their difference upon the earth?

The scholar said: The salt water is the likeness of the knowledge of the outward, in which there is no summons (daʿwa) and no summoners, just as salt water yields neither crop nor anything else. The fresh water is the likeness of the knowledge of the inward, whether as springs within the earth or as water flowing over the earth. So too is knowledge: the outward, by which the summoners run their course, and the inward, which is kept hidden.

The boy said: How did water come to be a likeness of knowledge?

The scholar said: Because water is the life of every living thing, and likewise knowledge is the life of every knower.

The boy said: What is the likeness of the air that surrounds all things?

The scholar said: The air is too great to be set as a likeness for any thing it might signify.

The boy said: Is it greater, or are the heaven and the earth as great as it, so that they too are likenesses?

The scholar said: Glory be to God, rather it is greater than both. The breadth of the earth within the atmosphere is like a signet ring in an open desert. The breadth of the atmosphere within the sky is like a signet ring in an open desert. And the breadth of the sky and its regions within the breadth of the air is like an egg in an open desert. What thing to be signified could comprehend this, so as to be a proof and a likeness of it?

The boy said: Then what is its meaning?

The scholar said: It is a proof of the One who has nothing like Him, glory be to Him and exalted above what they associate with Him.

The boy said: But did not the Creator say, "فَلَا تَضْرِبُوا لِلَّهِ الْأَمْثَالَ" (So do not strike parables for God)?

The scholar said: And did He not also say, "وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى" (And to God belongs the highest similitude)?

The boy said: Glory be to Him who has set enigmas in His Book so that one may come to it by its own door, and you are one of its gates. Make plain to us how this is.

The scholar said: Everything has a meaning, and the meaning of the parables is this: the outward is evidence of the inward. He, blessed and exalted, has forbidden that He be measured against anything of His creation, so that one should say, "This is the likeness of God," as one says, "This is the likeness of the Imam," or "This is the likeness of the ḥujja," or "This is the likeness of the dāʿī." Their likenesses became signs pointing to them and to their ranks. So it is not fitting, in His greatness, that the Creator be measured against anything of His creation by any word or deed, and it is not said, "This is the likeness of God." Rather, the parables are taken according to their meanings, for their meanings carry signifiers: the small among them points to the great, the great points to the highest meanings, and the highest points to the Most High, the Highest. Therefore He said, "وَلِلَّهِ الْمَثَلُ الْأَعْلَى" (And to God belongs the highest similitude), meaning the highest proof, for there is no proof of anything higher than the proof of God. And that is the air: highest above the heaven and the earth, encompassing all things while nothing among them encompasses it. It is the highest similitude, and it is the proof of the One who has nothing like Him. He did not say that there is nothing like Him, for indeed there is nothing like Him, but rather He meant that no proof of Him is like other proofs. To Him belongs the highest similitude, and there is no proof like His proof among the proofs of any other, of those of His creation whom we have described. So blessed be God, Lord of the mighty Throne.

The boy said: The meaning has grown vast and the reach has widened. Was there, before the air, some thing between it and God, given the greatness of what you have described in it?

The scholar said: Yes.

The boy said: What is it?

The scholar said: The time.

The boy said: And what is the time?

The scholar said: It is the time of the will by which He created what He willed and commanded what He wished.

The boy said: You have plunged me into the depths of the seas of the highest assembly. Return me to the sciences of this world and their likenesses, that I may seek help through them in my affair and be made strong by them to bear what I am meant to bear. For I have feared for myself and grieved for my mind, lest I be led astray into what I cannot fathom.

The scholar said: You rose high by your question, and so the answer rose with you to the highest of the outward meanings, and there your opinion was lost and your mind bewildered. How then would it be if I disclosed to you its inward? You and I would be as Moses and al-Khiḍr, the Knowing One.

The boy said: Does this inward have an inward more inward still?

The scholar said: By my life, the inward has an inward that is loftier in station, wider in power, and more complete in guidance, and it is the utmost of proofs to the road of salvation.

The boy said: Then I see here three layers of knowledge: the outward, the inward, and the inward of the inward. Is there a proof of this?

The scholar said: And where is the proof, if not for this and for that to which it points?

The boy said: Then show me a likeness of these three layers.

The scholar said: I will show you from the Book of God, exalted is He, and from the argument of the intellect, and from what the whole body of the parables points to.

The boy said: I did not think there was any teaching beyond what I had already reached. Truly the affair of God is far greater than what the imaginings of the pretenders can rise to.

The scholar said: As for the Book of God, exalted is He, His word: "يَا بَنِي آدَمَ قَدْ أَنْزَلْنَا عَلَيْكُمْ لِبَاسًا يُوَارِي سَوْآتِكُمْ وَرِيشًا وَلِبَاسُ التَّقْوَى ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ" (O children of Adam, We have sent down upon you a garment that covers your shameful parts, and adornment, and the garment of godliness, that is better). This is three: the outward, the inward, and the inward of the inward.

Al-Ghulam said: You have spoken truly.

The scholar said: As for the argument of the intellect: a thing is known only by a name, an attribute, or a meaning. The meaning is what the thing is in itself, the name is what it is called, and the attribute is what points to it. Likewise the religion of God has an outward name and an inward proof pointing to it, and it stands only by the two together. Nothing is pointed to except by a noun or an attribute, as in the saying of one who says, "The moon has risen." The meaning is the disk, "moon" is its name, and "has risen" is its attribute. Were he to say "has risen" and then fall silent, it would carry no meaning. And were he to say "moon" and then fall silent, it would carry no meaning, until, joining the name and the attribute, he says, "The moon has risen," and then the meaning is made plain and the speech is sound. Just so, if you say "outward," it calls for an answer, that one ask, "Outward of what?" And if you say "inward," it calls for an answer, that one ask, "Inward of what?" But when you say, "the outward of the religion and its inward," the speech is understood, and it becomes a religion having an outward and an inward, just like the thing that has a name and an attribute, as He said: "وَذَرُوا ظَاهِرَ الْإِثْمِ وَبَاطِنَهُ" (And forsake the outward of sin and its inward). So sin became a thing having an outward and an inward. Such too is the greater religion of God, whose laws are called its outward and whose inward is its inward, and it is subsisting in itself. To the knowledge of its meaning there are many parables that point, such as the hidden egg, which has an outward that shields it, an inward that is the white within, and an inward of its inward, the yellow yolk, which is its essence and its meaning.

The boy said: Are not the world and the hereafter a pair, the soul and the body a pair, and the knowledge of the outward and the inward a pair, and are not the pairs of this kind, as you have described, many?

The scholar said: You have spoken truly. But they are pairs, so of what are they the likeness?

The boy said: They are pairs that point to the religion of God.

The scholar said: You have spoken truly. The pair is an outward name and an inward attribute, pointing to the knowledge of God and to the religion of God, and that is the inward of the inward. Likewise the creation was created in three stations. The first is the creation of the angels, the second the creation of human beings, and the third the creation of the beasts. The outward of knowledge is the limit of the beasts, and whoever knows only that, with no inward, is at the level of the beasts. The knowledge of the inward is the knowledge of the children of Adam and their limit, and whoever knows it is a believer and is at the level of human beings. And the knowledge of the inward of the inward is the knowledge of the angels. Whoever knows it is spiritual in knowledge and bodily in frame, a prophet sent, whom God appoints as His deputy in His earth and by whom He establishes the argument over His creation. He is the veil of the angels, the cipher of revelation and its interpreter to the children of Adam. With him are the keys of the gardens, which none enters except the one who obeys him, and with him is the passage from the Fire, which none enters except the one who disobeys him. And the people are but two men: a divine scholar, and one who learns.
