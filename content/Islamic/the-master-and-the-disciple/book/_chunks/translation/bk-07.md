The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has guided us to His religion, for we could not have been guided except by His favor. Tell me about the beginning of this creation and its composition. Is it a single creation or many, seeing how much difference there is among people in their opinions and their passions?"

The scholar said: "As for the creation of bodies, it is various, as you see in the variety of tongues and colors. This is itself a proof of God's power, made plain through His signs, so that people may recognize one another: the child knows his father and the father his son, the man knows his wife and no other, and the woman knows her husband and no other. So did God create them in His justice. But as for the creation of the instrument, that is, the senses, both what is visible of them and what is hidden within, it is a single creation, and what is apparent will tell you the truth of what is concealed. Have you ever seen a learned man given three eyes as an increase in him, or an ignorant man left with a single eye as a deficiency in him, so that one might say the scholar sees by his surplus and the ignorant fails to see by his lack? Have you seen any part of the outward instrument given to one man over another? You see their creation as one level creation in the outward instrument, and the meaning of the composition is the same in all. From this we have come to know that the inner senses follow the same pattern of justice. God would not be just in what is apparent and unjust in what is hidden. He did not fashion for the scholar two hearts in one breast, nor did He deprive the ignorant of anything. The instrument is one, and the composition belongs to the One who gathered it. By this very instrument, which He made for them and which they failed to use, God has raised His argument against them. The Almighty said: 'They have hearts wherewith they do not understand, and they have eyes wherewith they do not see, and they have ears wherewith they do not hear.' Do you suppose, then, that God in His bounty and generosity charged them with what they were unable to bear? No. He is far fairer than that."

The boy said: "I do not see it otherwise, nor would I say otherwise. But I ask you about the difference of passions. From where did it come, when the instrument is whole and the origin of the composition is one?"

The scholar said: "As for the origin, it is one. All of them descend from Adam, and Adam is from dust, created by his Lord, who breathed into him of His spirit. But they departed from the Sunna in their marriages. They were born upon a basis other than the true faith and grew up in other than guidance. I do not doubt that among them are children of unlawful union who claim as fathers men who are not their fathers. So when the roots were corrupted, the branches grew corrupt and distorted, and their return to the truth became hard, weighed down as they were by arrogance against obedience, sloth in worship, and envy of the friends of God. Thus they did not keep within the bounds of the lawful, holding back from the forbidden."

The boy said: "If the origin is one and the instrument is one, from where, then, does the difference of passions arise, when the meaning of their seeking is also one, since everyone seeks the good for himself?"

The scholar said: "By my life, the meaning of their seeking is indeed one, but they differ in striking the mark, like archers aiming at a target. They are agreed in shooting at it with resolve and with the bow, yet for all that they differ in whether they hit it. So too the hearts within the breasts are gathered upon the minds, and the minds are gathered upon the safeguarding of the bodies and the securing of their dignity. But when the call of truth appeared, they parted ways. Some held that answering the Messengers and humbling themselves before them was itself a part of one's dignity and a guarding of the self against folly. Others held that denying the Messengers and raising oneself above obedience to them was a part of one's dignity and a guarding of the self against the narrowness of worship. The root in both was the same: the seeking of ease for the body and of honor for the soul. So one group chose what the Messengers had promised them of the reward of God, and though in this world that reward was rare and costly, they chose it for its permanence. The others chose the ease of this world, and though it was perishing, they chose it for its presentness, the ease of asking for it, and the swiftness of its reward. The very hearts by which the first sought the bliss of the Hereafter were the same in kind as the hearts by which the second sought the bliss of this world. Each man looks only to himself, and each supposes himself his own true counselor. So they came together in the seeking and differed in the striking. This is the justice of God, His generosity, and the openness of His giving: to every seeker is given what he sought. Whoever desires the reward of this world is given from it, and whoever desires the reward of the Hereafter is given from it."

The boy said: "But the people of this world are ranked in classes within it. Some are rich beyond the need to labor, kings by their abundance of wealth, and some are poor and in need of wealth, serving the rich to gain from them. Then the bliss of this world gathers them all upon one footing. Are they all alike in the evil of the end, or is there a difference between one and another?"

The scholar said: "It is a part of God's justice that He joins them one to another and does not cut them off from their companions and those they loved. They are equal in His punishment, just as they were joined together in disobeying Him."

The boy said: "And what of this religion, which God has honored and perfected, which He has accepted as a religion for Himself and made binding upon His servants? Will He accept a part of it from them without the rest, so that they fall into classes as the people of this world do, or will He accept it only from one who brings it whole?"

The scholar said: "If the people of this world are ranked in classes within it, then the people of religion are ranked in degrees within their religion. Justice requires that the Creator be worshipped to the full measure of His will, for great is His riches and great is their need of Him. Yet His mercy widened toward them. He knew that among them were the weak, so He accepted from them whatever they could manage of sincere striving in work and of effort spent in obedience, whether little or much. Then He charged each soul according to its capacity and its labor, and gave to every possessor of merit his due merit, so that they stand in degrees before their Lord. Then, by His justice, He made the outcome of their obedience to be this: that they be joined to the people of complete religion and excellent works among His servants. The Almighty said: 'And whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger, those will be with the ones upon whom Allah has bestowed favor: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous. What excellent companions they are. That is the bounty from Allah.' This they attain by the effort they poured into obedience, not because their deeds equal the deeds of the righteous, but as a mercy from God toward them, even though they are weak and their works are slight."

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has done this for them. Is it to be feared, then, that anyone should be punished for anything other than disobedience, even when his work is little?"

The scholar said: "God is more just than that."

The boy said: "Then is it to be hoped that anyone might enter Paradise without works at all, such as the insane and small children?"

The scholar said: "That falls within the grace of God, His generosity, and the breadth of His mercy."

The boy said: "What, then, is the limit of striving in obedience and the measure of sincere work, so that God is bound to accept a man's excuse in all that lies beyond it?"

The scholar said: "The full reach of his knowledge and the utmost of his strength."

The boy said: "Then describe for me the duty of knowledge and the duty of action, and the goal of each, that I may test myself against them. If I find I can reach the goal in seeking my Lord's pleasure, I will press on. If not, I will halt at the limit of my strength, and God is the first to excuse me for what I cannot do. I place my trust in the justice of God and feel safe from any wrong on His part, but I fear the evil within my own soul."

The scholar said: "Do not feel fear where you have come to feel safe, and do not feel safe where you ought still to fear. Strive for God with your person and with your wealth. For God does not accept what He hates, nor does He hate what pleases Him. He has hated unbelief for His creation and is pleased when His servants give thanks and act with justice in what He has decreed, so He accepts nothing else. Truthfulness and faithfulness are the two gardens of His Paradise, and none is saved with Him except the people of truthfulness and faithfulness."

The boy said: "Then make plain to me the goal of the work within my own self, and what God has made binding upon me in it."

The scholar said: "Obedience to God ennobles those who give it, even when their origins are humble, and good work is the crown of those who do it. As for what is binding upon you in your own self, it is to perform every obligation and to shun every forbidden thing, for this is the very substance of your religion and the purity of your body. And the whole of what makes God pleased with you and brings His acceptance is this: that you extend to others what you would love to have extended to you, and that you withhold from others what you would love to have withheld from yourself. In this lies justice, and God is pleased with justice."

The boy said: "Yes, this is the binding truth, and whoever does not act by it has no share with God. Is there, then, any allowance beyond what you have mentioned, in a time of dire need?"

The scholar said: "God has opened that door for His servants, out of His tenderness and mercy. He said: 'Whoever is compelled, without willful transgression, there is no sin upon him.'"

The boy said: "Praise be to God, who has not laid upon His servants any hardship in religion. I am resolved to hold fast to what He has commanded, and I seek His help in doing as He has ordered me. But what is my duty regarding my wealth, which you joined to my own person when you said that I must strive with my person and my wealth?"

The scholar said: "As for your wealth, it is all that God has granted you of every kind of gain. Take it only from what is lawful, and spend it only in its rightful place."

The boy said: "And what is the lawful source from which it is taken, and what is the rightful place in which it is spent?"

The scholar said: "Its lawful source is honest earning in which you have wronged no one and which you have not tainted with falsehood. As for the rightful claim upon it, that is its zakat: look at all that has come into your hands, whether little or much, and take out a fifth of the finest of it, the portion you yourself would most love to receive, and give it to those whose claim upon you is binding. In this way your wealth is purified by charity, just as your body is purified by work. Work is a shield, and zakat is a cleansing, and whoever gives the zakat has truly prospered."

The boy said: "I will do so, and I repent to God for what has passed. Then what am I to do with the remainder of the wealth?"

The scholar said: "You shall spend upon yourself and your family in a fitting manner. If your surplus still exceeds your need, share it with the poor among your brethren and extend your kindness to everyone who turns to you in want, for the one who has no bounty of his own is in need of another's bounty toward him. So be swift to every good and foremost in every act of generosity, for this is the very goal of the work, if only you can be patient in it."

The boy said: "Yes, patience, and then more patience. How could a man in great need fail to be patient toward one who has no need of his service? Every needy person is patient, and every one who has been favored gives thanks."

The scholar, delighted by the boy's words, said: "Praise be to God, who has guided you to speak rightly. I ask Him to help you carry the work through to completion, for that is the perfection of virtue."

The boy said: "I had long been asking another man's hand for the credit of a favor I ought to have done for myself. Now that the credit for it has come to rest with me, will I deny it to my own soul? I can find no one who would cheat my soul more than I would if I did. So here is the whole of my wealth before you, in five shares. One share is its zakat. One share is the expiation for what I have squandered. One share is thanksgiving to God for what I have come to know. One share is for the poor among my brethren. And one share is for you to accept by your grace, that it may become provision for you. Take all of it to yourself, and God will send its like in its place."

The narrator said: The scholar's heart was moved for the boy when he saw how the jewel within him had come clear, how the ordeal had drawn out a praiseworthy result, how his merit had shone and his work had been purified.

The scholar said, while his tears ran down: "O my son, for the sake of one like you let the scholars travel the long road; for the sake of one like you let the earth stand firm and the heavens hold steady; for the sake of one like you let the bonds of kinship be made plain, and let the rain come down from the openings of the clouds. So rejoice, my son, in what God has given you and in the breast He has opened to Islam. Receive the glad tidings of a light from your Lord and a nearby opening, and accept God's gift to you and the benefits of His grace upon you with abundant work and noble thanks. As for my share of this wealth, I have no need of it. I have come out from wealth, and it is the temptation of wealth that I fled. It would not be right, my son, that you should turn me back to the very thing I have come to detest. As for the share of your brethren, you shall keep it for them, so that when they ask you for something you may hand it to them by your own hand, that they may know your kindness and thank God for your goodness. As for the share of expiation, hold it with you until you come to know your sin. As for the share of thanksgiving, it is a trust you hold for God until you reach your maturity and rise to the highest of what you have come to know. And as for the share of zakat, it too is a trust for the one to whom it is owed, held in your keeping. Take all of it to yourself until you meet him, for he is the one who judges for you and against you."

The boy said: "And who is this generous one over you, whose judgment you do not overstep and whose command you do not transgress?"

The scholar said: "He is the one whose right God has made binding upon me and upon every believer, the one in whose hand are the keys of the gardens and the landmarks of the kingdom, the one whose palms are opened

The scholar spoke on of the great one to whom he would bring him: "With the light of the Mount, he is the cause behind the signs, the one whose knowledge has raised him to the loftiest of ends. Through him your light is completed, through him Allah perfects your affair, and at his hand the blessings come to you."

The boy said, "Such kindness as this, to whom does it belong, and who are you yourself?"

The scholar said, "I am his son and one of his servants in good works, seeking his pleasure and shrinking from his displeasure."

The boy said, "I think myself far from his good pleasure, yet near enough that, had I never known of him, I would have an excuse. Now that I know, is any excuse left to me?"

The scholar said, "An excuse is granted only before one is brought within reach of such knowledge."

The boy said, "Shall I speak, or shall I hold back?"

The scholar said, "Say what seems best to you."

The boy said, "Then should you not, at this very moment, take me by the hand to the gate of remission until I enter, and to the house of light until I draw from it? This is part of the full measure of truthfulness that is binding upon you."

The scholar said, "That is indeed your due, but only with his permission and by his decision."

Then the scholar took his leave of the boy and departed. The boy wished to furnish him with provision for the road, but the scholar was loath to accept it. He went on until he came to his elder father, and described the boy to him, and the father recognized the mark upon him and knew it for the mark of the people of goodness and the mines of virtue, and he marveled at it.

He said, "Bring this boy to me quickly, for I hope he will be a door of mercy that Allah has opened for the people of his age."

The scholar tarried only a little before returning to the boy, and he found him altered in body from the state he had known.

The scholar said, "What is this I see, so unlike the state in which I left you?"

The boy said, "And what have you found changed in me, may my father and mother be your ransom?"

The scholar said, "Weakness of body and a yellowness of color."

The boy said, "That is from a desolation that came over me after you left."

The scholar said, "And what is this desolation, and what is the cause of this weakness?"

The boy said, "Your absence made me desolate, and dread of what might befall me before I met you again made me weak."

The scholar said, "Allah has known what is in your heart, and so He has made the trial light upon you and has cast mercy for you into the heart of the one who has charge of you. So take up your provisions for the journey, for I will carry you to the fullness of your hope and bring you to the door of your goal. And there is no power except by Allah."

At this the boy was so overcome with joy and awe that his soul all but parted from his body, until Allah, knowing what was in him, took hold of it and returned it to him. When he revived he said, "Praise be to Allah, who has never ceased to be merciful to the believers."

Then he gave abundant thanks to his father and bent to his hands to kiss them, and to his feet. He rose in haste, took up the gear of travel, and the two set out until they reached the greater scholar. They asked leave and were admitted; they entered, greeted him, and sat by his leave. When the assembly had settled around them, the boy said to his teacher, "Teach me how to speak and how to ask for my need."

The teacher said to him, "It is not lawful for me to teach you anything while you are in this place, out of reverence for its master. You stand before one who knows your need and knows what you intend."

So the boy held his peace, and the two remained with the scholar a while, until his affair was completed and his guidance perfected, until he reached the utmost of his hope and knew what was for him and what was against him. Then they bade farewell and went out.

The two withdrew together, unseen. They had spoken nothing in the assembly but the words of peace and of glad greeting, and the boy had turned pale from the intensity of his awe and could not speak. The master, the lord of the house, gave orders that the two be treated with honor and that whatever they needed be provided; and he went on with them to his own house. When he had brought them in and greeted them and shown his gladness at their coming, he said to the scholar, who was his friend, and between whom there was brotherhood and an old acquaintance, "My brother, is this the boy of whom we used to hear?"

He said, "Yes. This is the one whose report has grown sweet in the hearts, so that his remembrance is upon the tongues of the believers."

The boy said, "Has the tidings of me truly reached the friends of Allah, so that they recount my mention in their gatherings?"

The scholar said, "Yes, my brother. So you are."

The boy said, "Favors have surrounded me from every side, and bounties have come upon me from every path. I do not know whom to thank, nor whom to repay."

Then they all sat and talked through their night. When morning came they sought leave to enter upon the Shaykh. When they were admitted and had completed their greeting, the Shaykh gave them leave to sit, and they sat; and the Shaykh took up his speech.

He said: "Praise be to Allah, who has opened the sealed hearts by His light and unlocked by His justice what was closed to the seeker; who has made remembrance easy by His gentle making, as a kindness to His servants and a mercy from Him to those who long for Him. He has not charged His creation with the full reality of what is owed to Him, nor has He been content that they deny His blessings; rather He has charged them, out of His justice, with what they are not too weak to bear, so that the matter is set in balance between two extremes, and is therefore named justice. He has spread out His hands with generosity, in kindness and in bounty, disappointing none who call upon Him and denying none who keep silent. His blessings pour down and His favors are unstinting, neither reserved for the one who gives thanks, for the greatness of His reward, nor withheld from the one who is ungrateful, for the greatness of His punishment. He has kept His blessings too noble for any stinting, and made His proofs too sure to be denied. He is too encompassing to be contained, too evident in His signs to be rejected. He is found yet not bounded, known yet not described. Glory be to Him: how mighty, and how gentle to His creation; how compelling, and how merciful to His servants; how mighty in power, and how just in His ruling; how exalted, and how near to His people; how near, and how far above all imaginings. So worship Him and give thanks to Him; to Him you shall return."

Then he said: "As for what follows: it is part of the honor of minds that they be put to work in seeking; and among the fruits of seeking is the finding of what truly is; and among the marks of that which truly is, is its sweetness. Every sweet and flowing spring is a drink whose surface is there to be drunk and whose depth is veiled; the one who seeks it never wearies of thought, and creation never reaches the end of it. Its landmarks are a right, and the right of the Real is a duty, and from this it is called a right, for the establishing of its obligation. Whoever denies a right its due is a wrongdoer. Is it not among the rights of thought that the hearts pay their alms through good manners; and among the rights of good manners, the seeking of knowledge; and among the rights of knowledge, that it be acted upon; and among the rights of acting upon it, that it be made pure through obedience to those in authority; and among the rights of obedience, that it be perfected in hardship and in ease alike, in good fortune and in trial the same. And none is granted this save those who are patient, and none is granted it save one of a great share."

Then he turned his face toward the boy and said, "O young man, you are honored as a dear friend newly come, and greeted as a guest with a purpose. What is your name?"

He said, "Ubayd Allah, son of Abd Allah."

The Shaykh said, "That is your description, and word of you has already come to us."

The boy said, "I am a free man, son of Abd Allah."

The Shaykh said, "And who freed you from ownership, that you became free?"

The boy said, "This scholar freed me," and he pointed with his hand to the scholar who had brought him.

The Shaykh said, "Tell me: if this man is himself owned and possesses nothing, is his freeing of you valid for you?"

The boy said, "It is not valid."

The Shaykh said, "Then what is your name?"

At this the boy fell silent, at a loss for an answer.

The Shaykh said, "O young man, how is a thing to be known that has no name?"

The boy said, "Then I am newborn to you; so give me a name."

The Shaykh said, "That waits upon the completing of seven days."

The boy said, "And why is it deferred to the completing of seven days?"

The Shaykh said, "For the honoring of the newborn."

The boy said, "And if the newborn should die before the seven days are complete?"

The Shaykh said, "Nothing harms him; he is named even after that."

The boy said, "And this name by which you would name me, is it mine to own?"

The Shaykh said, "Then you would be one who is worshipped, though you were but newly born?"

The boy said, "How, then, is it to be spoken of?"

The Shaykh said, "The name is master over you, and you are owned by the name. So do not press beyond your bound, and withdraw until your term is complete."

At this the boy rose in haste in the beauty of his obedience, and did not answer back, in the beauty of his manners. He went out, and his father went out with him, until they returned to their lodging. The lord of the house greeted them and said, "How fares this brother of ours in his need?"

The scholar said to him, "A promise has been given; the one who was promised is thirsting, and the term is near."

So they waited until the seventh day, then sought leave of the Shaykh. When he had granted them leave, he ordered the boy to wash and to put on his cleanest garments. As the boy set about his preparation, his heart sensed that his need would be granted on that day, and that this preparation he had been ordered to make pointed to a good that would come after it. When he had done what he was ordered and the two went out together, the boy went out saying, "A day of good fortune, and a patron of good fortune." He repeated it again and again, and nothing moved him to this but the fullness of his joy and the keenness of his longing to know what would bring him nearer to the pleasure of Allah. When the two came in upon the Shaykh, they found that he had taken up the implements of his work. He greeted them with peace, and they returned his greeting.

Then he bade the boy draw near to him, and the boy drew near; and he brought him close, and he came close. And he took up speech such as no fancy has ever encompassed, that no pen has ever traced, that has never crossed the heart of any human being; speech that has no place in the schools of the preachers and is not made plain in the compendiums of books, for the greatness of its worth, and is unveiled to none but its own people. And when he had discharged what was due and brought him out of the wildernesses of bewilderment that had led him astray, to the very end of guidance, his path was made pure and his draught made sweet, and he came to know his Lord. So he released his ihram by magnifying the sanctities of Allah, held fast to the sure rope, circled the Ancient House, spent his rites in remembrance, and completed his Hajj by the greatest sign.

Then he and his father continued to come and go to the Shaykh, studying what he had given, the boy patient in his perseverance, never wearying of the question for its nobility, never sated with the answer for its sweetness. So they remained in that state for a long while, until it was known that the root had drunk deep and the branch had risen high.

Then he said to the Shaykh, in thanks to Allah and to him: "Praise be to Allah, who has made you worthy of this station, and revived through you a faith and put to death through you an ignorance, and made you a place of return for the people and a sanctuary. You are the gate of the people of heaven and the ascent of the people of earth. From the light of your might the lights appeared, and from the pouring of your palm the rivers ran. May you remain so, until the one who is pleased with you gives you his pleasure."

The Shaykh said: "As for you, may Allah bless you in what He has given you, and aid you by His enabling grace to guard what has come to you, and set you upright in His will, and smooth your way by the light of His guidance, and open your breast with the keys of wisdom, and ennoble your affair with the path of the call, and perfect your merit with the confirmations of answered prayer, and bring you, in obedience to Him, to the best of your hope of His good pleasure. And I now take my leave of you, my son, and give you leave to depart. Hard it is upon me to part from you, but three things have driven us to it. The first is that we are bound on a journey to another land. The second is your long separation from your family, together with the burden you took on in going out without your father's permission, for in him there is a heavy grief and a great anger that nothing will lift but your return, though I hold out hope for your father, if you are gentle with him. And the third is the duty owed for the right of this grace that has reached you, for it is binding upon you to live by it and to call others to it."

The boy said: "Your parting is a sorrow to me, and your loss a lack in me; but your counsel is more rightly guiding, and your obedience more binding, and your judgment more sound. So enjoin upon me what you see fit, and dismiss me when you will, and be gracious to me by sending my father with me to my land, that I may lean upon him in my work and entrust all my affairs to him."

The Shaykh said: "That I shall do. And I enjoin upon you the fear of Allah who created you, and the keeping of the trust for which you have struggled, and the safeguarding of your father, who raised you when you were small and toiled long in your affair, with his own person and with his wealth. Do not clothe him in the garments of disdain, nor set him upon the vessels of ruin; do not blot out his name, nor turn your speech away from his mention, nor deal falsely with him in your city; and in the beginning there is room enough for you."

The boy said: "May Allah reward you, as a lord of grace, on behalf of every grateful one, with good; and may the Lord of bounty repay you for your shepherding, when hands are too weak to repay you. I will keep your commandment. So do not cut off my share from the blessing of your supplication."

Then the two rose and clasped hands and embraced, and each bade the other farewell, unable to master his tears and unable to speak but by signs. Then they parted, and the scholar and the boy journeyed on until they drew near to the boy's city, in which his father was.

The scholar said to the boy: "My son, you have learned the counsel of the Shaykh, and there is no right guidance but in his word. This is your city, and we have reached its edge; so sit with me apart from the road, for I wish to remind you of something of my own affair and to enjoin upon you what you should do."

When the two had sat, the scholar said: "My son, you know the state of your father and his enmity toward the people of this matter, and this has been compounded upon him by your going out with me and your absence from him without his leave or his assurance. I mean to carry out my own father's counsel by keeping myself hidden in some part of this wilderness, while you return to your father, that his soul may be soothed and his fury toward us may subside. And I hope, if you are gentle with him, that he will be nearer to what you desire than any other. The absence has alarmed him, and the solitude has distressed him."

When the time came to part, the two of them rose, shook hands, and embraced, and each gave his farewell to his friend. Neither could contain himself for the weight of the parting, and neither could speak except by gesture. Then they took their separate ways.

So the scholar and the boy went out together, and when they had drawn near to the boy's city, the city in which his father lived, the scholar said to him: "O my son, I have understood the will of the Shaykh, and you have not, for guidance lies only in his words. This is your city, and we have reached its edge. Sit with me here, apart from the road, for I wish to remind you of some of my concerns and to counsel you in what you should do."

When they had sat down, the scholar said: "O my son, I know your father and his enmity toward the people of this matter. To that has now been added your going out with me and your absence from him without his leave or his trust. I wish to honor your father's view by keeping some of these affairs concealed, so that you may return to him, and his heart may be set at ease, and he may remain among us. And I hope, if you are gentle with him, that he will be nearer to what you desire than anyone else, for your absence has worn him down and his solitude has come to frighten him. Know, my son, that Allah has opened to you the least of your striving, a thing I did not think you would reach until a long stretch of time had passed and you supposed yourself a grown man. Allah has been good to you, so be good to yourself, and honor the one through whom Allah has honored you. We have thought well of you, and our hope in you has been great. Hold fast, then, to the trust your father entrusted to you, and be firm in your affair. Piety and watchful devotion are the guardians of your religion and your work; thanksgiving and patience are an increase to your light. Ask of Allah the opening of your provision and the goodness of His success granted to you."

Then the scholar said: "I commend to you six qualities: that you rely upon Allah when you are resolved; that you speak with a ruling when you are called upon; that you do not grow angry when you are wronged; that you do not weary when you are slandered; that you bear patiently with everyone who answers you toward the good; and that you take for yourself the best of that to which you call others. For Allah will help you and open the way before you."

The boy said: "May Allah reward you with three goods: an imam whose argument you have upheld, a proof of God whose trust you have counseled, and a dead man whose life you have graced. My heart is filled and my breast is brimming with light and gladness at the greatness of what you have set forth. I do not know which of your words is the finer: the outward, which you arranged in its order, or the inward, which you unfolded in its meanings."

The scholar said: "Praise be to Allah, who has made His way easy for you, brought its far distance near, and helped you to bear its weight. I now take my leave of you and turn away. Is there any need?"

The boy said: "My needs rest upon three: a need already fulfilled, a need for which I hope, and a need I ask you to grant me as a favor."

The scholar said: "I have understood your three needs, and I answer you in what you have asked. As for the need that has passed, it is your life in knowledge after death. May Allah bless you with that life and bring you in it to the best of what you intended. As for the need for which you hope, it is to act upon what you have learned and to have it accepted when you are brought forth; may Allah bring that about for you by His mercy. As for the need you ask of me, it is as though you wished for an increase upon what you have heard."

The boy said: "There is no increase greater than your knowing what is within me. So grant me what you see fit."

The scholar said: "As for my knowing what is within you, none knows the unseen but Allah; yet the believer sees by the light of Allah. As for the increase, I will make its matter plain to you and set you upon the foundations by which you are to work. Look upon all those things whose outward form I have described to you, which you have taken as parables. Whatever among them is praiseworthy is a likeness of the truth and its people, its best for their best, and its signs point to them. And whatever among the parables is blameworthy, I have turned it upon the enemies of the truth and its people, its great ones for their great ones and its small ones for their small ones, and its signs point to them. These are the foundations of the knowledge upon which all practice turns; if you preserve them, you will have no need of any other. And I commend to you the fear of the One who created you when you were nothing, the keeping of His covenant, and the fulfillment of the right He has imposed upon you and upon all His creation."

When he had finished his discourse, brought the boy to his place of safety, and completed his nurture of him, he said: "O my son, take care of yourself, for I have a concern besides you, and this is the parting between me and you."

At the mention of parting the boy gave such a gasp that he almost destroyed himself; his spirit nearly took flight, and his soul nearly left his body. Then he bent to kiss the scholar's feet and wiped their dust upon his cheeks, and he bade him farewell and departed. The boy returned to his house with his heart taken from him in grief, unable to hold back his weeping in sorrow at the parting from his elder father, the father who had been the cause of his blessing, until his family and his neighbors grew afraid for him. He passed his day and his night restless with sorrow and alarm. Then his father came in upon him, filled with anger.
