Abu Malik asked, "And who are these that know the science of heaven after the prophets and the messengers? For we know of none of them in our time, nor have the people had any covenant with such men since revelation was cut off by the books, and the nations follow nothing now but transmitted reports. So what do you say of this?"

Salih answered, "Any religion that is established only upon narration is not called a religion, for religion is what is valued by God in its own right and admits no difference within it, whereas the narrations, as you well know, are full of disagreement, each denying the other. And the religion of God is dearer than that."

"Yet when they differ in the narration," said Abu Malik, "they refer back to the books. Whatever agrees with the books they accept, and whatever contradicts them they reject as false."

"But what is found in the books only in general terms," Salih replied, "who then will make it specific for them? And what is found there already specified, who will connect it back for them? Who among them is trusted concerning what God has made lawful and what He has forbidden? I heard you myself on the day of the quarrel, when a distressed man came to you and said, 'O Abu Malik, I swore an oath of divorce and then broke it.' You said to him, 'Some hold that she is now cut off from you and not lawful to you until she marries another husband, and some hold that she is still your wife and is not made forbidden to you by a single pronouncement of divorce.' The man asked you, 'Then which of the two shall I act upon?' and you told him, 'Act upon whichever of the two you wish.' So he rose and left you not knowing whether the home he returns to is lawful or forbidden. Is this how judgment is rendered in the religion of God? Is this how His lawful and His forbidden are determined?"

Abu Malik said, "By my life, the Sharia of this age has been ruined, corrupted, and left idle, and we hold now to nothing but the name of religion. So what is the state of religion with you?"

"And what is religion, O Abu Malik?" Salih asked.

"Command and prohibition," he said, "the lawful and the forbidden, the Sunnah and the obligations."

"And in your view," said Salih, "is Sharia anything other than this?"

"I know of nothing beyond what I have described to you."

"Then you have gathered religion and Sharia onto a single ground?"

"Yes."

"Then religion has been left idle by the suspension of the Sharia," said Salih, "and corrupted by its corruption. What is left with you?"

At this Abu Malik fell silent for a time, his head bowed, turning over in his mind the matter of his religion. Then he raised his head and said, "I do not see that anything is left with me. So what religion do you hold?"

Salih said, "Religion, with me, is that whose veil is made firm against the corruptors and whose doors are opened wide to the seekers, so that the one who brings it is ennobled by his precedence and the one who seeks it attains the good; through its excellence works are honored, and through its justice the sacred laws flourish. It is a cause joining heaven to earth, connected and unbroken, the loop of those who take hold and the rope of those who cling fast, the ark of tranquility, the ship of salvation, and the light of life. Its content is generous, its sciences are safeguarded, its jewels are marked with a sign, and their worth is hidden from no one who looks upon them."

"If God has a religion by which men are to be judged one day," said Abu Malik, "with which He is pleased, and by which He rewards and punishes, then it must be in this sense you describe. And now the excellence of the attribute has become plain, so how then is the One who is thus described?"

"The good comes only from what is good, O Abu Malik," Salih said. "Set your light in the lamp-niche of your mind and grasp what is intended for you. The claims of destiny have pulled you in their contest, the robes of vanity have dazzled you, and you have taken upon yourself a burden that no one else can bear for you, together with the affairs of those who follow you in good faith, who have listened to your speech, accepted your guidance, and trusted your judgment. It is your duty to guard yourself and them from a fire you cannot escape passing near, so seek out the road to deliverance from it. You are the most favored of them in the grace of God and the most bound to give thanks for it, for I have known you to recognize the good and to hope for its reward. As for what you asked, the confirming of that to which I have called you, it is my obligation to establish it for you from whatever aspect you wish."

"All that you have said so far is sound and firmly true," said Abu Malik. "As for the narrations of the common people, religion is not set right by them, nor are they fit for it. There remains for us only to know that to which you call us."

"I call you," said Salih, "to the justice of God and His oneness."

"How excellent is that to which you call," Abu Malik replied. "Indeed, this is the very doctrine we profess, and we know its excellence above all other schools of thought."

"That is not where your thought has gone, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "The doctrine you mean is a description of justice without knowledge of the One so described, and a use of the names of God without knowledge of His oneness, and this I refuse. What I call you to is the knowledge of God's justice and the true landmarks of His unity. If you wish, I will hold you back from the corruption of that saying however you like."

"If the doctrine of the people of justice is corrupt and their profession of oneness is denial," said Abu Malik, "then there is no firm truth left upon the earth, unless the meaning lies outside all the doctrines altogether. So show us the corruption of justice and of oneness, and from where it comes."

"Justice and oneness are among the noblest of all doctrines," said Salih. "Oneness is too great to be corrupted, for there is nothing set against oneness, and justice cannot be corrupted, for it is the judgment that stands between God and His creation. But the doctrines are all spoiled by the way men have corrupted the saying of justice and the rest, for it becomes a mere claim by which no statement is proven, and they do not encompass the knowledge of what they say until they return to the command of God. So take up whichever of the two you wish and defend it, until I strip it away from you by the argument of truth."

"I will not defend a saying I do not hold," said Abu Malik, "nor one whose author never asked this of me, who never appointed me his agent, nor am I a guardian over the masters of these doctrines. I will defend before you only my own saying. I say that God is One, nothing is like Him, just in His judgment, who does not burden His servants with what they have no power to carry. He commanded them and forbade them, yet He did not command by compulsion nor forbid by force; rather His command is a giving of choice and His prohibition is a warning, so the obedient did not obey under coercion, and the disobedient did not disobey as one overpowered. This is my meaning and what I profess."

"Did I not tell you," said Salih, "that this doctrine is among the noblest? But these are names and attributes that profit nothing unless their meaning is present behind them. It is like your saying, 'fire is hot.' You have spoken truly, yet the truth does not profit you, for your saying 'fire is hot' is only the name of an attribute, and to utter it profits nothing if no food is cooked by it and no darkness is lit by it; those who speak it remain in the dark without the meaning of fire being present. Were names and attributes to stand in things in the place of meanings, then the mere mention of fire would burn your tongue, and the mention of food would leave you full."

"As for what you say of these things, you have spoken truly," said Abu Malik, "for their names and attributes profit nothing unless their meaning is present. So show me that my words about justice and oneness are names and attributes that require the knowledge of a named and described reality."

"You say that God is One," said Salih, "and 'One' is four letters. We see two letters joined and two letters separate. So which of them is 'one,' or are they all together 'one'? And you say 'Allah,' and that too is written in letters. Is the alif alone the name, or are all the letters together 'Allah,' or are they signs pointing to God, or do you say something other than this?"

Abu Malik was struck silent for a time. Then he said, "I find no way out of this except that they are signs pointing to God."

"Which of them points to God?" Salih asked. "And by what do they point to Him, by their being apart or by their being joined? Or singly and together, as when you say 'Allah,' where the alif stands alone and the three letters are connected. Which then indicates God, this one standing alone, or these joined together?"

"I do not know what to say to you about this," said Abu Malik. "Take us on to some other door."

"Then the door to the knowledge of oneness has been shut in your face, O Abu Malik," said Salih. "Whoever taught you it in this fashion has closed it against you. Is it not so?"

"Yes," he said.

"Then I ask you about your knowledge of God," said Salih. "From where does it come, and from whom did you take it? Was it from an attribute that led you to One described?"

"No."

"Was it from a name that led you to One named?"

"Yes."

"And what is the name that led you?"

"Allah."

"Is 'Allah' a name," asked Salih, "or the One named?"

"The One named."

"Then what is the name?"

"Allah."

"So 'Allah' is a name for Allah?"

"Yes."

"Then what is the difference between the name and the One named?" asked Salih. "And which of the two do you worship, or both?"

Abu Malik was perplexed for a time, turning over his answer in his mind. Then he said, "I find no way through this except that I have seen that every craft has a maker, and so I judged that this vast creation has a Creator who resembles nothing of what He has made, just as craftsmen do not resemble the things they fashion."

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "you have strayed from the first meaning. The narrowness you met in the meaning of oneness has driven you to seek Him by analogy and to set up likenesses for Him, and God is dearer than that. For everything made is made from some prior thing, whereas God did not create things from anything that existed before them. As for these craftsmen, you know them because you have watched some of them with your own eyes and seen how they work; so you passed judgment upon a making whose maker was hidden from you, on the strength of an action resembling it that you witnessed among the craftsmen you have seen and known, reasoning by comparison. But when God created, He did not make you a witness to the creation of what He fashioned, nor did He have any likeness that fashions as He fashions. Yet you have testified from a present likeness to an absent one. I never knew you, O Abu Malik, to be content with analogy in your religion, so how can you be content to seek the knowledge of your Lord by measurement? Your very words, 'nothing is like Him,' prove, out of your own mouth, that He has a likeness and that nothing is like that likeness. Had you meant true oneness, you would have said, 'He is not like anything,' and you would not have said, 'nothing is like Him.' Glory be to the One whom no reach of aspiring thought can grasp, and whom no soaring of the pointing mind can attain by perception, exalted far above the description of those who are charged with duty. O Abu Malik, had you turned this search upon one of His servants, or a king from among His angels, you would be left unable to comprehend what is said of that one and how he was made, so how then of his Creator? The lights of the people of the earth fall short of perceiving the light of the sun, and the sun is but one of the lights of this world. How then shall one who falls short before a creature of His perceive the Creator Himself? Say now what seems right to you."

Abu Malik cast his eyes to the ground, bewildered, finding no path to speech.

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "raise your head and say whatever comes to you."

"Speech is gone, and there is nothing to say," said Abu Malik. "Minds are confounded before the answer, the imaginings are struck dumb before thought, and the faculty is too weak; by it one can reach nothing but the seeking and the asking."

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "the One who is of such greatness, do the eyes perceive Him?"

"No, He is higher than that."

"Is speech heard from Him?"

"There is no speech except from a speaker," said Abu Malik, "and the speaker is described by speech, and God is too great for such attributes."

"This Vast One of greatness, this Exalted One of power," said Salih, "would He be unjust in His creation, charging them with what they cannot bear and commanding them with what they cannot grasp, and then punishing them if they fail to do it?"

"Whoever charges the obedient servant with what he has no power to bear," said Abu Malik, "has laid an act of disobedience upon him, and to punish him for it would be a wrong, and God is dearer than that."

"O Abu Malik," said Salih, "the One who is thus removed from injustice and thus exalted above wrongdoing, would He be content that ignorance should be a familiar companion to His servants?"

"Ignorance is a sure denial of thanksgiving and a persistence in unbelief," said Abu Malik, "and God does not love the unbelievers."

"Were they then created as knowers," asked Salih, "already aware of what is wanted of them?"

"No," he said. "He created them ignorant, knowing nothing at all."

"Then from where do they come by the knowledge of what He wants of them," asked Salih, "so that it is made binding upon them to please Him, and so that they may be guarded from His wrath?"
