The father's anger did not spare him. "O my son," he said, "is this the reward of sons to their fathers? I taught you only the best that a child can be taught by a father. You never denied your own self nor renounced your beliefs until the day I grew old with this strange man. You drew him in upon me, and you crossed over with him in his affair without ever consulting your parents. If what I knew was true, then you have hindered me in my very self, for you concealed the truth from me. And if it is false, then you have disobeyed me in your own ruin, and whatever has reached him has thereby reached me."

The boy answered gently. "O my father, I have shown you nothing but mercy and compassion. You raised me as a mercy from you, and you nourished me, choosing me over family and wealth. I have no wish to be unkind to you on this day of ours. Rather I ask that you grant me, out of your bounty, one of two things."

"Name the two," said the Shaykh. "What are they?"

"Either you allow me to keep to my courtesy and my kindness toward you, for I am your son whom you raised when I was small, and our affair is in the hands of God; or you grant me leave to debate with you, and give me your assurance that I am safe from your anger, so that I may draw upon your mind."

The Shaykh weighed both. "As for courtesy on the outside while our hearts differ within, that is a reproach that would stand between me and you. How long can outward agreement hold when what lies beneath it differs? I do not want that. But your debate and your candor you shall have. So say what seems best to you."

The boy said, "Consider my standing with you for forty years. Can it be anything other than one of two cases? Either you are a scholar, and you have withheld from me the teaching I was owed. If so, do not blame me for seeking my own salvation through another, since you forbade it to me. Or you are ignorant, and then you are excused in my eyes; but in that case you needed the stranger even more than I did. Were I to reach him before you, it is because of your age and the lateness of your birth, for I have heard his words just as you have heard them, and I have seen what you have seen."

The Shaykh's heart was rent by the boy's words. He saw that there was no escape for him from either argument, and his eyes overflowed with tears. "O my son," he said, "the argument you have laid against me falls likewise upon you, if you keep your affair hidden from me. So tell it to me. If it is true, I will accept it from you, as a dignity to myself; and if it is false, I will turn you back from it, as a dignity to you and out of compassion for you."

So they debated at intervals and disclosed things to one another at length, until they were both guided to the right path: acting upon the religion of God, upholding it, holding fast to it, calling others to it, and cooperating in it. Through the two of them God revived many of His creation. The son went out into the world to give the good news of his father's guidance and to call others to come to the teaching, and he returned and became a cause of guidance for them. God blessed the people of that country through them and at their hands. God's command was fulfilled, and slander was disappointed.

The boy's name was Salih, and his father's name was al-Bakhtari. The two of them were honored above their people and raised to a high standing for the excellence of the manners they had acquired. News of them spread through the land, and with it news of their religion, until it reached a group who still held to the old creed. Al-Bakhtari had long carried influence among them, and they feared that his departure from their religion would cut off that influence. So they went out and gathered before a scholar of theirs named Abu Malik, Abd al-Jabbar, the mighty servant, who was called among them, for his gravity, the abundance of his judgments, and his defense of his religion, "the heel of the inks." He was their authority through his knowledge, his grasp of speech, and his discernment of opinions.

When they came in to him they said, "O Abu Malik, do you not see what the boy has gone over to?"

"Which boy?" he asked.

"Salih ibn al-Bakhtari."

"And what has he gone over to?"

"We tell you that a stranger came to the country, with a doctrine and a creed he calls people to, and we do not know what it is. The boy went to him and went out with him without his father's leave, and was absent a while. Then he came back speaking the stranger's words and calling to what the stranger called to. And we are told that his father has now answered him in his opinion and taken his side. This grieves us, and we have held back from them for three reasons. First: al-Bakhtari's household was as our own, for the bond of religion we shared, and we dread estrangement after intimacy and hate division after brotherhood. Second: if these people are upon the truth, then we have failed to follow them and to help them in it. Third: if they have gone astray, then we have failed to turn them back and to support the truth and its people. You are the head of our religion and one of our standards, and we have come to inform you and to consult you, and we will not go against your opinion."

Abu Malik said, "Be gentle in your judgment, for what you say has given me pause. You know that al-Bakhtari and his son were among those we counted as people of gravity and piety, and that they have rights over us and old favors to their credit. You have said of them what you have said, and you are people in whom I trust the truth above all else, so that God has spared you from resorting to anything less. This is news that has come upon a people, and an argument has been shown to them, and they have followed it and been satisfied by it. We cannot rule them astray and so wrong them, nor rule them guided and so wrong ourselves by imitating them, nor say they were deceived and refute them without knowledge of them, for they are nobler with us than that. What we must do is look at this news with a fair eye, by the light of justice and with a steadfast mind. It has been said: he who is steadfast in his affairs keeps his authority secure, and he who is hasty cannot keep his foot from slipping.

"A breach in most things admits repair, and what is spoiled in them can be replaced, as with a flaw in food, whose harm is easily remedied and whose cure is near. But a breach in religion is gravely damaging and greatly ruinous. Its flaw cannot be mended nor exchanged for another, as with a flaw in a medicine, which grants its taker no reprieve and leaves him no way to save himself. We have been taught that the best of provisions are the treasures of sound minds and the acquisition of manners, whose fruits are praiseworthy; and among their fruits is the following of forbearance. Forbearance is the crown of the learned. By it noble resolve lifts them to the highest of glories, their merit is made plain in every place, and they attain the honor of gain with ease of demand. For ourselves we have laid up a measure of that forbearance we borrowed, and something of the manners we acquired in the past of our days amid narrow circumstances and hard striving. We endured that for its worth, and we call upon it against the blows of events and the accidents of the age. We cannot let it go to waste. This is the very hour of our need for it, and of putting it to use in what has come upon us in the matter of our brothers.

"There is no sense in leaving them and what they are upon without laying it open and debating it with them, with the forbearance of the capable and the earnestness of seekers, free of any doubt in what we hold and free of any accusation against these people. They may have turned from the truth in forgetfulness, and they are nobler than that; or they may have gone out into error, and that is beneath them. Falsehood has no cleverness left to it once the truth stands plain. So reflect with me on the affair of these people, for it cannot escape one of three cases. Either they are upon what we know of the religion, and what has happened among them is only some addition of manners, reports, and verses, and the speech of men is much; or an increase has come to them upon the religion they already hold, and they have rejoiced in it, for knowledge is the lost property of every believer, and an increase of knowledge is better than a lack of it; or a guidance has come to them other than what they had, through the prophecy of a prophet brought to them and calling them to it, which they have followed upon clear proof. And this is not to be denied of the command of God. Creation will never reach the limit of God's decree over them or His will for them, however hard they strive, for God is at work every day."

They said, "O Abu Malik, we came to you with hearts full of rage against these people, ready to declare them unbelievers, to refute their opinion, and to make lawful to ourselves their blood and their wealth. And now we find our hearts humbled by your words and by the way you have stood up in argument on their behalf and spoken in their defense."

Abu Malik said, "This argument of mine is not for them. It is for the truth, that I may not betray it in its absence. I have risen for the argument of the truth when the one who would argue for it is not here. Nor is it a defense of them; I have defended myself and you against speaking falsehood and hurling accusations into the unseen about what we do not encompass in knowledge. For otherwise we would be like the tyrants of the nations before us, who were pleased with their own ways of judgment and rejoiced in the knowledge they had, until they imagined they had encompassed all that God commanded and taught and willed for His creation. They said God would send no messengers after their messengers, and they never supposed that beyond the knowledge they had reached there lay any further way. So they ruled that God should cut off His argument from His creation and His deputies in His earth. They made the knowledge of God in His servants, and what He sent His prophets and messengers with, no greater than the reach of their own minds; and whatever passed beyond that was, to them, misguidance. By that opinion they went astray and led many astray. They made lawful by their own ruling what God had forbidden, and by their own opinion they killed the prophets of God. I beg you, by God, do not follow the whims of a people who went astray before you. People of intellect may be swayed by whim toward every notion and deceived away from every path, save the path of religion, for in seeking it there is safety, and its merit is not hidden."

They said, "O Abu Malik, we will not go against your command, nor is your counsel hidden from us. We will not obey you at the beginning of our affair only to disobey you at its end. You have pointed to a course and hinted at a search. If you see fit, take us to these people, that we may have their doctrine laid open to us and look into it. If it is sound guidance, we will follow it, and if it is error, we will avoid it."

Abu Malik said, "This is doubt on your part about what you yourselves hold, and one who doubts neither knows his own right nor the falsehood of another."

They said, "Then we hold firm to our truth and reject whatever else there may be, whatever it be."

Abu Malik said, "That is not how meaning is sought, nor is guidance found by rejection."

They said, "How then is the search to be made, if we neither reject falsehood nor affirm the truth?"

Abu Malik said, "If you already know the truth and accept it, and know falsehood and reject it, then you are not among the seekers at all. You are among those already learned in prophecy and guidance, who judge by revelation over the people of the world."

They said, "Then we are content with the truth we hold and have no doubt in it, and we ask to be excused from the labor of seeking."

Abu Malik said, "Are you not content that they should have merit over you in one respect, that you would grant them merit over you in many? If you will not consent to stand in need among the ranks of the seekers, will you then sit in ignorance among those who hold back?"

They said, "How has this come upon us, and in what have they been given merit over us?"

Abu Malik said, "Their merit over you is that they know what you know and, beyond it, what you do not. This is a matter that has come upon a people. If you do not seek them out, they will seek you out to it; and if you will not listen to them, they will strive against you for it. So they will gain merit over you both by being first and by their striving. As for holding back from the search, that is ignorance entire, and negligence, and a fault: upon the learned until he teaches, and upon the ignorant until he learns."

They said, "O Abu Malik, you have caught us with proof from every side, and your every word is right. Now open for us a door in your counsel by which we may act."

Abu Malik said, "To be dazzled by one's own opinion is blindness, and to neglect the search is error; yet there is no shame in the search upon the seekers. But the seeker must know the doors of seeking before he can rightly seek the truth by that knowledge. Whoever seeks the truth without knowing how to seek is the quicker to deny it, for falsehood shows its first signs in display and in agreement with prevailing opinions, while the truth shows its first signs in hardship and in opposition to whims. And none forsakes the agreement of his whim and bears patiently the onset of hardship except one whose heart is sound."

They said, "Here a door has opened for us, in which you point us toward the knowledge of seeking. Show us, then: what is that knowledge, and what is the sound heart?"

Abu Malik said, "As for the knowledge of seeking, it is that you know yourselves to be in need; and the needy one seeks his need in the humility of want before what he seeks. As for the sound heart, it is a heart that does not persist in denying whatever is brought to it, whatever it be, until the truth is what makes itself plain and shows its merit, and falsehood is what lays bare its own defect. Only then does such a heart discern falsehood by its reason and accept the truth by acceptance."

They said, "But we have been taught that whoever stands in this position, who neither verifies the truth nor refutes falsehood, is ignorant in his conduct: for he does not know the truth, that he might follow it, nor falsehood, that he might avoid it."
