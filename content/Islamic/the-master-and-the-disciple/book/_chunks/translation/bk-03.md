His eyes overflowed with tears, and so he broke off his speech. He took leave of the people, rose, and set out upon his journey. The people wept at the warmth and the eloquence of his sermon, and they wished to rise and go with him; but out of courtesy they held back, keeping only his memory with them in all of this. They did not truly know what they had assented to, and so they turned away to their houses.

The youth kept the company of the Master, with his permission, until the Master reached the end of his journey. The boy had a father who was a great sheikh of the Arabs, the most honorable of them in lineage and the most influential of them over the people of religion. When they arrived, the Master went in and brought the boy in with him. He called for food, and when they had finished and were settled into their talk, the boy spoke.

"O wise one," he said, "you have spoken and I have heard, you have preached and I have been informed. You adorned the religion with the finest ornament, you made it desired with the best of desire and the fullest of provision, and you called to it by the noblest of its trusts. The minds yearned toward your question, by the fissures your words opened in the hearts. Such excellence could only appear from one whose inward reality, the batin, has been made whole as a teacher, and whose outward bearing, the zahir, has broadened his forbearance, for he is a fountain of wisdom from a firm origin, whose blessings rise over the worlds, and the branches of whose branches are fruit for the seekers. I hope that you are one of those blessed branches, for I see in you a noble mind, a sound core, and a perfect intellect. You have said that minds are driven by ignorance to seek knowledge and capacity, and my mind is among the most needy and the most destitute of them. So is there for me a way to life?"

The Master said: "He who created the creatures by His power did not create them in vain, nor did He fashion them scholars from the beginning, nor does He abandon them in the wastes of ignorance, nor is He content that stupidity should be their culture or the garments of ignorance their clothing. He has appointed for them, as an exit from all of this, the very faculty by which He composed their constitution and completed their creation. He made hearing, the eyes, and the hearts filled with lights, as a mark of His honoring them and as an argument standing against them. Then He set the treasures of His wisdom and the marks of His will within doctrines that please Him, doctrines borne by His elect from among His creation. He charged them to hold that religion and to call others to it, and He imposed upon all the answering of His call by the faculty we have named, so that God might distinguish the corrupt from the good. So the seeker, by his seeking, comes to see, and the one heedless of his own backwardness is made known by it. There remains no argument for anyone against God after His Messengers, and no excuse for going astray after the evidence has been given."

The boy said: "The absence of any excuse has frightened me, and the presence of the evidence has made me long for it. I implore you for a proof, and I will be a lowly servant to you."

The Master said: "God did not create men as scholars. He created them as small children, ignorant, knowing nothing. There is no great save after small, and no scholar save after ignorance. Smallness is the prelude to greatness, and ignorance is a guide to knowledge. In the same way, knowledge cannot be borne except by the introduction of a lesser knowledge before it, one quicker than it into the hearts and smoother into the understanding, so that its beginning may be steadied in the imagination, the limbs may be humbled to its obligations, the ribs may shiver at its depth, and the minds may bear its aim. This is the Sunna of God with His servants. It does not befit us to depart from the community by our own opinion, nor to follow, under our own guidance, anything other than the Sunna."

The boy said: "I am too small for what I desire, so raise me. I am ignorant of what I seek, so teach me. I am bound by your favor, so have mercy on me. You yourself were once in this very state, and God drove to you the blessing at the hands of one whose trial with you was great and whose thanks became due from you. I know well the condition of one who suffers the narrowness of ignorance and the need of a man whose resourcefulness has failed."

The scholar said: "The state you describe is indeed as you say, and I am jealous for you to be free of it. But my mercy toward you is not to bring your hope nearer to you while your labor lags behind. Every claim is accepted only when it is truthful; knowledge gives no benefit until it is acted upon, and action gives no benefit until it is accepted. The truthfulness of speech is its justice, and the proof of knowledge lies in the open roads of deeds and the documented likenesses, in all that the ends of the lands have contained and over which night and day have differed. Do not pursue an argument idly, do not cast one aside idly, and do not accept one by mere imitation, lest the first doubt that rises against it tear it out of your heart. Do not deny an argument after its proof stands, lest you become a partisan of falsehood; and do not abandon inquiry into it and understanding of it, lest your opinion be left barren. Think well of me without holding back, strive in your questioning without suspicion, seek the religion as one like you should seek it, and seek God's help in your affair. God does not let the reward of the seeker go to waste, nor deprive of recompense anyone He sees seeking the good."

The boy said: "You have dealt justly with yourself, in fitting and effective deed, and you have pointed to the truth in the finest of words. I ask God's help, and I ask you concerning this matter to which you call: what is it, whose is it, and to whom does it belong?"

The scholar said: "It is the religion of God which He has accepted, and from Him alone can it be accepted, not from any other. He delivered it first to the elect among His Messengers, and then in general to all His creation. Upon the one specially favored is thanks for it, action by it, and the call to it. Upon the one deprived of it is the seeking after it until he comes to know, and obedience to the one who guides him."

The boy said: "This favor that God has singled out for His friends: are all such favors equal in their worth, so that men are equal in their excellence, or do some surpass others?"

The scholar said: "Have we not already said that every knowledge has before it a lesser knowledge that taught it, and that every favor has its bearers, so that they stand at graded ranks before their Lord, each charged according to what he has gained of knowledge? Then to every possessor of merit is given his merit, in justice from God toward His creation and in mercy to His servants."

The boy said: "This to which you call me, is it that very favor, or is it something other than it?"

The scholar said: "It is not identical with it in its full reality, nor is it wholly other than it. Rather it is from it, as a single scoop of water from the breadth of the Euphrates. I call you to what God has accepted for His servants and honored on the day He completed it, mighty and guarded, generous and hidden. He ennobled it by His reward, an honor for the one who upholds it, and He made its retribution a debasement for the one who wastes it. Its outward form is figures, and its inward reality is knowledge. Its wisdom is a light, and its justice is plain. This is what I call you to."

The boy said: "What has made you more entitled to this matter than others, and more deserving of it than the rest, when you claim it is from God for all creation alike, so that everyone lays claim to it and denies it to his neighbor? What has made you more worthy of the name of truth than they, when this matter came to you only by the same road it came to them?"

The scholar said: "There is no scholar who speaks a word of truth but that he has drawn it from the very origins from which we ourselves have drawn, and in this they meet us, acknowledging it from us and among themselves. What then separated us from them was their abandoning of those principles while we held fast to them: we obeyed where they disobeyed, we preserved what we were entrusted with where they squandered it, we upheld what we were charged with where they neglected it. So they agree with us in acknowledgment and differ from us in deed. Their acknowledgment is a witness for us against them, and their squandering is the sign of their poverty toward us and their need of what is in our hands. They are like brothers who inherited great wealth from their father and divided it equally between them. Some squandered their portion until they fell into poverty, while others kept theirs, guarded it, and traded with it, so that the wealth in their hands was multiplied. Then the poor among the brothers turned to the wealthy, to serve them and to seek their bounty. So too are these who wasted the way of their Messengers: their ignorance drove them to seek knowledge from the people of the noble origins. The proof that we are more deserving of the truth than they is their need of us and our sufficiency without them. So our scholar has become the one who answers their ignorant, our clothed one covers their naked, and our rich one feeds their hungry. Thus the truth is bound to the one of merit, the credit belongs to every possessor of favor, and thanks are owed to every doer of kindness."

The boy said: "As for your superiority over them, that has become clear; and as for the argument of truth, it has been established against every one of the common people who squanders. But what is your argument against me, when I already acknowledge what you have said, stand by what you have described, deny what you have denied, and know the truth and its worth, and know falsehood and its people?"

The scholar said: "My argument against you is the same as my argument against them, since you have parted from them in outward description but agreed with them in the inner meaning."

The boy said: "How can that be, when I acknowledge the truth to its rightful owner while they deny it?"

The scholar said: "If you have parted from them by acknowledgment where they denied, you have agreed with them in withholding where they withheld. For what does acknowledgment profit without a turning back to act, and what does denial harm when the reality is present?"

The boy said: "Then I turn back to the people of truth by their right, if you make me know them, or to an agent who can establish it for them. Show me which of the two you are able to do."

The scholar said: "Do you not see that you have been driven to need the establishing of the rightful owner of the truth, or of his agent, just as the common people were driven to need the establishing of the truth itself? Deeds have set you apart from them, and ignorance has joined you to them."

The boy said: "You speak truly. Make it clear to me."

The scholar said: "Have you not acknowledged that my argument stands against you and against the common people?"

He said: "Yes."

The scholar said: "Then can anyone establish for himself a truth that belongs to another?"

He said: "No one is able to do that."

The scholar said: "Then the argument has been established against you and against the common people. Nothing remains but submission, and turning to the one whom you cannot do without and who can do without you."

The boy said: "As for my poverty, I acknowledge it. As for the One who is rich while I am in need, I seek Him, so have mercy on me."

The scholar said: "Have you ever seen one whom poverty drove to a man of bounty attain anything of that bounty except by earning or by favor?"

The boy said: "Yes. Nothing of it is attained except by earning or by favor."

The scholar said: "And do the people of favor gain any share of what belongs to the people of earning?"

He said: "No."

The scholar said: "Then seek this matter by work, and you will attain it by desert. Do not seek it by flattery, for then you would attain it by favor, and so be left deprived."

The boy said: "You are the key to goodness and its guide. Show me the more meaningful of the two paths and the nearer of them to guidance. The greatness of my need has bound my captivity fast to you."

The scholar said: "O my son, there is nothing beyond you, and no reproach against you. What you have hoped from me shall reach you. But it has limits not to be transgressed, and conditions not to be forgotten."

The boy said: "Set whatever limit you love, and lay down whatever condition you see fit. I hope you will find me patient under your testing and grateful for your kindness."

The scholar said: "The first of the limits is to uphold the outward meaning of the Book and its laws, and to act upon what you know of it. For whoever acts for God by what he knows, God guides him to what he does not know."

The boy said: "But the Books are many, and all of them are from God. For every Book there is a scholar among creation who cleaves to it, and they are all agreed upon guarding it and upon the religion within it."

The scholar said: "We have come back to our first words, and we are made to need the settling of what should have needed no settling."

The boy said: "How is that?"

The scholar said: "From your saying that the Books are many, that all are from God, and that each has its own scholar. Do you then imagine that the words of God and His Books cancel one another, that the first of them gives the lie to the last, and the last to the first?"

The boy said: "I do not imagine that. But what is the proof of it?"

The scholar said: "Had creation been able to do all that is in the first Book, it would have led them to the second; and had they done what is in the second, it would have led them to the third, until the end of the work is reached in the last of them, since the last is worthier than what came before it. For though all of them are from God, the later is nearer to the present age and clearer in its way, being an abrogation of what preceded it, with nothing coming after it to abrogate it."

The boy said: "You speak truly, and you have shown me the knowledge of truth and the sign of truthfulness. I hold to the limits you have ordered upon me. So what are your conditions for me?"

The scholar said: "My conditions upon you are five: do not fail me if I entrust you; do not conceal anything from me if I ask you; do not seek me out until I answer you; do not ask me for anything until I begin with you; and do not mention my affair to your father."

The boy said: "I will do all that you have named, and it is easy for me set against the greatness of what I hope from you. But how am I to manage my father? He is one of the heaviest trials upon me and the most severe I have to bear. Will you permit me to remove myself from him?"

The scholar said: "To withdraw from him would be a signpost pointing others toward you. Rather stay with him, be dutiful to him, satisfy him with some measure of outward agreement, keep your secret from him, and veil your affair. God will suffice us against his harm and the harm of others."

The narrator said: Then the scholar and the boy took leave of one another, and they remained apart for a long stretch of their days. The boy stayed perplexed in his affair. He knew nothing but the law at which he had been made to halt, he did not know where the scholar dwelt, and he could not seek him out, bound by the condition laid upon him, even as the absence lengthened. All the while the scholar was watching his patience and his endurance of what had been laid upon him. At last, on a quiet night when creation was still and the spies had grown heedless, when they had settled together in their assembly, the boy felt that his need was drawing near and that the chance to be alone with his companion had come. He fell down before him in prostration, and when he raised his head, the scholar spoke.

"I saw you prostrate in thanksgiving," said the scholar. "Thanksgiving before the deed breeds suspicion, and thanksgiving cut short after the deeds makes the deeds themselves vain."

The boy said: "All your words are truthful, and all your works are mastery. My thanks are only for the favor that has already come from you, and by thanking I hoped for an increase from you."

The scholar said: "Thanksgiving deserves that it should bring increase to the thankful. Your right is now due, so turn to me with your heart, bring your understanding present, and listen to what is laid before you."

The boy said: "I have brought present the instrument of understanding and taken up the equipment of hearing. Say what seems good to you."

The scholar said: "The religion has a key that unlocks it and locks it, like that which distinguishes between unlawful union and lawful marriage."

The boy said: "This key you have named is great in the sight of God, since it distinguishes the lawful from the unlawful, and truth from falsehood. So what is it?"

The scholar said: "It is the covenant of God, which affirms His rights, gathers His obligations, and makes Paradise binding for His friends, the rope of God upon His earth and the safe haven among His servants. I will recite it to you, and I will bind you by it."

The boy said: "Yes, take from me whatever you love. I do not spurn your judgment, and I do not overstep your way."

The narrator said: Then the scholar set about reciting the covenant to the boy, delivering it slowly to him and binding him fast by it, while the boy could not master himself for awe, and his tears ran down from the intensity of the lesson, until he reached the end of the covenant. Then he praised God and glorified Him, and gave thanks for what had come to him, and he knew with certainty that, by accepting their pledge, he had entered the Party of God (Hizb Allah) and the company of His friends. Then he fell silent from his praising, and the scholar began to expound and explain.
