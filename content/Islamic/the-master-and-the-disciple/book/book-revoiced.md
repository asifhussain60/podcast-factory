The Journey of Knowledge

Let us embark on a journey, a journey of the heart and mind, to understand the profound wisdom that guides us. Imagine a man from Persia, not dull or unlearned, but rather sharp-witted and cultured. Yet, he was enveloped in a profound ignorance, a thirst he could not name, mistaking mirages for water. This is the ignorance of not knowing the ultimate truth, the knowledge that truly matters. But Allah, in His infinite mercy, found him in this state, illuminated his path, and enriched him with knowledge, transforming him into a scholar, a dweller of the sacred.

The Best of Good Deeds

Once his guidance was complete and his hopes fulfilled, a sacred duty settled upon him: to express gratitude to his Creator and to serve his Lord. He carried a profound saying from his father, a truth that resonated deep within his soul, much like the soul resides within the body:

خَيْرُ الْحَسَنَاتِ إِحْيَاءُ الْمَوْتَى

"The best of good things is to raise the dead."

He reflected on this, realizing that he himself was once "dead" in ignorance, and Allah had revived him with knowledge. He understood that he was neither the first nor the last to be ignorant. Therefore, it was his sacred duty to pass on this divine trust, this blessing, to those who would come after him, just as it had been passed down to him. This sacred knowledge, a divine commandment, reached him through a chain of transmission, from one to another, even though those who cherished it most were the highest of Allah's creatures. He knew he was not the endpoint of this transmission; rather, he was a grateful recipient, obligated to continue the chain.

The Seeker's Quest

Driven by this profound realization, he left his family and wealth, dedicating himself to Allah. He traversed lands and tribes, seeking answers and fellow seekers, but found none until he reached the far reaches of al-Jazīra (the Island). There, in a city where people were heedless, he observed a group engaged in aimless religious debate, lost in their own whims. He sat apart, listening intently, discerning their methods.

He recognized three significant qualities in their discourse: they were actively engaged in argument, they belonged to the Ummah (community), and they were earnestly seeking religious understanding. He believed that a sincere seeker is always on the verge of finding what they seek.

When their discussion concluded, they approached him, asking about his identity and origin. He humbly replied, "I am a servant of Allah, and I am one of the dwellers of His Sanctuary." When asked about his need and work, he responded, "My need has passed me by, and I am at work in seeking it."

They inquired if anything they said pleased him. He replied, "All of it is strange — to one who marvels." They clarified that wonder can be praiseworthy or reprehensible. He agreed, stating that words, too, can be praiseworthy or reprehensible.

He defined praiseworthy words as those whose origin is from Allah and which call to Allah. Reprehensible words, he explained, originate from caprice and lead away from guidance. Convinced by his wisdom, they asked to hear his own words. He humbly stated, "I have nothing to say — but I am a follower of the right saying."

They then urged him to share his doctrine, hoping his presence would be a blessing and a door of mercy. He agreed, listening attentively to them before speaking.

The Divine Wisdom

He began by explaining that Allah, in His infinite power, created everything with perfect measure and purpose. He laid out the paths of existence, both outward and inward, free from whims. Allah is beyond ambiguity, beyond the grasp of mere observation or imagination. While minds may be bewildered in their attempts to comprehend His essence, He does not manifest in a way that His attributes can be fully possessed or His path altered.

Rather, Allah is generous. His knowledge is veiled by its own grace, and His secrets are safeguarded by their rightful custodians. His honor is the very essence of guidance, perfected by piety, and illuminated by the light of lights. This divine wisdom is a sacred trust, deposited in the hearts of the righteous, the keepers of the Word, the honor of scribes. They discharge this trust faithfully, never exceeding its bounds. It is the rule of judgment and the bounties of the Most Merciful.

Allah has honored, shielded, protected, and concealed this wisdom, entrusting His covenants to its chosen bearers. Thus, the thoughts of thinkers and the schemes of schemers cannot fully grasp it. Its outward manifestation is a remembrance and evidence for the guided, while its inward essence is a refreshing drink, its sky lowered for beholders, its land paved for seekers, offering stations for students.

This wisdom provides a guide for those who walk towards Allah, with signs whose wonders unfold, pointing to Him through parables. Its path is preferred, its method meticulously laid out, its rulings binding, and its Sunnah (practices) are like laws. Truthfulness ennobles its doctrines, and justice refines its gains. For every good, there is a leader and a way, and Allah has made it a garden and a guide to its inner chamber.

Minds, compelled by ignorance and need, are driven to seek knowledge and capability. Blessed is Allah, who has made night and day a succession for those who would be reminded or would give thanks. May Allah bless those He chose to worship Him and made a warning to the worlds.

He concluded with a powerful exhortation: "Fear Allah. Fear those who fear Him. Fear Him with the fear of one who would please Him. Seek knowledge. Learn. Work. And have mercy."

Etymology

Here, we delve into the linguistic roots of some key Arabic terms to deepen our understanding.

Ummah (أُمَّة): This word comes from the root أ م م, which signifies "mother," "origin," or "community." An أُمَّة is a community of people united by a common bond, often a shared faith or purpose, much like a mother is the origin of her children and the center of the family.

Sunnah (سُنَّة): Derived from the root س ن ن, meaning "to make a path," "to establish a way," or "to sharpen." Thus, Sunnah refers to the established practices, traditions, and exemplary conduct of the Prophet Muhammad, which provide a clear path for believers to follow, much like a sharpened tool creates a clear cut.

His eyes welled up, and tears choked his words. He bid farewell to the people, rose, and departed. They wept, deeply moved by his warmth and the eloquence of his speech. For a moment, they yearned to rise and follow him. Yet, to maintain an appearance of courtesy, they celebrated him from where they stood. They were unaware of the profound implications of what they had just agreed to. They then dispersed to their homes.

However, one among them did not. A young man from the group, the youngest and most contemplative, followed him. Discipline had sharpened his mind, and reflection had filled his heart. Now, the Master's words had been laid before him as a precious gift. This boy's father was a revered Sheikh among the Arabs, highly esteemed and influential in religious matters. So, with the Master's permission, the boy accompanied him until their journey concluded. The Master then invited him inside, called for food, and once their conversation had settled into a quieter, more relaxed rhythm, the boy began to speak.

"O wise one," he said, "I have spoken, listened, and preached in my time. I have been instructed. I have adorned the religion with the finest ornaments I could find and longed for it in the noblest way I knew. I have called others to it through its most honorable trusts. Our minds were hungry for a question from you, because of the openings your words carved into our hearts. Such excellence can only emanate from someone whose inner self has been perfected as a teacher, and whose outer being has expanded into wisdom. For he is the seventh fountain of wisdom drawn from a firm origin, his blessings reaching all worlds, and the branches of his branches bear fruit for seekers. I hope you are one of those blessed branches, for I see in you nobility of opinion, soundness at your core, and completeness of mind. You said that minds are driven by ignorance to seek knowledge and capacity. My mind is among the most needy and destitute. May I find life from the way?"

The Master responded, "The One who created the creatures by His power did not create them in vain. He did not create them already learned, nor does He abandon them to the misguidance of ignorance. He is not content for them to rest in cultural stupidity or to wear the garments of unknowing. He has provided them a way out of all this, the very path by which He composed their constitution and completed their making. He gave them hearing, eyes, and hearts full of lights—as a sign of His dignity towards them and as an argument against them. Then He placed the treasures of His wisdom and the marks of His will in doctrines that please Him, entrusted to His elect among His creation. He entrusted them with the religion, called them to it, and laid upon them the duty of answering Him, using the instrument we have named—so that He might separate the evil from the good. The seeker, by his very asking, comes to see; the one who is ignorant even of his own backwardness becomes known. After His Messengers, no one has any argument left against Him, and no excuse to go astray."

"The lack of any excuse has frightened me," the boy said, "and the existence of the evidence has made me want it. I am begging you for a proof. I will be a servant to you."

"Allah did not create men as scholars," the Master said. "He created them small, ignorant, knowing nothing. There is no greatness except after smallness, no scholar except after ignorance. Smallness is the prelude to greatness; ignorance is a guide to knowledge. Knowledge itself is not bearable except when something has gone before it—some prior knowing, quicker to the heart than the thing itself, smoother to the understanding—so that the learner is steadied in his first imaginings, his obligations are made lighter for him, his wings begin to shiver towards it, and his mind can carry its meaning. This is the way of Allah with His servants. Let us not deviate from it in religion by our own opinion, and let us not follow anything alongside it except the way itself."

"I am too small for what I want," the boy said, "so raise me. I am ignorant of what I have asked for, so teach me. I am bound by your favor, so have mercy on me. You yourself were once in this very situation. Allah forbid that you should withhold from me—through my own hands—the blessing of that great trial Allah once laid upon you, the one for which He is to be thanked through your person. I know what it is to be in the suffering of ignorance and the helplessness of having no resources."

"The state you describe is exactly as you say," the Master answered, "but I will not be merciful to you by doing for you what you must do for yourself. Every claim is accepted only when it is true. Knowledge is no benefit unless it is acted upon, and action is no benefit unless it is accepted. The truthfulness of speech is its justice. The proof of knowledge lies in the open ways and in the documented exemplars that have lasted through the lands, by which night and day themselves have been distinguished from each other. Do not pursue an argument carelessly, and do not dismiss one carelessly. Do not accept anything on imitation alone—because the first doubt that rises against it will tear it out of your heart. But do not reject the proof of an argument either, or you will leave an opening through which falsehood enters. Do not let your own opinion make inquiry and consensus useless. Think well of me without reservation. Strive in your questioning without suspicion. Seek the religion. Seek someone like yourself. And seek Allah's help in your matter. Allah does not waste the reward of the one who seeks, and does not deprive of reward anyone He sees pursuing the good."

"You have dealt justly with yourself," the boy said, "in a way both fitting and effective, and you have set the truth out in the best phrasing of it. I ask Allah's help, and I ask you about this matter you are calling me to: what is it? Whose is it? To whom does it belong?"

"It is the religion of Allah that He has accepted," the Master said, "and the only one from whom it can be accepted is Him. He delivered it first to the elite among His Messengers, and then to the whole of His creation in general. The one specially favored with it owes thanks for it, action upon it, and a call to others to take it up. The one deprived of it owes the searching after it, so that he comes to know it, and the obedience due to whoever guides him to it."

"This favor that Allah singled out for His friends," the boy asked, "are all such favors equal in excellence, or do some surpass others?"

"Have we not said that every kind of knowledge has its predecessor who taught it," the Master said, "and that each predecessor carried his own portion—so that they stand at graded ranks before their Lord, each one accountable for what he has acquired? Then every one of them is set right in his very making by the merit of his bestowal, and so becomes a mercy upon Allah's servants."

"This thing you are calling me to," the boy said, "is it that favor itself, or is it something other than it?"

"It is neither identical with it, nor wholly other than it," the Master said. "It is from it—the way a single chamber of water is from the breadth of the Euphrates. I am calling you to what Allah has accepted for His servants and ennobled. It is the day on which I bring it to completion—precious, secure, generous, hidden. Its honor, along with its reward, is dignity for the one who lifts it up. Its retribution is debasement for the one who loses it. Its outward form is figures; its inner reality is knowledge. Its wisdom is a yoke. Its justice is plain. This is what I am calling you to."

"What makes you more exclusively entitled to this matter than anyone else?" the boy asked. "If you claim it is from Allah for creation in general, then everyone has the same claim on it that you do. What gives you a greater right than they to the name of truth—has this matter come to you by some route that bypassed them?"

"There is no scholar who speaks a single word of truth," the Master said, "except that he has taken it from the very origins we ourselves have taken it from. In this they agree with us, by acknowledging it from us and among themselves. What separated us from them was this: they abandoned those principles while we held fast to them. We obeyed where they disobeyed. We preserved what we were entrusted with where they squandered it. We did what we were commanded where they neglected. So they agree with us in acknowledgement and disagree with us in fact. Their acknowledgement is a witness for us. Their loss is the sign of their poverty toward us and of their need for what is in our hands. They are like brothers who inherited a great wealth from their father and divided it between themselves. Some of them squandered their share until they fell into poverty. Others kept it, preserved it, traded with it, and the wealth in their hands multiplied. Then the poor among the brothers came back to the wealthier ones, to serve them and seek some of their bounty. So too those who lost the way of their Messengers come now, in their ignorance, to seek knowledge from the people of the original sources, the generous ones. The proof that we are more deserving of the truth than they is precisely their need of us, their dependence on what is in our hands—so that our knowledge has become the answer to their ignorance, our garment covers their nakedness, and our abundance satisfies their hunger. The truth is binding upon anyone who would be virtuous. The credit belongs to the one who confers the favor. Thanks is owed to the one who bestows the kindness."

"As for your superiority over them, that has become clear," the boy said. "And as for the argument of truth, it has been established for every honest seeker. But what is your argument against me—when I already agree with what you have said, stand by what you have described, deny what you have denied, and recognize the truth and its excellence along with falsehood and its people?"

"My argument against you," the Master said, "is the same as my argument against them—through my disagreement with them in description, and my agreement with them in meaning."

"How can that be," the boy asked, "when I acknowledge the truth to its owner, and they deny it?"

"If their denial is what keeps you back from joining them," the Master said, "they too have refrained from what is forbidden to them. So what is the benefit of acknowledgement without an agent to put it into effect?"

"Then take me to the people of truth, so that I may know them," the boy said, "or to an agent who can prove it to them. Show me which of the two you can do."

"Do you not see," the Master said, "that you have been made to need the proof of the rightful owner of the truth, or his agent, in exactly the way the public has been made to need a proof of the truth? Action has separated you from them, just as ignorance has joined you to them."

"I have believed," the boy said. "The matter has become clear to me."

"Have I not firmly established my argument against you and against the public?"

"Yes," he said.

"Can anyone prove to others a truth he holds only for himself?"

"No one is able to that," he said.

"Then the argument has been established," the Master said, "against you and against the public. Nothing remains but submission, and the appointing-over-yourself of the one whom you cannot do without and who can do without you."

"As for my poverty," the boy said, "I acknowledge it. As for the One who is rich while I am in need—I ask of Him, and I ask Him to have mercy on me."

"Have you ever seen someone driven by poverty to seek the help of a generous man," the Master asked, "obtain anything from him except by acquisition or by favor?"

"Yes," the boy said. "Nothing can be obtained except by acquisition or by favor."

"And do the people of favor reach the people of grace by way of acquisition?"

"No," he said.

"Then seek this matter by work," the Master said, "and you will be granted it as a merit. Do not seek it by flattery, or you will be deprived of it."

"You are the key to goodness, and its guide," the boy said. "Show me the more meaningful of the two roads, and the nearer of them to guidance. I am the most destitute of those who follow you."

"My son," the Master said, "there is nothing more for you, and nothing more is expected of me. What you hoped to receive from me has reached you. But there are limits not to be crossed, and conditions not to be forgotten."

"Set whatever limits you love," the boy said, "and lay down whatever conditions you approve. I hope you will find me patient under them, and grateful for your kindness."

Etymology

The Arabic term for "knowledge" is علم. Its root is ع-ل-م, which means "to know," "to perceive," or "to be aware." From this root, we derive words like عالم (scholar), معلوم (known), and علامة (sign or mark). The connection between the root and its derivatives highlights how knowledge is about understanding and recognizing signs and information.

The Arabic term for "wisdom" is حكمة. Its root is ح-ك-م, which means "to judge," "to govern," or "to be wise." From this root, we get words like حاكم (ruler or judge), حكم (judgment or rule), and محكم (firm or solid). The etymology suggests that wisdom is not just about knowing facts, but about having the ability to make sound judgments and govern oneself or others effectively.

The Arabic term for "religion" is دين. Its root is د-ي-ن, which means "to obey," "to judge," or "to be indebted." From this root, we derive words like مدين (indebted), ديان (judge), and مدين (city, as a place of governance). This connection implies that religion involves obedience to divine laws, accountability, and a sense of indebtedness to Allah.

The Arabic term for "truth" is حق. Its root is ح-ق-ق, which means "to be true," "to be real," or "to be deserved." From this root, we get words like حقيقة (reality or fact), تحقيق (investigation or verification), and محقق (investigator or verifier). This shows that truth is about what is real, verifiable, and justly deserved.

We are about to embark on a profound journey, one that will illuminate the very essence of our faith. Imagine a wise teacher, a guide, who sets before you not a rigid set of rules, but a path to deeper understanding. This path begins with a fundamental principle, a limit that, once grasped, unlocks a world of knowledge.

### The First Limit: Acting on What You Know

Our guide begins by stating the first limit: to uphold the outward meaning of the Quran and its laws, and to act upon what you already know. This isn't about seeking out hidden meanings or accumulating more information. It's about putting into practice the knowledge you already possess. Think of it like learning to swim. You don't just read books about swimming; you get into the water and practice the strokes you've learned. The road to understanding the concealed aspects of our faith begins with obedience to the clear teachings already in our hands. It's a powerful reminder that true knowledge isn't just intellectual; it's experiential.

### The Unity of Divine Books

Now, imagine a young, eager student, full of questions and perhaps a touch of youthful overthinking. This student, upon hearing about the Quran, might wonder about the many divine Books that have come before it. He might say, "There are many Books, and all of them are from Allah. For every Book there is a scholar from among creation who adheres to it, and they are all united in safeguarding it and in the religion it contains." This sounds generous, doesn't it? It seems to honor all revelations.

But our wise guide sees deeper. He understands that this seemingly inclusive statement could inadvertently dilute the unique claim of the final Book. He gently brings us back to a crucial point: "Have they then issued the verdict that the words of Allah and His Books cancel one another out — that the first of them gives the lie to the last, and the last gives the lie to the first?"

This question strikes us silent because, of course, they haven't. The divine Books do not contradict each other; rather, they form a continuous, evolving narrative.

### The Arc of Revelation

Our guide then provides a profound argument, one that has resonated through generations: "If created beings had been able to do what is in the first Book, then it would have pointed them to the second; and if they did what is in the second, it would have pointed them to the third — so that the work culminates in the last of them, because the last is what the earlier ones were pointing toward. Even though all of them are from Allah, the later is nearer to the present age and clearer in doctrine, since it copies and confirms what came before it, and nothing came after it to copy or supersede it."

This isn't about one Book being "better" than another. It's about understanding that each earlier revelation was a guiding hand, pointing forward. The final Book, the Quran, is the destination all those hands were leading to. To truly honor the earlier Books is to embrace the one they were preparing humanity for. To scatter our loyalty across all of them as if they were peers is to miss the clear direction they were drawing. Imagine a series of signposts on a journey; each one is important, but they all lead to a final destination. The Quran is that final destination, confirming and completing the messages that came before it.

### The Five Conditions of the Seeker

With this understanding firmly in place, the student, now enlightened, declares his belief and readiness to adhere to the limits. Our guide then lays down five conditions, like precious coins counted with care:

1.  Do not be absent if I summon you.
2.  Do not conceal anything from me if I ask you.
3.  Do not question me until I answer you.
4.  Do not request anything until I begin with you.
5.  Do not mention what passes between us to your father.

Let's reflect on these. They are not mere rules; they are disciplines for the seeker.

*   **Presence:** To be present when called signifies readiness and commitment.
*   **Transparency:** To hide nothing when asked fosters trust and openness.
*   **Patience in Inquiry:** Not to interrupt with questions before the answer has come teaches patience and allows for a complete understanding to unfold.
*   **Receiving Grace:** Not to grasp before what is meant for you is offered cultivates humility and allows for divine timing.
*   **Confidentiality:** To keep this sacred knowledge private, even from one's father, highlights the profound and personal nature of this spiritual journey.

The student readily accepts the first four, but the fifth, concerning his father, weighs heavily on him. He considers withdrawing from his father, thinking distance would better protect the secret.

### The Wisdom of Remaining Present

Our guide, with his profound insight, offers a different path: "Withdrawing from him would be a sign to others. Rather, stand with him and his estate with some outward agreement, and keep all this your secret and conceal it. Allah will be enough for us against his evil and the evil of others."

This is a powerful teaching for those who seek immediate, dramatic change. It teaches us that the outward appearance of our lives can continue as before, while a profound inner transformation takes root. Flight or overt rebellion can be a confession, and sometimes, a secret needs to mature in silence before it is ready to be revealed. It's about cultivating an inner world of faith while navigating the external realities of life with wisdom and discretion.

### The Discipline of Absence

After this profound exchange, a period of absence follows. The student is left with only what the law has taught him, unable to seek out his guide due to the condition of waiting to be summoned. This absence, though difficult, is itself a teaching. It cultivates the very patience and endurance that the guide is asking of him. It allows the student to feel the cost of discipline before receiving more profound doctrines. It's like a seed planted in the earth; it needs time and silence to grow before it can blossom.

### The Meaning of Thanksgiving

Finally, on a quiet night, the student is reunited with his guide. Overwhelmed with gratitude, he prostrates in thanks. His guide observes this and offers a crucial insight: "I saw you prostrating in thanksgiving. Thanksgiving before the work inherits the obligation; thanksgiving cut short after the work makes the works themselves vain."

This is a profound teaching on gratitude. Thanksgiving offered before a task begins makes that task binding; you have thanked Allah for it, so now you are obligated to fulfill it. And gratitude that is broken off after a work is finished can diminish the value of the work itself. True gratitude is not just a gesture at the beginning or the end; it is the very essence, the medium through which our actions flow. It is a continuous state of being, enriching every endeavor.

### The Key to Religion: The Covenant of Allah

The student, now ready, asks for the deeper knowledge he has longed for. And his guide reveals a profound truth: "Religion has a key that opens it and closes it, like that which distinguishes between forbidden union and lawful marriage."

Imagine the weight of this image: a single key that distinguishes the lawful from the unlawful, truth from falsehood, just as a marriage contract sanctifies a union. Without this key, outward actions might appear similar, yet stand on opposite sides of an absolute line.

The student, recognizing the immense significance of this key, asks, "So what is it?"

The guide answers: "It is the covenant of Allah, which establishes His rights, gathers His obligations, makes Paradise binding for His friends — the rope of Allah upon His earth, and the safe haven among His servants. I will recite it to you, and bind you to it."

This "rope of Allah upon His earth" immediately brings to mind the Quranic verse:

وَاعْتَصِمُوا بِحَبْلِ اللَّهِ جَمِيعًا وَلَا تَفَرَّقُوا

Q|3:103

This verse calls us to "hold fast, all together, to the rope of Allah, and do not be divided." The covenant, our guide explains, is this very rope. It is not just a feeling or an inclination; it is the binding instrument that establishes Allah's rights over our lives, gathers our obligations, and makes Paradise a certainty for those whom Allah has taken as His friends. It is the cord that keeps us from falling, connecting us firmly to our Lord.

### Becoming Part of the Party of Allah

With a heart full of acceptance, the student declares his readiness. The guide then recites the covenant, word by word, binding the student to it. The intensity of this instruction brings tears, a profound realization of what it means to be truly moved. Through this pledge, the student understands, not just believes, that he has become part of the Party of Allah:

أُولَٰئِكَ حِزْبُ اللَّهِ ۚ أَلَا إِنَّ حِزْبَ اللَّهِ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

Q|58:22

"Those are the Party of Allah. Truly, the Party of Allah — they are the successful."

This is an irrevocable moment, a profound transformation. The student falls silent, absorbing the magnitude of what has transpired. And then, the exposition begins, the deeper explanations unfold, revealing the layers of meaning within this sacred covenant.

### Etymology

*   **Quran (قرآن)**: The word قرآن comes from the root قرأ (qara'a), meaning "to read" or "to recite." It refers to the holy book of Islam, which is meant to be read, recited, and understood.
*   **Allah (الله)**: The word الله is the Arabic word for God. It is derived from the root أله (alaha), meaning "to worship" or "to adore." It signifies the one, unique, and all-powerful Creator.
*   **Covenant (عهد)**: The word عهد comes from the root عهد (ahada), meaning "to promise," "to pledge," or "to commit." It refers to a solemn agreement or pact, especially between Allah and humanity.
*   **Rope (حبل)**: The word حبل comes from the root حبل (habala), meaning "to tie," "to bind," or "to connect." In the context of "the rope of Allah," it symbolizes the strong connection and guidance that links believers to Allah.
*   **Prostration (سجود)**: The word سجود comes from the root سجد (sajada), meaning "to bow down" or "to prostrate." It refers to the act of humbling oneself before Allah, touching the forehead to the ground in worship.
*   **Thanksgiving (شكر)**: The word شكر comes from the root شكر (shakara), meaning "to be grateful" or "to thank." It signifies expressing appreciation and gratitude for blessings received.
*   **Party (حزب)**: The word حزب comes from the root حزب (hazaba), meaning "to gather" or "to form a group." It refers to a faction, group, or party, and in the Quran, "حزب الله" (Hizb Allah) refers to the party or company of Allah's friends.

Our journey into understanding the universe begins with the very first cause, the origin of all origins. Allah is the Creator and Composer of all things, bringing them into existence from nothing. We should not think of Him as merely the first in a sequence of created things, but rather as the First of all firsts, the ultimate Inheritor of everything. There is nothing before Him to compare, and nothing after Him that will not ultimately return to Him.

The creation of all things began from light. From this primordial light, three fundamental words emerged. The first is the will (الإرادة). From the will branched the command (الأمر). And the command then divides into the saying (القول) – when Allah wills something, He simply says:

كُنْ فَيَكُونُ
"Be, and it is."
Q|2:117

Notice the precise order: first the will, then the command, then the saying. These are three words. From them, the two letters of كُنْ and the five letters of فَيَكُونُ are derived, totaling seven letters. From these seven letters, seven fundamental things branched forth.

From the light, Allah created air (الهواء). Air is unique, singular, and without a partner. It surrounds everything but is itself surrounded by nothing. It stands as a testament to the One, Allah, of whom it is said there is nothing like Him. Then, from those initial three words, Allah created three more things: water (الماء), darkness (الظلمة), and light (النور). Following this, He created the heavens (السماوات) and the earth (الأرض). From water, He created smoke (الدخان). From the heat of darkness, He created condensation (التكاثف). From light, He created fire (النار). These three things branched from the three before them, each becoming a companion to its origin because it was drawn from it. And air, the seventh, encompasses all of them, presiding over them just as the unique encompasses the paired. This was the very beginning of creation, the genesis of all things.

Once creation was established upon these seven principles, and the path of forms was laid down based on likeness and analogy, Allah created the seven heavens and their celestial signs from smoke. From condensation, He created the seven earths and their inherent powers. These seven heavens and seven earths branch from those initial seven origins, and everything you see above or beneath you traces back to this lineage.

Now, let us look closely at the signs of the heavens and the powers of the earth. What are they, and what is their proof? We return to the three words and the seven letters that created the seven things: "the will to command by saying." This phrase contains twelve letters. Its signs in the heavens are the twelve constellations (الأبراج). Its witnesses are the seven kingdoms (الممالك). Its signs upon the earth are twelve, with ten belonging to the seven islands (الجزر). And its proofs are the seventeen seas (البحار). These are the signs of the heavens and the earth—signs we encounter daily, so plainly presented that we often overlook them.

Then, the heavens and the earth were veiled in darkness from the black heat, which represents the authority of the earth. Allah then pierced this veil with His lights, causing light and darkness to intermingle. They are two distinct realities, and He distinguished between them by night and day, through two signs. He made the night, with its sign a path of darkness, and apportioned it. He made the day, with its sign a path of light, and apportioned it. He adorned the night, in its darkness, with the moon (القمر) and the stars (النجوم); and the day shines, in its light, gleaming through the sign of the sun (الشمس). Then, the days produced states that hover between night and day, and these are the seven days of the week. The sign of each is this: every day has twelve hours, and every night has twelve hours—the twelve again, returning in another form.

When the creation of things was combined with these principles, Allah made them in pairs—male and female. This does not refer only to the literal, bodily sense of male and female. It signifies a deeper meaning: the active and the receptive. From this principle, the custom of pairing became the very habit of creation. Allah created all essences in this manner: animals, including humans, beasts, birds, and crawling creatures. This custom then extended into speech itself, where speech also became paired: adjectives and descriptions, names and meanings. Glory be to Him who created all pairs, making them manifestations of His command, exemplars of His religion, and signs of His power—pointing by things to their origins, and by parables to their interpretations.

Then, Allah pierced it with His light, opened it with His command, created it by His knowledge, and sealed it among His names with the letters of:

When He had completed all of them by His making and composed them in pairs by His measure, He selected for Himself, from the choicest of His will and the secrets of His unseen, a great knowledge and a clear light. He made these two a religion—the religion by which Allah draws His creation back to Himself. Then, He honored that religion with laws and with books, made it manifest by parables and by the sunna (السنة), and appointed, from the choicest of His creation, banners, signs, and rulers. He made them the houses of His prophethood (النبوة), the treasuries of His knowledge and His wisdom, His interpreters, and the bearers of His revelation (الوحي). Their succession in His earth is itself the evidence of the path of salvation—both in the apparent (الظاهر) and in the inner (الباطن). Some of them are imams and speakers (نُطَقَاء). Some are arguments (حُجَج) and chiefs (نُقَبَاء). Among them are the summoners (دُعَاة) to the good, and among them are the learned. In their dwellings, they are like the stars of heaven.

By them, the works were called, and by their names, the parables were named. Their Imam is like the great sun among the lights, surpassing all sight; he is their apparent and their likeness. The argument, and the gate of their Imam, is like the shining moon—its appearance and its likeness. Their summoners are like the manifest stars—the zawāhir (الظواهر)—their appearances and their likenesses.

All the pairs we have described are at once apparent and inward—figure and likeness, the seen face and the meaning behind it. The apparent are the seven heavens; their inner counterparts are the seven speakers (نُطَقَاء), and the first of the speakers are the messengers (الرسل). The seven earths are outwardly parables; their inner counterparts are the Imams of guidance (أئمة الهدى). For each of those Imams, there are twelve chiefs (نُقَبَاء), and their counterparts in the seven heavens are the twelve constellations. Likewise, each Imam of guidance has twelve arguments (حُجَج) by which Allah is invoked upon his people; and their counterparts in the earth are the twelve islands, the dwellings of the chiefs. On every island stands an argument. And for every argument, there are summoners—sometimes many, sometimes few. Allah increases in creation what He wills, so that the argument of Allah may reach His creation, ensuring that people will have no argument against Allah after the messengers.

Through these causes—the causes of Allah we have been describing—the argument of Allah reaches His creation, and His justice appears among them, outwardly and inwardly. The inner (الباطن) is the religion of Allah by which He has bound the saints of Allah. The outer (الظاهر) is the laws of religion and their likenesses. So, the religion of the laws is one and the same; and the laws of religion are a body and a guide. There is no body without a soul (الروح), because the soul is its life. And there is no power for the soul except in the body, because the body is its instrument. So it is with the apparent laws and their likenesses: they have no foundation except in the inner religion, because that inner religion is their light and their meaning, the spirit of life inside them. And the inner has no strength except in the outer, because the outer is its body and its proof. The knowledge of this world cannot be perceived except through the outer. The knowledge of the hereafter cannot be perceived except through the inner. Not a single letter of the letters of the subordinate, and not a single saint of the saints, can be perceived through the outer alone in any abundance—because the parables are too many and the laws too wide.

The more one speaks of this, the more there is yet to be said. But wisdom hides its truths and conceals its last from its first, because of its preciousness and the purity of its essence. The bright lights of wisdom cover one another as the light of the sun covers the light of the moon and the stars and weakens them. And so it is with the words of wisdom—they adorn the tongue of the wise, but the deepest of them lie always beneath the brightest of them, waiting.

The boy, having explored the depths of wisdom, now turned his gaze to the world around him, the very ground we walk upon. He observed the diverse opinions of people regarding this world. Some dedicate themselves entirely to it, seeking only to accumulate more, while others condemn it, practicing asceticism. Yet, even those who renounce it cannot fully escape its grasp, for only a small part of it is forbidden, while much is permissible. The boy noted that the scholar had not yet spoken about this world and eagerly awaited his perspective.

The scholar began by explaining that no one, neither the learned nor the ignorant, truly condemns this world. The ignorant appreciate its virtues because they know nothing beyond it. The learned, however, understand its true value through its deeper meaning. Only an ignorant person would truly condemn the world, and how can creation condemn the work of the Creator? Instead, the scholar suggested that the world is designed for those who perceive seven fundamental aspects, each branching from seven origins: heaven, earth, darkness, light, vessels, prosperity, and misery. These seven qualities each give rise to seven further qualities.

The heavens, the scholar explained, are seven. The earths are also seven. Light manifests in seven days and seven nights: Saturday is for rest, Sunday for boundaries and tradition, Monday for the bladder in its profound sense, Tuesday has three dimensions (apparent, inward, and the inward of the subtle), Wednesday is for the four (the sanctuary), Thursday is the fifth of Allah's causes, and Friday is Allah's greatest law. Darkness, in turn, represents the seven nights of paradise, corresponding to the seven days, with a night for each day and a day for each night.

The vessels, too, are seven: camels, horses, mules, donkeys, elephants, oxen, and ships. These seven vessels facilitate travel across the earth's diverse regions. Camels are for sandy deserts, horses for plains, mules for mountains, donkeys for short journeys, elephants for marshes and wastelands, oxen for salt flats and thickets, and ships for the seas. Each vessel serves a specific domain.

The blessings of this world, the scholar continued, are founded upon seven elements: air, water, light, darkness, food, clothing, and marriage. Air is the medium between the sky and earth, and the very breath of life for animals. Water, a topic he would revisit, is essential. Light is the essence of sight. Darkness fosters the growth of vegetation. Food sustains the body. Clothing, even in paradise, offers protection. Marriage is the source of reproduction and offspring. Some of these are keys to all goodness, while others are mines of blessings. How can any of them be blameworthy when they are all gifts and blessings from Allah, for which His servants should be grateful? Their counterparts in misery are also seven: heat, cold, hunger, thirst, fear, sickness, and death. These seven things require patience and understanding of their deeper meaning.

The boy acknowledged that these were the attributes of the world, and he had no doubt that everything in it was created by the Creator. He then asked what scholars meant when they criticized the world and what the ignorant meant when they praised it. It seemed to him that the ignorant, despite their lack of knowledge, had somehow grasped the truth, which would imply that the scholars, who contradict the ignorant, were mistaken.

The scholar gently corrected him. The scholars did not err in censuring this world, but their meaning is not understood by ordinary people. The ignorant, while correct in praising the world, did not truly comprehend what they were praising. One who is ignorant of a thing merely becomes accustomed to it.

The boy, eager for clarity, asked the scholar to reveal the truth of what the scholars say and to expose the falsehood in the speech of the ignorant, as the apparent reality seemed to suggest otherwise. He sought relief from his ignorance through divine knowledge.

The scholar explained that this world, and everything within it, has an apparent and an inner meaning. Its true strength lies only in Allah, for He is to the world as the soul is to the body. One who knows the soul finds grace in the body. But one who does not know the soul and acknowledges only the body is merely observing a corpse, and corpses are forbidden. This is why scholars forbade this world: because it is dead. They fled from this dead thing, which Allah has forbidden, until life comes to it from where Allah wills. The ignorant, however, are mistaken when they praise this world because they do not know its inner meaning or Allah's true intention behind it. They imagine Allah created the world for some other purpose, but Allah does not create things in vain. If the world and its contents were created solely for the world itself, it would be a fleeting creation without consequence or meaning. Any action without consequence is mere play, and any meaningless speech is falsehood, and Allah is far too exalted for that.

The boy, now enlightened, affirmed the truthfulness and excellence of the scholars and the falsehood of the ignorant, as proven by the argument. He felt that the distress he had expressed had been lifted, having gained an inner understanding of the world.

The scholar then asked the boy about the reward for one who brings him out of the distress of poverty into the capacity of welcome. The boy replied, "Thanks and praise." The scholar then asked about the reward for one who brings him out of the distress of ignorance into the capacity of knowledge. The boy answered, "Obedience and action."

The scholar continued, explaining that this world and everything like it is an apparent manifestation of the hereafter, which resides within it as its soul and life. Whoever seeks this world without knowledge of the hereafter is misguided, for their quest lacks meaning and consequence. But whoever seeks the hereafter through this world, believing in it, their quest has meaning, consequence, and is praiseworthy.

The boy, now convinced, asked the scholar to reveal the features of this world and its contents, and to provide parables from within.

The scholar explained that Allah has created nothing in this world—no animal, no bird, nothing wet or dry, no inanimate object like mountains, stones, trees, or minerals, no gold, silver, or gemstones—nothing, great or small, that is not made by parable. Even an atom, despite its smallness and subtlety, has limits: in front, behind, right, left, above, and below. These are six limits, and the seventh is the atom itself. This applies to everything Allah has created, from the ant and mosquito to all that is in the heavens and earth, and everything in between, great or small. The parable is that each has six limits, and the seventh is the proof, pointing to the six origins from which all things were brought into being. The air surrounding them is the seventh, the encompassing one. If one opens their heart to these questions and considers the parables, they will encounter all of them in their inner forms. The scholar invited the boy to ask whatever seemed good to him.

The boy then asked about the likeness of heaven, with its height and breadth above all things, and its encompassing diameters.

The scholar replied that it is like the speaking Imam (الإمام الناطق), who is exalted above all creation by his virtue and high degrees, and who encompasses all rulings and the rulings of the Shari'a by virtue of his knowledge.

The boy then inquired about the likeness of the zodiac and its stars.

They are like the twelve chiefs (النقباء), the chiefs of the speaking Imam. Its stars are his summoners (الدعاة), who call to goodness with the permission of their Lord.

What, the boy asked, is the likeness of the earth and its breadth, which serves as a foundation for creation and is praised?

It is like the gate (الباب) of the speaking Imam during his lifetime, and his successor (الوصي) after his death. It is a guide for the people and a source of resolve for those who seek guidance, for they are knowers of parables and of the inner interpretation (التأويل).

And the twelve islands in the earth?

These are parables of the twelve arguments (الحجج), the arguments of the guardians.

The rivers and springs?

They are like those who call to goodness (الدعاة).

The seven kingdoms of the heavens and their heavens?

These are the causes between Allah and the speaker (الناطق), and they belong to Him. These are the seven, and the orbits (الفلك), for these are the causes of the great Allah.

The seven seas?

These are the causes between Allah and the Imams of the speaker. One should take note of these causes.

The boy pressed on, asking about the salt water and fresh water, and their difference on earth.

The scholar explained that salt water is like the knowledge of the apparent, in which there is no da'wa and no summoners, for salt water yields neither sowings nor anything else. Fresh water is like the knowledge of the esoteric. As for the springs in the earth and the water flowing above it, knowledge is just so: the esoteric is the knowledge of the hidden, the apparent is the knowledge of the summoners, and the inward is the hidden esoteric.

But how, the boy asked, did water come to be like knowledge?

Because water, the scholar said, is the life of every animal. And in just this way, knowledge is the life of every world.

The boy then looked up and asked about the air that surrounds all things.

The air, the scholar said, is too great to be set as a parable.

Is it greater, or are the heavens and the earth as great as it?

Glory be to Allah. He is greater than both. The capacity of the earth within the air is like a ring lying on a flat plain. Whoever has the mind to grasp this image will have proof.

And what is its meaning? the boy asked.

It is proof of the One who has nothing like Him, a refutation of what they associate with Him.

You have brought me to the shores of the seas of the Most High. Return me now to the sciences of this world and the things that resemble them, so that I may use them in my own affairs and grow stronger by them to bear what I am meant to bear. I feared for myself, and I felt sorry for my mind, that it might wander into a country it cannot survive.

You have risen above your own question, and you have done well to feel that fear. The outward meaning by itself is already enough to set your opinion and your mind reeling — what would it be if I drew back the curtain on an inner meaning? You and I would be like Moses and al-Khiḍr walking the shore together, the one shocked by what the other knows.

Is there, beyond this inner, another inner more subtle still?

By my life, the inner is the highest of all dwellings. It is greater in power and more complete than the outward, for it is the ultimate guide along the road of salvation.

Then I see three layers of knowledge here — the apparent, the inner, and the inner of the inner. Is there a proof of this?

Where else would the evidence be except in this very thing, and in what it points to?

Then show me these three layers.

I will teach you from the Book of Allah and from the argument of the intellect. This is what the audience of parables points toward.

I did not imagine there was any doctrine beyond what I had already reached. If only I had commanded — Allah is the greatest of all things, before whom the illusions of the arrogant are humbled and exalted.

As for the Book of Allah, He has said:

يَا بَنِي آدَمَ قَدْ أَنزَلْنَا عَلَيْكُمْ لِبَاسًا يُوَارِي سَوْآتِكُمْ وَرِيشًا ۖ وَلِبَاسُ التَّقْوَىٰ ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ

Q|7:26

That is three: the outward, the inward, and the inward of the inward.

I am convinced.

As for the argument of the intellect — nothing is known except by a name, an attribute, or a meaning. The meaning is what the thing is. The name is what it is called. The attribute is what guides toward it. In just the same way, the religion of Allah has outward names and inward proofs, and neither can be fulfilled except by the other. It is indicated only by a noun or an attribute — as when someone says, "The moon has risen." The meaning is the disk in the sky; "moon" is its name; "has risen" is its attribute. If he says "has risen" and falls silent, nothing has been conveyed. If he says "moon" and falls silent, nothing has been conveyed either. Only when the name and the attribute are joined, and he says "the moon has risen," is the meaning clear and the speech sound.

So too, if you say "apparent," you must say "the apparent of what?" If you say "inward," an answer is owed: "the inward of what?" But if you say, "the apparent of the religion and its inward," the words are understood, and both together become its religion — its apparent and its inward. As for the thing that has the name of an attribute, He said:

وَذَرُوا ظَاهِرَ الْإِثْمِ وَبَاطِنَهُ

Q|6:120

So sin itself became a thing with an outward and an inward. So too is the religion of Allah, the Greatest. The one who says "the laws" speaks of a thing whose outward is outward, and whose inward is inward — each existing on its own. And knowledge itself: its meaning has many parables that point to it, such as the hidden egg, which has an outward — the shell; an inward — the white within; and an inner of the inner — the yolk, which is its essence and its meaning.

Are not this world and the Hereafter a pair, and the soul and the body a pair, and the knowledge of the apparent and of the subtle a pair, and so on — many pairs, all of them?

I accept that. But are they pairs of a single kind?

The pairs all point to the religion of Allah.

I accept that as well. The pair is this: the outward is a name, and the inward is an attribute, and what they signify together is the knowledge of Allah and the religion of Allah — which is the inner. So too is the creation of the three houses of worship: first the creation of the angels, second the creation of human beings, and third the creation of the beasts. The apparent is the knowledge of the limit of beasts; whoever knows only that, without the secret, is like the beasts. The knowledge of the inward is the knowledge of the children of Adam alone; whoever knows it is a believer, like a true human being. The knowledge of the inner of the inner is the knowledge of the angels; whoever knows it has become the spiritual counterpart of the physical knowledge of the body, and he is a prophet sent by Allah in His land, by whom He argues against His creation. He is the veil of the angels, the symbol of revelation and its interpreter for the children of Adam. With him are the keys of Paradise, which only those who obey him will enter; and with him is the permission for the fire, which none enter except by his staff.

The people are only two men. One has been taught the purpose of knowledge, whose soul has been animated by the spirit of certainty — a scholar by his knowledge and a master by his work. The other is a learner, still seeking, working for his own survival. The rest of the people are savages and a rabble who know nothing. The followers of every false guide have been deceived by his error and seduced by his ignorance; they imagine they are doing well, but they have been deprived of the true examples, and they will not be left behind by what Allah has promised them — and the disbelievers will have the like of it.

So this is what virtue is, and this is how knowledge and its exposition unfold. Blessed are the guardians of Allah, and blessed are the followers of benevolence. Will I attain to this great station — the example of houses above all houses?

O my son, this is a station Allah has honored above every house and every degree, and you will only attain it by virtuous work. If the soul does its work, and you seek it with honest certainty and a clear, conscious heart, I have hope that you will realize from it what the saints of Allah, the righteous, have realized.

And who can aspire to attain the likeness of a station to which Allah raises His guardians?

Their Allah is your Allah, and between Allah and anyone else there is closeness only through meeting Him and doing good deeds. The knowledge by which Allah has raised His guardians — you are at its beginning, and if you act on it, you will reach its end. Allah is bound to inform you of the totalities given to the righteous among them, and He will not deprive you of anything He gave them.

Can you help me toward that? Was it not granted to you first?

This is a place where nothing avails except the work of its own people.

How, then, can you help in it at all?

The sower can prepare the land, sow the seed, and water it — but he cannot bring out the plant or the leaves. A man can be a craftsman whenever he wills — but he cannot create from his work whatever he wills. So do not reproach me, my son, that you seek my help in a thing in which I do not yet see you fully established. May Allah expand your chest and illuminate your heart with guidance. Fear Allah and do what is best, for Allah does not waste the reward of the best of deeds. You are already on the path of salvation, the path of guidance, the path of the people of piety — your path, the one you are already walking. Hold fast to the rope to which you have been called, until you are guided by a rope from Allah to the rope of Allah, for that is the goal of the seekers.

Is it not the rope of Allah to which you have called me?

It is the rope made apparent to you. It is your rope and your most trustworthy link, your حجة (argument) — your argument — and the door of your Imam.

Then what is the rope of Allah itself, and what is the rope of the rope of Allah?

That is the end of your guidance and the end of all the worlds.

There is no power except in Allah. The one who has no proof has drowned in the depths of ignorance, just as the one who has no ship has drowned in the seas of the world.

And what is this power I have mentioned, which they cannot possess except by Allah?

We say, لَا أَمْلِكُ لِنَفْسِي نَفْعًا وَلَا دَفْعَ مَا أَكْرَهُ إِلَّا بِاللَّهِ — "It is not for me to bring any benefit to myself, and I have no power to repel what I hate, except by Allah."

Why is it that you say,

لَا حَوْلَ وَلَا قُوَّةَ إِلَّا بِاللَّهِ

Q|18:39

and you do not say "there is no stratagem except in Allah"?

In our sciences we have it that حول (change) is the same حول (change) as in Allah's saying حولين كاملين — "two complete years." It means a year. And the public has shown us that قوة (power) — power — means mules, because Allah says:

وَآتَيْنَاهُ مِنَ الْكُنُوزِ مَا إِنَّ مَفَاتِحَهُ لَتَنُوءُ بِالْعُصْبَةِ أُولِي الْقُوَّةِ

Q|28:76

They said: the عصبة (band) is forty men, and the قوة (power) is the mules. The keys to his treasures filled forty mules.

So by your words "there is no power except in Allah," do you mean there is no year and no mule except by Allah? Is that really the interpretation of your saying and its meaning?

I have no other opinion than this. It is for the weak and the lowly. It resembles something from the words of wisdom, and I do not think the meaning is anything else — for this is stronger in speech and more powerful in the play of imagination. Do they have some inner interpretation of it other than this?

Creation has not uttered any true word except that it has an origin in the subtle, and there is no false word except that it has a meaning. So I return speech to its meaning. Its meaning is that the impossible becomes impossible, and whoever has departed from his proper position has changed — yet the truth, if it is not false, is rather a speech by which a person perceives what follows by analogy and by form, just as he perceives what is in front of him by likeness and parable.

I am convinced. So what is the meaning of these two words, and others like them?

As for الـحول (the Imam) — he is the Imam of Allah and His truthful caliph, the lord of the twelve captains, just as the حول (the year) is completed only in twelve months. The Imam is called حول (Imam) because he يحول (converts) — he converts, he turns — the words of the Creator gently into the words of human beings, until they are understood. They then become apparent speech, and the words of wisdom acquire a body, while the words of the Creator carry within them the spirit of life and the light of salvation. The excellence of the words of wisdom over every other speech is the excellence of the words of the Creator that lie within them. The Imam is called الـحول (the Imam) because of this converting of words, and he does not change them except by the permission of Allah.

And قوة (power) — power — is his حجة (argument) and his door, whom Allah has strengthened to bear the heavy burden of utterance. He takes the speech from the Imam as a whole, and distributes it among the دعاة (preachers), the preachers, in detail, according to their houses. So the حجة (argument) is called قوة (power), because he is strengthened only by Allah. Whoever says "there is no power except in Allah" means: there is no Imam and no حجة (argument) except by Allah, and from Allah comes their light and their sustenance.

As for the عصبة (band), the band, it is the first power — they are the ten arguments. Joseph too said to his father:

يَا أَبَتِ إِنِّي رَأَيْتُ أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كَوْكَبًا وَالشَّمْسَ وَالْقَمَرَ رَأَيْتُهُمْ لِي سَاجِدِينَ

Q|12:4

By the sun he meant the Imam, by the moon his حجة (argument) and his door, and by the planets the twelve captains. Likewise Joseph's brothers said:

لَيُوسُفُ وَأَخُوهُ أَحَبُّ إِلَىٰ أَبِينَا مِنَّا وَنَحْنُ عُصْبَةٌ

Q|12:8

By "band" they meant the twelve captains — let him choose a man from among us. Within them lay the lack of right opinion in the matter of Joseph, peace be upon him. One of them showed envy toward him when Allah favored him. Yet the command of Allah was fulfilled in Joseph, and the outcome of their plot was remorse and repentance before him.

Praise be to Allah, who cleansed them of their impurity and returned them to obedience. What is the meaning of the king's saying — I see seven fat cows being eaten by seven lean ones, and seven green ears of grain and others dry? What are they? And what is a dream-vision itself?

As for the dream-vision, it is like outward work — like a mirage one comes upon and finds nothing. So too is the work of the outward without the inward: nothing of it is accepted of him, however much there is. Likewise the vision in a dream is not realized from what one sees, even if one sees it many times.

The king's saying "I see," in the people's account, refers to seven things turning upon seven. The seven fat cows are the causes of Allah between Him and His creation. The seven lean ones are the seven appointed speakers, the ناطق (speakers), who seek what comes from Allah through His seven causes. The seven green ears are al-Khiḍr — a cause between Allah and the أولياء (guardians), the guardians. The seven dry ones are the guardians, obliged to receive from Allah at the hands of these seven causes. So blessed be Allah, Lord of the worlds.

And what is the like of this among us?

This is the foundation of Islam. Its child is like a grain that sprouted seven ears — meaning that seven or eight imams came out of his loins.

How can Allah forbid this world and say,

وَلَا تَغُرَّنَّكُمُ الْحَيَاةُ الدُّنْيَا

Q|31:33

Allah has spoken truly. He did not say that the world should not tempt you. He said: be not tempted by the life of this world. Life has four meanings. There is a life apparent in this world, whose consequence is annihilation, and there is the life of the Hereafter, whose consequence is permanence. He is saying: do not work for a perishing life — work for the lasting life. This is His saying,

يَا لَيْتَنِي قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِي

Q|89:24

meaning, would that one had sent forward, in the perishing life, something for the lasting life.

And the two lives in another sense: a life of outward knowledge, and a life of inward knowledge. The knowledge of the outward is the life of this world — and that is the lower knowledge — and the knowledge of the inward is the life of the Hereafter. This is why He said, Be not tempted by the life of this world — meaning, do not let yourself be tempted by the outward of knowledge and the acting on it alone. He will only accept from you the inward together with the outward; He does not accept the outward without the inward.

And what of one who knows the inward knowledge but does not know the outward, and does not act on it — what is his standing among the people of religion?

He is at the level of the prophet — but if his inward does not rise up, neither does his outward rise. There may be something good in him; but if he abandons something he was commanded to do, and lets his inward stand alone like the outward of fruits — for if the rind is peeled off the fruit before it is harvested, the fruit is spoiled and cannot be repaired afterward; and if the body is severed, the soul cannot be sustained in it for an hour — then he is in the same case. So religion: if its obligations are disrupted, they cannot be restored to their owner. Even so, his leaving the outward without the permission of the One who imposed it on him cannot escape one of two characteristics. Either he abandoned it from incapacity — and if he was incapable, he was so to the One who commanded him — or he abandoned it in disobedience. The disobedient is a sinner, and the disobedient is not near to the guardians of Allah; rather he is their enemy, and they are his enemies, because he severs what Allah commanded him to keep joined.

And what of one who knows the outward knowledge and acts on it, but does not know the inward — what is his standing among the people of religion?

An evil standing. He is like a body that has been raised up but into which no soul has been breathed. He is counted among the dead, and the dead are the abodes of the disbelievers. Like one who disbelieves in the signs of Allah, he is an enemy of religion and of its people.

I see now that the outward is not valid except by the inward, and the inward is not made good except by the outward; that the world is not lawful except for those who know the Hereafter, which is its true life and its inward; and likewise, religion is not granted to its people unless they establish both its outward and its inward.

Yes — this is the meaning, and this is the work. The fulfilment of all that Allah has commanded is what is worthy of His pleasure, and the abandonment of any of what Allah commanded exposes one to His displeasure.

Then whoever knows the outward together with the inward, and establishes them both — is he a true believer?

Yes. He is a true believer.

Is there not also one who learns and then works for the Hereafter, and along with that takes the world and its good and what swings to him of its gains?

Yes — and the world is not made good except for them. They are the most righteous in this world and its foremost people.

But I do not see the people of this character — the ones who are the scholars among the people — except that these things of the world and what is in it have been pushed away from them, until the ignorant among them suppose that the world is forbidden to them. They are remote from this world; they see of it only a portion, and their souls find it heavy. Their colors have yellowed without obvious cause, their bodies are weak, their skins cling to their bones; they do not eat their food even while they are eating it.

When I heard him mention my brethren and describe them so, I beamed, and my eyes overflowed with tears; at moments I could not speak from the intensity of the lesson. The boy wept at my weeping, until I feared some of our enemies might catch wind of us. I took hold of myself and my heart settled within me, and I turned back to him.

O my son, the people you have described in this way looked upon themselves by the light of life and worked in their affairs with the certainty of salvation, and they did not grow arrogant over themselves. O my son, this world is seen by the eye of wealth and children, and the Hereafter is seen by the eye of knowledge and certainty. O my son, these people's faith in the unseen is sound; they believed the promise and the warning, and they were satisfied; they were terrified by the fear of the threat, so they laboured; they feared the drunkenness of death, so they hastened to good deeds. Their bodies were weakened by the rigor of fasting, and their colors yellowed by the length of their vigils. They do not sleep when the sleepers sleep, out of fear, nor do they eat of sustenance more than a morsel. The pleasure of pondering desires has been inspired in them, and the sweetness of remembrance has distracted them from the appointed hour of sleep. The desires of this world's spectacle have been emptied out of them; they remember death often, so that life does not torment them; they have seen the Hereafter as certain, and they have seen this world only as a fragment. So they refused to work the world, even from what is not forbidden to them.

This is the true news. In this sense the scholars hated this world; and the people were heedless of them, supposing they had forbidden to themselves what Allah had made lawful for them. You have described to me the arrogance of the people of the world in their bodies, and their heedlessness of the Hereafter.

If people would only think about the world, their states would change — from weakness to strength, and from strength back to weakness, from bliss to demise, and from life to death. They would then look at those who passed before them, the vanished centuries: the good and the wicked went down to the same level. They did not see a reward for the good in this world, nor a punishment for the wicked in this world — so they would have known that they had been created for something other than this world. But the world weighed on them, and they did not bear upon themselves the provisions of what was being asked of them. So they took this paltry portion as a substitute, and they were satisfied with the bliss of this world in place of the Hereafter. This is their effort, and this is their meaning; they have no argument before Allah, and no propriety toward His guardians, when the news has already reached them of what is dreadful.

Praise be to Allah, who has guided us to His religion — and we could not have been guided except by His favor. Tell me about the beginning of this creation and their composition: is it a single creation, or different? And why do I see so much difference in opinions and in passions?

As for the creation of bodies, it is different, as you see, in the variety of tongues and colors — and that is a proof of Allah's power, made clear by the signs, so that the created among them can know one another: the child knows his father and the father knows his son; the man knows his wife and no other woman, and the woman knows her husband and no other man — just as Allah created them. But as for the creation of the instrument — namely the senses, what is apparent of them and what is hidden — it is a single creation, and what is hidden inwardly will tell you the same. Have you ever seen a scholar with three eyes as an increase in him, or an ignorant man with one eye as a deficiency in him, so that one might say the scholar saw by way of increase and the ignorant failed to see by way of decrease? Have you seen any increase in the instrument of one over another? No — you see their creation only as a single, level creation in the apparent instrument, and the meaning of the composition is the same. So we have come to know that the inner senses follow this same example. It is justice. Allah would not have been just in what is apparent and unjust in what is hidden, nor would He have made for the scholar two hearts in one cavity, nor deprived the ignorant of anything. The instrument is one, and the composition belongs to the One who gathers it. Thus Allah has argued against them by the very instrument He has made for them, which they did not use. The Almighty said:

لَهُمْ قُلُوبٌ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ أَعْيُنٌ لَا يُبْصِرُونَ بِهَا وَلَهُمْ آذَانٌ لَا يَسْمَعُونَ بِهَا

Q|7:179

Are they unable to bear it, or its burden? No — He is fairer than that.

I do not see it otherwise, and I do not say it. But I am asking you about the difference of passions. Where did they come from, when the instrument is whole and the origin of the composition is the same?

As for the origin, it is one. All of them are from Adam, and Adam is from dust, created by his Lord, who breathed into him of His Spirit. But they deviated from the سنة (practice) by marriage: they were born on a non-religious basis and grew up upon another way. And I do not doubt that among them are children of adulterers who call upon fathers who are not their own. When the origins were corrupted, the branches were corrupted with them; the way back was lost to them, and it became difficult for them to return to the truth — with their arrogance in obedience, their laziness in worship, and their envy of the guardians of Allah — so they no longer held to the limit of the lawful and the forbidden.

If the origin is the same and the instrument is the same, where, then, does the difference of passions come from, when their meaning in seeking is the same — for everyone seeks the good for himself?

By my life — their meaning in seeking is one, but they differ in hitting the mark, like archers aiming at a target. They are unanimous in shooting at it with courage and with the instrument, and they differ only in striking it. So too, the hearts in the breasts and the minds are united, and the minds are united in safeguarding and in securing the dignity of the body. When the call of truth appeared, they differed. Some of them held that answering the Messengers and humbling themselves before them was part of one's dignity toward oneself and the protection of it from folly. Others held that denying them and lifting himself above their obedience was a part of his dignity toward himself and his protection of it from the narrowness of worship. The origin was the same: to seek ease for the body and dignity for the soul. So some of them chose what the Messengers promised them of the reward of Allah — even if it cost them, in this world, what was dear to behold — because of its permanence. Others chose from the bliss of this world, even though it perishes, because of its presentness, the ease of its asking, and the swiftness of its reward. The very hearts by which the first sought the bliss of the Hereafter, by these the second sought the bliss of this world. Each looks at himself, and each supposes himself an adviser to himself. So they came together in the seeking and differed in the striking. This is the justice of Allah, His generosity, and the extending of His giving: each seeker is given what he sought; whoever sought the reward of this world is given from it, and whoever sought the reward of the Hereafter is given from it.

The people of this world have classes within this world: some are wealthy in business, kings of abundant wealth; some are poor in money, serving them to receive from them. Then the bliss of this world brings them together on one side. Are they all alike in evil consequences, or is it for some of them and not the others?

It is of the justice of Allah that He joins them to one another and does not separate them in their forsaking of Him and in their affection for one another. They are equal in His punishment, just as they were, in His disobedience, joined together.

Etymology

حجة (Hujjah)
The root of حجة (Hujjah) is ح-ج-ج (H-J-J), which means to argue, to dispute, or to prove. From this root, we get words like حج (Hajj), the pilgrimage, which involves a journey and a striving, much like an argument strives to reach a conclusion. A حجة (Hujjah) is an argument or proof, something that establishes truth and convinces others. In the Ismaili context, it refers to a high-ranking spiritual dignitary who serves as a proof or argument for the Imam, guiding people to his teachings and authority.

حول (Hawl)
The root of حول (Hawl) is ح-و-ل (H-W-L), which means to change, to transform, or to turn around. This root gives us words like حول (Hawl), meaning a year, as a year marks a complete cycle or turning of seasons. In the text, حول (Hawl) is used to refer to the Imam, who transforms or converts the profound words of the Creator into language that human beings can understand. This transformation is a gentle turning of complex truths into accessible wisdom, much like the turning of a year brings about new phases.

قوة (Quwwah)
The root of قوة (Quwwah) is ق-و-ي (Q-W-Y), which means to be strong, powerful, or capable. From this root, we derive words like قوي (Qawiyy), meaning strong, and تقوى (Taqwa), meaning piety or consciousness of Allah, which implies inner strength and resilience. قوة (Quwwah) literally means strength or power. In the text, it refers to the حجة (Hujjah), who is strengthened by Allah to bear the heavy responsibility of conveying the Imam's message. This strength is not merely physical but spiritual and intellectual, enabling him to distribute the Imam's teachings effectively.

عصبة (ʿUṣbah)
The root of عصبة (ʿUṣbah) is ع-ص-ب (ʿ-Ṣ-B), which means to bind, to tie, or to gather together. This root gives us words like عصب (ʿAṣab), meaning nerves or tendons, which bind parts of the body together. An عصبة (ʿUṣbah) refers to a band, a group, or a company of people who are bound together by a common purpose or kinship. In the context of the text, it refers to a group of strong men or, metaphorically, to the ten arguments or captains who support the Imam and the حجة (Hujjah).

ناطق (Nāṭiq)
The root of ناطق (Nāṭiq) is ن-ط-ق (N-Ṭ-Q), which means to speak, to utter, or to articulate. From this root, we get words like نطق (Nuṭq), meaning speech or articulation. A ناطق (Nāṭiq) is a speaker or one who articulates. In Ismaili thought, a ناطق (Nāṭiq) is a prophet who brings a new divine law or dispensation, articulating the divine message for humanity. The seven appointed speakers mentioned in the text are the great prophets who have brought different cycles of revelation.

أولياء (Awliyāʾ)
The root of أولياء (Awliyāʾ) is و-ل-ي (W-L-Y), which means to be near, to be close, to be a friend, or to be a guardian. From this root, we get words like ولي (Walī), meaning a friend, guardian, or saint, and ولاية (Wilāyah), meaning guardianship or spiritual authority. أولياء (Awliyāʾ) is the plural of ولي (Walī), referring to the guardians, saints, or close friends of Allah. These are individuals who are spiritually close to Allah and are entrusted with guiding others.

سنة (Sunnah)
The root of سنة (Sunnah) is س-ن-ن (S-N-N), which means to establish, to set a course, or to follow a path. From this root, we get words like سن (Sann), meaning a way or a practice. سنة (Sunnah) refers to the practice, custom, or tradition, particularly the established practice of the Prophet Muhammad. In the text, it refers to the established religious basis or practice from which people deviated through marriage, leading to corruption in their origins and branches.

دعاة (Duʿāt)
The root of دعاة (Duʿāt) is د-ع-و (D-ʿ-W), which means to call, to invite, or to supplicate. From this root, we get words like دعوة (Daʿwah), meaning a call or invitation, and دعاء (Duʿāʾ), meaning supplication or prayer. دعاة (Duʿāt) is the plural of داعي (Dāʿī), meaning a caller or preacher. In the Ismaili context, دعاة (Duʿāt) are missionaries or preachers who invite people to the teachings of the Imam and spread the message of the faith.

We find ourselves at a profound crossroads, where the very essence of faith and our place within it are questioned. Imagine a world where people are categorized by their wealth or status. Now, consider if our spiritual journey operates on a similar principle. Does Allah accept our devotion based on our worldly standing, or is there a deeper, more equitable measure?

### Degrees of Faith and Divine Justice

Just as there are different levels in our worldly lives, our spiritual journey also has degrees. This is a matter of divine justice. Allah is worshipped according to His will, the vastness of His sovereignty, and our immense need for Him. His mercy extends to all. He knows our weaknesses, so He accepts what is easy for us to strive for, whether it's a little or a lot of effort in our actions and obedience. Each soul, touched by His boundless grace, holds a unique degree with its Lord. Worship is the path to these degrees.

Allah says:

وَمَن يُطِعِ اللَّهَ وَالرَّسُولَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ مَعَ الَّذِينَ أَنْعَمَ اللَّهُ عَلَيْهِم مِّنَ النَّبِيِّينَ وَالصِّدِّيقِينَ وَالشُّهَدَاءِ وَالصَّالِحِينَ ۚ وَحَسُنَ أُولَٰئِكَ رَفِيقًا ۝ ذَٰلِكَ الْفَضْلُ مِنَ اللَّهِ

Q|4:69-70

This divine favor is attained through sincere effort in obedience, not merely by the quantity of our deeds. The righteous achieve their high ranks purely through Allah's mercy, even if their efforts seem small.

### The Compassion of Allah

It's natural to wonder if anyone could be punished for having little to offer, even if they are not disobedient. Rest assured, Allah is far too just for that. His grace, generosity, and boundless mercy allow even those who cannot perform works, like the insane or small children, to enter Paradise. This is a testament to His immense compassion.

### The Limit of Our Effort

So, what is the true measure of our effort and sincere work that Allah will accept our excuses for what we cannot do? It is the full extent of our knowledge and the utmost reach of our capacity. We are only accountable for what we know and what we are truly capable of.

### The Duty of Knowledge and Action

To truly understand our path, we must grasp the duties of knowledge and action, and their ultimate goals. This allows us to assess ourselves honestly. If we can reach the goal of pleasing our Lord, we press on. Otherwise, we stop at the limit of our capacity, knowing that Allah is the first to excuse us for what we cannot do. We must be wary of our own souls, for while Allah is just and we are safe from His injustice, we must fear the evil within ourselves.

Do not fear where you have found belief, but do not feel secure where you should still fear. Strive for Allah's sake with both your person and your wealth. Allah does not accept what He dislikes, nor does He dislike what pleases Him. He dislikes ingratitude in His creation and is pleased when His servants are thankful and act justly. He accepts only those who come to Him with truthfulness and faithfulness. These are the gardens of His Paradise, and only the people of truthfulness and faithfulness are saved.

### The Goal of Our Work

The goal of our work, and what Allah has made binding upon us, is this: obedience to Allah ennobles us, regardless of our origins, and good work is the crown of those who perform it. Personally, we must fulfill every obligation and avoid every forbidden act. This is the very religion of our soul and the purification of our body. To please Allah and be accepted by Him, we must extend to others what we wish for ourselves and refrain from doing to others what we wish to avoid. This is justice, and Allah is pleased with justice.

### Ease in Religion

Allah, out of His compassion and mercy, has made religion easy for us. He says:

فَمَنِ اضْطُرَّ غَيْرَ بَاغٍ وَلَا عَادٍ فَلَا إِثْمَ عَلَيْهِ

Q|2:173

This means that whoever is compelled to do something without willful transgression bears no sin. Allah has not made religion a hardship for His servants. We must hold fast to His commands and seek His help in fulfilling them.

### The Duty of Wealth

Our wealth is whatever Allah has granted us. We must acquire it only from lawful sources and spend it only in its rightful place. A lawful source means acquisition without wrongdoing or falsehood. The rightful claim upon it is its Zakat (زكاة). From all that comes into our possession, whether little or much, we must take a fifth of the best of it – what we ourselves would love to receive – and pay it to those who have a binding claim upon us. In this way, wealth gives its alms, just as our body gives its alms through work. Work is the gateway to Paradise, and Zakat (زكاة) is purification. Whoever pays Zakat (زكاة) has succeeded.

After fulfilling our Zakat (زكاة), we should spend upon ourselves and our family with kindness. If our bounty exceeds our needs, we must single out the poor among our brethren and extend our favor to all who are willing to receive it, for those without their own merit need the favor of others. We must hasten to every good and rush to every act of bounty, for this is the goal of work, if only we are patient. Patience, and then more patience, is essential, especially when faced with the poverty of those who cannot do without our help. Every needy person practices patience, and every favored person should give thanks.

### The Unveiling of a Jewel

When we truly understand and act upon these principles, it is as if a hidden jewel within us is revealed. This journey brings forth praiseworthy results, transforming our bounty and Zakat (زكاة) into our very work. Such a transformation can move hearts and bring tears of joy.

For those who embody such virtue, scholars will travel far, the earth will rise, and the heavens will remain stable. Kinship bonds will be strengthened, and rain will descend from the clouds. We must build upon what Allah has given us and the heart He has opened to Islam. We receive glad tidings of light from our Lord and a swift opening, accepting Allah's gift and the gains of gratitude that come with great work and noble thanksgiving.

### The Role of the Imam

The true guide, whose right Allah has made binding upon every believer, holds the keys to the heavens and the landmarks of the kingdom. His hands are opened with the light of Sinai. He is the cause behind the signs, and his knowledge calls to the loftiest of ends. Through him, our light is completed, Allah perfects our affairs, and we are blessed. He is the one whose kindness belongs to all, and we are his servants, seeking his pleasure and shunning his displeasure.

An excuse for not knowing is granted only before exposure to such profound knowledge. Once we know, we are bound by it. Our duty is to seek guidance from him, but only with his permission and decision.

### A New State of Being

The journey of seeking knowledge and guidance can transform us, leading to a new state of being. Our physical state might even reflect the intensity of our inner struggle and longing. The absence of our guide can cause grief, and the fear of not meeting him again can weaken us. But Allah knows what is in our hearts and makes the affliction easy. He casts mercy into the heart of our guide, who then helps us prepare for our journey, accompanying us to the door of our goal. There is no power except by Allah.

Such joy and reverence can be overwhelming, almost causing our souls to leave our bodies, until Allah takes hold of us, knowing our condition, and returns us. Then we realize that Allah has not yet withdrawn the mercy of compassion from believers. We offer great thanks to our guide and show our reverence.

### Entering the Council of Wisdom

When we finally reach the greater scholar, our teacher, we seek permission and are admitted. We enter, offer greetings, and sit with his permission. In such a sacred council, we might feel overwhelmed, unable to speak, turning pale from awe. Our guide, however, knows our needs and desires. He takes our hand, and we stay with the master until our guidance is complete, and we reach the end of our hope, understanding what we have and what is required of us. Then we pray and depart.

The master, the lord of the house, ensures we are honored and our needs are met. He expresses his pleasure in us, recognizing us as those whose story is sweet in the hearts of believers. We are surrounded and overtaken by this grace, unsure whom to thank or repay.

### The Dignity of Minds and the Fruits of Inquiry

The Shaykh (شيخ) begins by praising Allah, who opens hearts with His light and is pleased by His justice. Allah's remembrance becomes a kindness and mercy to His servants. He has not burdened creation with what they cannot bear. The affair is set in the middle, between two extremes, which is called justice. His hands are spread with goodness; He does not disappoint those who call upon Him, nor turn away those who are silent. The bounty of the heavens and gratitude due to the Lord are not reserved only for the thankful, for His reward is immense. Nor is there withholding from the ungrateful, for His punishment is great. He shields His blessings from denial and provides documented proofs against it, so the evidence against ingratitude is unlimited, known yet indescribable. He is the most merciful and mightiest, the most just in His ruling, the highest yet closest to His people, the nearest one whom we keep close, banishing illusions. So, we worship Him and give thanks to Him, for to Him we shall return.

Beyond that, it is the dignity of minds to be used through inquiry. One of the fruits of inquiry is what genuinely exists, and a mark of existence is its sweetness. Every freshwater spring has a plain surface but veiled depths; it never tires of the thought that pursues it, and creation never reaches the end of its purpose. Its proper features are its rights, and a right is a duty, calling us to fulfill our obligations. Whoever withholds what is due to a right acts unjustly. Is it not the right of thought to pay Zakat (زكاة) to the hearts through good manners? And the right of good manners is to seek knowledge; the right of knowledge is to be acted upon; the right of action is to be performed in purity, in obedience to the First; and the right of obedience is to be perfected in both hardship and ease, in goodness and in trial. Only the patient and truly fortunate receive it.

### The Naming of the Soul

When asked his name, the boy initially responds, "Ubayd Allah, son of Abd Allah" (عبد الله, ابن عبد الله) – the servant of Allah, son of the servant of Allah. The Shaykh (شيخ) acknowledges this as his description. The boy then declares, "I am free, son of Abd Allah" (عبد الله). The Shaykh (شيخ) asks who freed him, and the boy replies that this world possessed him, and he has returned it to the One who called it. The Shaykh (شيخ) understands, but then asks, "What, then, is your name?" The boy falls silent, puzzled.

The Shaykh (شيخ) explains that a thing cannot know that which has no name. The boy, feeling newborn, asks to be nurtured. The Shaykh (شيخ) instructs him to wait until the completion of seven days for the dignity of the newborn. If the newborn dies before then, nothing harms him; he is named after that. The Shaykh (شيخ) asks if the boy would be like an idol, even though he has been born. The name belongs to the boy, and he belongs to the name. It appears only within his limit and travels with his duration.

### The Day of Good Fortune

After seven days, we seek permission from the Shaykh (شيخ). He instructs the boy to wash and put on his purest garments. The boy's heart feels the need to relieve himself, a sign of the good to come. When he completes what is commanded, we all go out. The boy joyfully repeats, "The day of Sa'd (سعد), and the guardian of Sa'd (سعد)" – the day of good fortune, and its keeper – driven by his intense longing to know what would bring him closer to Allah's pleasure.

Upon reaching the Shaykh (شيخ), we find him engaged in his work. He greets us, and we return the greeting. He orders the boy to come down to him and brings him near. He then speaks of profound matters, beyond human imagination or expression, matters not to be mentioned in public assemblies, unveiled only to His own people.

When he has discharged his duty, guiding the boy out of his bewilderments to the very end of guidance, he explains his doctrine and gives him his draught of his Lord. The boy then removes his ihram (إحرام) by glorifying the sanctities of Allah, takes hold of the rope, circumambulates the ancient House, spends his rites in remembrance, and performs Hajj (حج) by the great sign.

### Etymology

Zakat (زكاة): The root word is ز ك و (z-k-w), which means to purify, to grow, to increase, or to be righteous. From this root, Zakat (زكاة) derives its meaning as a purifying tax or charity, as it purifies the wealth of the giver and fosters spiritual growth.

Shaykh (شيخ): The root word is ش ي خ (sh-y-kh), which means to be old, to be a leader, or to be a venerable person. A Shaykh (شيخ) is typically an elder, a respected scholar, or a spiritual guide, embodying wisdom and leadership.

Sa'd (سعد): The root word is س ع د (s-'-d), which means to be happy, fortunate, or auspicious. The day of Sa'd (سعد) refers to a day of good fortune or happiness.

Ihram (إحرام): The root word is ح ر م (h-r-m), which means to forbid, to make sacred, or to prohibit. Ihram (إحرام) refers to the sacred state a pilgrim enters during Hajj (حج) or Umrah (عمرة), where certain actions are forbidden, and the individual is dedicated to worship.

Hajj (حج): The root word is ح ج ج (h-j-j), which means to intend, to set out, or to argue. Hajj (حج) is the annual pilgrimage to Mecca, an act of worship that involves intending to visit the sacred sites and performing specific rituals.

Imagine a journey, not just of miles, but of the soul, where a young seeker, a boy, walked alongside his spiritual father, the Shaykh. They attended the school of wisdom together, the Shaykh pouring knowledge into the boy, and the boy, like fertile ground, absorbing every drop with unwavering patience and an insatiable thirst for truth. This sacred dance continued for a long, beautiful season. The Shaykh, with the discerning eye of a true teacher, watched and waited, knowing that the roots of understanding were drinking deeply, and the branches of insight were reaching for the heavens.

As the season of instruction drew to a close, the boy, his heart overflowing with gratitude, spoke to the Shaykh. His words were a melody of thanks, a testament to the profound impact of his teacher. He said, "Praise be to Allah, who has elevated you to this station – through you, He revives religion and extinguishes ignorance. You are a fortress for the people and a sanctuary for those like us. You are the gate to the people of heaven and the ascent for the people of the earth. From the radiance of your majesty, lights have emerged; from the cup of your hand, rivers have flowed. May you remain in this blessed state until you are pleased with the One who is pleased with you."

The Shaykh, preparing to send his student forth into the world, responded with a blessing and a charge. He said, "And as for you, may Allah bless you with what He has bestowed upon you, help you preserve what has come to you, and employ you in fulfilling His will. May He illuminate your path with the light of His guidance, expand your heart with the keys of wisdom, and ennoble your station with the method of the call (دعوة). May He complete His favor upon you with the modes of response, and grant you such instruction in obedience to Him that you may have the best hope of His good pleasure. I bid you farewell now, my son, and I give you my permission to depart. The weight of this parting is eased by three considerations. First, we ourselves are now journeying to another country. Second, your long separation from your family, and the fear you carried of leaving without your father's permission – his grief and anger are immense, and only your return will soothe them. Though I am vexed with your father, you must be kind to him. Third, the duty of fulfilling the right of this grace that has reached you, for it is now incumbent upon you to worship by it and to call others to it."

The boy, deeply moved, replied, "Your parting brings me sorrow, and your absence is a deprivation. But your counsel is more rightly guided than my grief, your obedience more binding upon me, your judgment more sound. So recommend to me what you see fit, and dismiss me when you will, and be kind enough to send my father with me back to my country. I will lean upon him. I will act through him. I will entrust to him all my affairs."

"I shall do that," the Shaykh affirmed. Then, like a master entrusting his apprentice with the world, he charged him, "I enjoin upon you the fear of Allah who created you. I enjoin upon you the preservation of this trust for which you have struggled. And I enjoin upon you the safeguarding of your father – who raised you when you were young, and toiled long in your affairs, with his own person and with his wealth. Do not clothe him in the garment of contempt. Do not board him on the ships of harm. Do not omit his name. Do not become distracted from his remembrance. Do not deceive him in your own city – for there is a sting at the very outset of such things."

"May Allah reward you," the boy said, "on behalf of the Master of grace, with every grateful one's thanks. And may He reward you for the long length of your shepherding, where no hand of mine can repay you. I will keep your commandment. Do not cut off my share from the blessing of your supplication."

Then, with hearts heavy with both joy and sorrow, they rose. They shook hands, embraced, and exchanged their farewells. The weight of their parting was so profound that words failed them, and they communicated through gestures – a lifted hand, a turned face, the silent language of the soul. Then, they took their separate ways.

The scholar, the lesser teacher entrusted by the Shaykh to guide the boy back into the world, set out with him towards his home. As they neared the boy's city, with the familiar rooftops of his father's home visible on the horizon, the scholar drew him aside.

"My son," he said, "I have understood the will of the Shaykh, and you have not. All guidance lies in his words. This is your city now. We have come to its very edge. Sit with us here, off the road for a moment, for I wish to remind you of some of my concerns, and to recommend to you what you should now do."

Once seated, the scholar began, "My son, I am aware of your father and of his enmity toward the people of this matter. And I know this together with the fact of your having gone out with me, and your absence from him without his permission and without his trust. I wish to honor your father's view by concealing some of these affairs, so that you may return to him and he may be at ease again and remain with us as a man among us. And I hope – if you are kind to him – that he will be closer to what you want than anyone else. He has been worn down by your absence. His solitude has become frightening to him. Know this, my son: that Allah has opened to you the least of your aspirations even before you suppose yourself to be a fully grown man – before a long stretch of time has passed. Allah has been good to you. So now be good to yourself. Honor the one through whom Allah has honored you. We have thought well of you, and our hope in you has been great. So you must preserve the trust your father entrusted to you, and stand firm in your affair. Piety and devotion are the guardians of your religion and your work. Thanksgiving and patience are an increase to your light. May Allah open your provisions and grant you good fortune."

Then, the scholar presented six profound principles, like six foundational stones for a wall of strength and wisdom. He said, "I commend to you six qualities: Trust in Allah when you are resolved. Speak with a ruling when you are called upon. Do not grow angry when you are wronged. Do not weary if you are slandered. Whoever responds to you with good, be patient with him. And devote yourself to the best of those upon whom you call – for Allah will help you, and Allah will open the way for you."

The boy listened intently, and when he responded, his words reflected the profound richness he felt from the gifts he had received. He said, "May Allah reward you with three goods: an Imam whose argument you have fulfilled and whose trust you have advised. And a dead man whose life I treated with kindness. My heart is full and my wings are light with the joy of the great things you have set before me. I do not know which of your words is the more excellent – the outward, in which you arranged the matter in its sequence, or the inward, in which you expounded its meanings."

"Praise be to Allah," said the scholar, "who has made His way easy for you, and brought you near to it. As I bid you farewell now, and am drawn away from you – is there anything still in which someone might help you carry a portion of his burden?"

"There are three needs," said the boy. "A need I have already fulfilled. A need I beg for. And a need I ask you to kindly fulfill."

"I have understood your three needs," the scholar said, "and I will answer you about what you have asked. As for the need that has already passed: your life is in knowledge after death – and may Allah bless you with a life in it, and grant you the best of what you intended. As for the need for which you pray: act upon what you know, accept what He brings, and Allah will bring it about for you by His mercy. As for the need you ask of me – it was as if you wished for an increase upon what you have already heard."

"There is nothing greater," the boy said, "than your knowing what is within me. So do as you see fit."

"As for my knowing what is in yourself," said the scholar, "only Allah knows the unseen. But the believer sees by the light of Allah. As for the increase – I will make it more luminous for you, and I will stop you upon the foundations on which you have been working, so that you may look back upon all those matters whose outward I have set before you and which you have treated as parable. Whatever in it was praiseworthy is like the truth and its people – the virtues of their virtuous, and the witnesses among them that point to them. And whatever was a reprehensible parable, I spent upon the enemies of the truth and its people – their elders and their young and their children – and the evidence within it points to them. These are the foundations of the science that works by them. If she preserves them, she has no need of any others. And I commend to you the fear of Allah – from whom you have come, and with whom you have done nothing – and the keeping of His covenant, and the fulfillment of the right that He has imposed upon you and upon all His creation."

When he had finished his discourse, when he had informed the boy of his safety and completed his nurture, he looked at him one last time. "My son," he said, "take care of yourself. I have others besides you. And this is the parting between you and me."

At the sound of that word, "parting," the boy gasped, his spirit nearly taking flight, his soul almost leaving his body. He stooped, kissed the scholar's feet, and took the dust from them, wiping it upon his own face, a profound gesture of refusing to let go. Then he said his farewell and turned away.

The boy returned to his house, but his heart was absent, consumed by an unnamed fear. He was overwhelmed by grief over the parting from his elder father, his second father, the very source of the grace that had reached him. He spent his days and nights agitated with sorrow and alarm, until his family and neighbors grew afraid for him.

Then his father entered, filled with anger.

"My son," the man said, "is this the reward of sons to their fathers? I taught you only the best that a child can be taught by a father. You did not deny your own self, nor renounce your beliefs, until the very hour I grew old with this strange man. You drew him in upon me. You crossed over with him in his affair without ever consulting your parents. If what I knew was true – then you have hindered me in my very self, because you concealed the truth from me. And if it is false – then you have disobeyed me in your own ruin, and whatever has reached him has by that same path reached me."

The boy answered him gently, "My father, I have shown you nothing but mercy, and nothing but compassion. You raised me as a mercy from you. You nourished me. You chose me over family and over wealth. I have no need to be so unkind to you in this day of ours that I should make you the object of one of two strands of gratitude that are owed to you."

"What," the father said, "is the difference between the two strands?"

"Either," said the boy, "you allow me to offer you my courtesies and my favors – for I am still your son, the one you raised young, and the one whom you have commanded us to attribute to Allah. Or you grant me leave to debate with you, and to give you my full confidence – so that I am safe from your indignation, and so that I may draw upon your mind."

The father considered. "As for your courtesy on the outside, while hearts differ within," he said, "that is itself a reproach between me and you. How long can outward agreement stand when its underpinnings differ? I do not want that. As for your debate, and your candor – you have that. So say what seems best to you."

And the boy said: "Have you seen my standing with you, for forty years, to be anything other than one of two strands? Either you are a scholar – and then you have withheld from me the teaching I am owed, and you must not blame me if you have driven me to seek my own salvation through another. Or you are ignorant – and you are excused with me, for ignorance is forgiven – but you needed a man who was a stranger to me. And so I have gone ahead of you to him only because of your age, and the lateness of your birth into this matter, for I have heard his words just as you have heard them, and I have seen what you have seen."

The father's heart was torn open by the boy's words. He knew there was no escape from any of these arguments, no door he could close against them, and his eyes overflowed with tears.

"My son," he said at last, "the argument that has been made against me is yours. And you will share a similar fate with me, if you keep your own affairs secret from me. Remember this for me. If what you say is true, I will accept the dignity of it from you for myself. And even if it is false, I have already rejected it for your sake, out of my compassion for you."

So they debated, at intervals. They disclosed things to each other at length, the son to the father and the father to the son, until at last they were guided together onto the right path – acting upon the religion of Allah, upholding it, holding fast to it, calling others to it, and cooperating in it. And through the two of them, Allah revived many of His creation. He sent the boy out into the world to preach, with his father's blessing now upon him, asking others to come to him. The boy returned to those others, and became a cause for their guidance. And Allah blessed the people of that whole country through these two men, and at their hands. Allah commanded what He commanded – and slander was disappointed, and went away with nothing in its mouth.

Let us explore a profound story about faith, seeking knowledge, and the unwavering truth. It is a tale that reveals how the same divine message that brings light to a family can also be presented to those outside, and their response unveils their true character.

**The Story of Salih and Abu Malik**

There once was a young man named Salih, son of al-Bakhtari. Their family was highly respected, known for their excellent manners and high standing in their community. Their reputation, along with the news of their newfound faith, spread far and wide. This news eventually reached a group who still adhered to their ancestral religion, the one they were born into before this new faith arrived.

Al-Bakhtari had long been influential among these people, almost like a brother in their old religion. They feared losing his influence now that he had embraced a different path. Troubled, they sought out their own scholar, a man named Abu Malik, known for his pride, numerous rulings, and deep knowledge of their faith. They called him "the heel of the inks," signifying his foundational role in their religious understanding. He was their ultimate authority.

They approached Abu Malik, expressing their concern: "O Abu Malik, do you not see what the boy has gone over to?"

"Which boy?" he asked.

"Salih, son of al-Bakhtari."

"And what has he gone over to?"

They explained that a stranger had arrived, inviting people to a new doctrine and creed. Salih had gone to this stranger without his father's permission, returning later to speak the stranger's words and advocate for his teachings. They heard that al-Bakhtari had embraced his son's views and even given him his garments, symbolizing his acceptance. They felt overwhelmed and had kept their distance for three reasons:

First, al-Bakhtari had been like a servant to them due to the religious brotherhood he had fostered. They feared estrangement after such intimacy and hated division after brotherhood.

Second, if this new path was the truth, they felt they had failed to follow and support it.

Third, if Salih and his father had gone astray, they had failed to guide them back and uphold the truth. They sought Abu Malik's counsel, promising to abide by his opinion.

**Abu Malik's Wisdom**

Abu Malik's response holds a crucial lesson. He advised them to "Judge gently." He acknowledged al-Bakhtari and Salih's past piety and their long-standing rights and virtues. He reminded them that this news had come to people who had followed an argument and found satisfaction in it. He cautioned against judging them as mistaken, which would be wronging them, or judging them as guided, which would be wronging themselves by blindly imitating them. He also stated that they could not dismiss them as deceived without true knowledge, as they were nobler than that.

He emphasized the need to approach this news with a fair eye, the light of justice, and a steadfast mind, quoting: "He who is steadfast in matters keeps his authority secure, and he who is hasty in his affairs cannot keep his foot from slipping."

Abu Malik drew a powerful analogy: a flaw in food is easily remedied, but a breach in religion is profoundly damaging, like a flaw in medicine itself, which cannot be repaired. He stressed that the greatest treasure is the heart and the acquisition of good morals, and among these fruits is the way of judgment. Judgment, he explained, is the crown of the learned, elevating them to the highest glories. He urged them not to waste the bounty of judgment and learning they had acquired, especially in this matter concerning their brothers. He believed there was no good in abandoning them without examination and reasoned discussion, with both seekers in agreement, free of doubt and accusation.

He then presented three possibilities for Salih and his father:

1. They were still on their known religion, and what had happened was merely an increase in literature, hadiths, poetry, and words.
2. An increase had come to them within their shared religion, and they rejoiced in it, for knowledge is a lost treasure for every believer, and its increase is always better.
3. They had received new guidance through a prophet, whom they had clearly followed. This, he noted, is not denied by Allah's command, for creation never fully grasps Allah's decree or will.

He cited the Quran:
كُلَّ يَوْمٍ هُوَ فِي شَأْنٍ
Q|55:29

This silenced them. They confessed that they had come with angry hearts, ready to condemn Salih and his father as unbelievers and even consider harming them. But Abu Malik's words had humbled their hearts.

**The Argument for Truth**

Abu Malik clarified that his argument was not for Salih and his father, but for the truth itself. He warned them not to betray the truth in their absence. He explained that by defending them, he was defending them against falsehood and against blindly condemning the unseen. Otherwise, they would be like the tyrants of past nations who, in their pride, believed they had encompassed all of Allah's commands and knowledge. They wrongly assumed Allah would send no more messengers after theirs, failing to consider that knowledge and doctrine might exist beyond their reach. They presumed to cut off Allah's argument against His creation and His vicegerents on earth, limiting Allah's knowledge to their own minds. This led them astray, causing them to disregard Allah's commands and even kill His prophets. He implored them not to follow the whims of those who had gone astray before them, for true intellect discerns truth from falsehood.

They assured him of their obedience and asked him to lead them to Salih so they could understand his doctrine. If it was reasonable, they would follow; if not, they would avoid it.

Abu Malik responded that this indicated doubt in their own beliefs, and one who doubts knows neither their own truth nor the falsehood of another. They insisted they stood firm on their truth and condemned falsehood.

"That is not how meaning is sought," he said, "nor is there guidance through lying."

They asked how to seek if they were not to deny falsehood and believe truth.

He explained that if they already knew and accepted the truth, and knew and rejected falsehood, then they were not seekers but rather learned individuals, ruling by revelation.

They maintained their conviction in their truth and exempted themselves from the seeking process.

Abu Malik then challenged them: "Have you not agreed, then, that they should be granted favor over you in one respect, so they should be credited over you in many? If you will not consent to be in need in the houses of the seekers, you will sit in ignorance in the houses of the seekers."

They questioned how Salih and his father had been preferred over them.

He explained that Salih and his father were preferred in their knowledge of what they already knew, and even more so in what they did not know. He emphasized that refraining from seeking knowledge altogether is utter ignorance and a defect, for the knowledgeable must teach, and the ignorant must learn.

**The Doors of Seeking**

They acknowledged his wisdom and asked him to open a door for them to act.

Abu Malik warned against marveling at one's own opinion, calling it blindness, and neglecting to seek, calling it error. He stated that there is no shame in seeking, but a seeker must know the "doors of seeking" to rightly know what they seek. One who seeks truth without knowing how to seek it is quicker to deny it. Falsehood, he explained, often appears acceptable, while truth often brings calamity and contradicts whims. Only a sound heart, he said, can refrain from passion and patiently endure calamity.

They eagerly asked about this "knowledge of seeking" and what constitutes a "sound heart."

He explained that the knowledge of seeking requires recognizing one's own need and humbling oneself before what is needed. A sound heart, he clarified, does not stubbornly deny what is said to it, nor is it arrogant about what it already holds. Truth reveals itself, while falsehood exposes its own emptiness. A sound heart discerns falsehood through intellect and accepts truth through acceptance.

They mentioned that one who does not verify truth or refute falsehood is ignorant in their actions, unable to follow truth or avoid falsehood.

Abu Malik responded that such a person has gone beyond the limit of liars, becoming "two ignorant men in one." They agreed he was ignorant, not a liar.

He distinguished between ignorance and lying: the ignorant person hopes for good and seeks knowledge, while lying is the worst of commodities, offering no guidance and earning no belief.

They accepted his words, recognizing the defect of lying and resolving never to lie. They also understood the door of seeking through reverence for what is sought. They asked if this reverence should be shown before or after the statement is given.

"Rather before the statement," he said, "so that you may grasp the statement with reverence."

They wondered if their reverence would be wasted if the statement turned out to be misleading.

"No," he replied. "It was reverence to Allah and humility before His command, and you will have its reward, even if the one before whom you humbled yourself did not deserve it. The seeker has fulfilled the duty of the request even if he has not fulfilled the duty of the truth — like a man who seeks something from some king, and is able to ask only with reverence. If the king gives him something of what he asked for, the king fulfills his duty of grace and earns the reward of what he gave. If he gives nothing, the petitioner has at least discharged the duty of asking gently, while stinginess obliged the other to withhold. So too with the seeker of knowledge: he must begin with humility, persistence, and patience in adversity, until he has fulfilled the duty of requesting — and then time will bring him what it brings."

They accepted this as clear truth and asked permission to visit Salih.

Abu Malik agreed, stating that he would not have troubled himself if not for the fear of turning them away from Allah's path. He offered to go with them, finding solace in their company and thanking them for dedicating him to this goodness.

They sensed he had anticipated this matter.

He clarified that while he hadn't anticipated this specific matter, he always expected deliverance from Allah, hoping for good, listening to every speaker, and seeking an increase from their words, whether right or wrong.

They understood seeking increase from truthful words but questioned seeking it from false words.

He explained that an increase of truth upon truth is light, and recognizing falsehood for what it is increases certainty, which in turn increases the light of the heart. Only people of certainty, he said, will be saved with Allah.

He then stood, declaring his intention to go to Salih. He urged anyone aware of a sin to repent to Allah, anyone with a debt to repay it, and everyone to purify themselves and put on the "garments of the request" with the intention of the needy. He believed that if they approached Allah with truthfulness, He would help them attain His truth.

They agreed to prepare in three days.

"No," he said.

"Two days, then?"

"Only today, if you will do it. Otherwise I go on alone."

They repented to Allah, set aside their debts, and prepared that very day, affirming: "There is no power except in Allah."

**The Encounter with Salih**

They set out together and arrived at al-Bakhtari's gate. After greetings, Abu Malik addressed al-Bakhtari: "O Abu Salih — how is the good son? And where is he?"

Al-Bakhtari replied, "Today my father is good, and I am the son of Salih. And he is good with his Lord."

Abu Malik, thinking Salih had died, recited the verse of return, "To Allah we belong and to Him we return," and asked if the boy had passed away.

"Salih does not die, and will never die," said al-Bakhtari. "It is no figure of speech. The eternity remains."

Abu Malik turned to his companions, noting that the meanings of three qualities had been transposed: the child became the father, the Lord was on earth, and the one who created annihilation became eternal. He called it a "whiff of perfume for perplexed students," hinting at deeper wonders.

Al-Bakhtari then asked Abu Malik his need, observing that his question already placed him at the threshold of an ordeal.

Abu Malik accepted that the first truth is an ordeal and stated his need was to meet the righteous servant.

Al-Bakhtari said there was no way to that, but asked if he meant his son, Salih. Abu Malik confirmed. Al-Bakhtari said it was with Salih's permission.

The elder immediately went to Salih, informed him of Abu Malik and his companions, and spoke of their intellectual virtues. He prayed: "O Allah, open the hearts of Your servants and guide them by Your mercy to the signs of what is sought; and expand our chests with the light of Your veiled light." He then cleared the council for them and sent word back through his father.

Upon entering, greeting Salih, and sitting in order, Salih's first words were: "O Abu Malik, you have favored us with your visit and outstripped us to goodness with your generosity. Yet you were more deserving that we should have visited you, to preserve your affection and to discharge the duty long owed to you."

Abu Malik praised Salih's virtuous nature, acknowledging his past honor and his current role as a caller and warner to good.

Salih then challenged Abu Malik, saying, "Their nation has come to me, O Ka'b al-Ahbar. Is it imitation, then? Where is your perfect mind and the noble understanding I have always credited to you?"

Abu Malik affirmed that Salih's stature was too high for mere prestige, and religion stood above imitation.

Salih questioned how he could be a warner when a warner is a prophet, and a prophet is an argument from Allah for and against creation. How could he be an argument for Abu Malik if not against him?

Abu Malik responded that he had come to believe, recognizing it as an obligation. He stated that he saw the truth above all else and that one who establishes themselves as a veil for religion and an Imam for truth opens their doors to seekers. Having learned the virtue of asking, he sought Salih out for his need.

Salih then asked if one whose virtue Abu Malik had come to know was also one to whom he owed a debt. Abu Malik affirmed this as obligatory.

Salih further inquired if, in knowing the virtue of asking, Abu Malik also knew its truth.

"And what is its truth?" Abu Malik asked.

"To know its faces — and then to seek what is obligatory upon you of the truth in each."

"What are its faces, and how many are they?"

Salih explained that seeking has three faces: a seeker who knows, a seeker who does not know, and a seeker who knows that he does not know. He asked Abu Malik which of the three he was.

Abu Malik admitted he needed to know all three, unable to claim a station he didn't understand.

Salih questioned how he could claim to be a seeker at all if he didn't know the faces of seeking. Abu Malik asked him to explain them.

Salih drew an analogy with seeking money, which has three kinds: seeking what is one's right, seeking what is owed on terms, and seeking alms. Similarly, the treasures of the Hereafter are sought in three ways:

1. The seeker who is a knower, a divine scholar, who seeks out the ignorant to revive them with knowledge.
2. The knowing seeker, a learned person who has mastered certain degrees of knowledge and seeks their full extent.
3. The ignorant seeker, who knows nothing beyond his need and asks scholars to gain knowledge.

"Yes," Abu Malik said, "these are the faces of seeking, in religion and in the world. As for me, I am the knowing seeker who knows nothing — only that he is in need. So make me know."

Salih then asked if Abu Malik came knowing by report or by direct sight. Abu Malik asked the difference.

Salih explained that knowledge by report is news, while knowledge by direct sight is eyewitness.

Abu Malik acknowledged this as another thing one needs to know.

Salih elaborated: knowledge by report is when an ignorant person learns of their ignorance from a scholar. Knowledge by direct sight is when a person already knows in their mind that they are asking about something but does not know the answer. Their chest is constricted by ignorance, and they hope for relief only through knowledge. Thus, their ignorance itself becomes a knowledge of their mind. Ignorance is a need, poverty is the narrowness of need, and distress is a great need that compels one to seek capacity, which is the breadth of knowledge. By this, Salih knew Abu Malik was in need.

Abu Malik confirmed he felt everything Salih described, recognizing his dire need.

Salih observed Abu Malik's haste in seeking before it was firmly established.

Abu Malik explained that poverty and distress had hastened him, and knowing Salih possessed capacity, he had asked.

Salih questioned how Abu Malik knew of his capacity and what was required of the request.

Abu Malik replied that he had been graced by Salih's grace and had come to ask.

Salih challenged him: how could he truly know Salih's capacity without first knowing it himself? And how did Salih know he was the one sought? Abu Malik had not seen Salih with a prophet's scholar or witnessed proofs from him. Salih warned against lifting the request to mere imitation, calling it a wrong against wisdom and oneself. He compared it to leaning on the bottom rail of a cage, where one's own injustice is compounded. He also likened taking knowledge as a tradition without gratitude to disbelief.

Abu Malik understood but hoped the truth would prove itself, wishing to avoid disputes that breed grudges.

Salih agreed that truth proves itself by its merit, but where rivalry is abundant, hearts are occupied with whoever speaks first. He emphasized the scholars' teaching that knowledge, like a seed, will not grow unless the heart is purified for it by argument.

Abu Malik invited Salih to present any arguments, comparing words of wisdom to a jewel whose light shines when turned over and whose bounty does not fade.

Salih believed him, but only if the jewel was genuine, not camouflaged glass. He explained that when disputes multiply and religious conflicts become worldly tyrannies, they disguise the essence of truth. The seeker is then obliged to test the essence to reveal its true worth and expose the camouflage.

Abu Malik asserted that he had upheld justice and truth. He rejected camouflage, adhering to the learned opinion of the "correct essence of speech." He challenged Salih to show a meaning more inward than essence, promising to prefer such jewels. He asked Salih to reveal the virtue of his essence through an ordeal, as they had already known and made known their own essence.

Salih responded that their "correct essence" was generous to grant, but when presented to "essence-traders" and "people of metals," it was tested in the forge and rejected as essence. They called it camouflaged glass and pointed out its defects, causing it to fall from the limits of essence. True essence, he explained, rises by its preciousness, recognized by people of insight who give thanks for it and compete for it, driving its price to every sum. Only the arrogant, who neither buy nor sell, reject it. Similarly, knowledge is not true until presented to those who know the science of heaven. If accepted, it is true knowledge; if not, it is nonsense. Idleness is not permitted in wisdom.

**The Nature of Religion and Sharia**

Abu Malik then raised a crucial point: "And of those who know the science of the heavens — the prophets and messengers? We know of none of them in our time. The people have had no covenant with such men since revelation was cut off by the books. The nations are no longer condemned except by reports. What do you say to this?"

Salih declared that any religion established only on narration is not a true religion. Religion, he asserted, is valued by Allah and admits no difference, while narrations, due to their disagreements and mutual denials, have not been revealed as a single body of truth. Allah's religion, he said, is dearer than that.

Abu Malik argued that if narrations differ, they refer back to the books, and those who agree with the books are accepted, while those who disagree have lied.

Salih countered: what if the books contain only general principles? Who will make them specific? And what is detailed in them, who will deliver it? Who is trusted in matters of Allah's lawful and forbidden? He reminded Abu Malik of a past incident where a man, Maqrub, came to him having broken an oath of divorce. Abu Malik had presented conflicting opinions from different people and told the man to act on whichever he wished, leaving him uncertain about the lawfulness of his actions. Salih questioned if this was how rulings worked in Allah's religion.

Abu Malik admitted that the Sharia of his age had been lost, corrupted, and disrupted, leaving them with only the name of religion. He then asked about the state of Salih's religion.

"And what is religion, O Abu Malik?" Salih asked.

"Command and prohibition, the lawful and the forbidden, the Sunnah and the obligations," Abu Malik replied.

"And what is Sharia, in your view, other than this?"

"I do not know of anything beyond what I have described to you."

"So you have combined religion and Sharia into one thing?"

"Yes."

"Then religion has been disrupted by the suspension of the Sharia, and corrupted by its corruption — what is left with you?"

Abu Malik was silent, contemplating his own religion. Finally, he raised his head and confessed, "I do not see that anything is left with me. So what religion do you have?"

Salih declared, "Religion is with me, and I am not veiled from it. The corruptors opened its doors to the seekers, and the seeker remembered the first one who brought it and followed in good; he sought it, and through it works were honored, and through its justice the laws flourished. It is the cause that joins heaven to earth, unbroken — the loop of the firm handhold, the rope of those who hold fast, the ark of tranquility, the ship of life, the light of life, generous in its content, safeguarded in its sciences, marked in its jewels, its virtue hidden from no one who looks upon it."

Abu Malik then asked how this religion, if lost, could be described.

Salih responded, "The good is only good, O Abu Malik. Place your light in the lamp-niche of your mind and grasp what is meant for you. You may have rights, and the robes of arrogance may have dazzled you, and you may have taken upon yourself what is not your own — what you have imitated from the ways of those who follow you in charity, so that they listened to your speech, accepted your guidance, trusted your opinion. It is your duty to protect yourself and them from a fire you cannot avoid passing through; so seek a way to escape it. You are the most fortunate of them in Allah's grace and the most obligated to thank Him, for your task is to know the good and to hope for its reward. As for what you asked — confirming what I have called you to — it is incumbent on you to establish it from the aspects on which it can be founded."

Abu Malik acknowledged the truth of Salih's words, admitting that common narrations were not valid for religion, leaving only the knowledge Salih called them to.

Monotheism cannot be corrupted because it has no opposition, and justice is not corrupted because it is the judgment between Allah and His creation. This allowed seducing devils to mislead, powerful individuals to kill prophets, and corrupt jurists to adorn themselves and destroy others. They despaired of prophets, forcing them to call aloud to truth and be called liars, leading to their angry killings.

The nation of the Magi, clinging to tyranny and idol worship, claimed Allah would send no messenger after theirs.

2. They have consensus among themselves and with Abu Malik's community, giving them the merit of being truthful.
3. Abu Malik's community testifies that their prophets are truthful and messengers of Allah, while none of them testified for any of Abu Malik's prophets. They have standing against Abu Malik's community by their unanimity that their knowledge is copied from the origin of their knowledge.

This, Salih concluded, is their argument. He emphasized that justice in creation's rule is obligatory upon the Creator, and the judgment of these people is obligatory. If the Creator abrogates and confirms as He wills, no one's ruling outranks His. Every man's saying falls away, and the Creator is foremost over creation and command. He cited the Quran:
كُلَّ يَوْمٍ هُوَ فِي شَأْنٍ
Q|55:29

Abu Malik admitted that his community had gone far astray.

Salih added that most of the first two nations went astray before them, casting off Allah's guardians, abandoning their rights, and allowing the foolish to prevail. They deliberately made lawful what Allah had forbidden, lest they imitate prophethood and people lose track of prophets and kill them. Instead, they distracted them with kingship and indulgence. Those known for honesty and piety on the surface helped them, until the community was stripped of those who commanded good and led toward those who forbade it. They called partisans of falsehood Allah's guardians, turning truth into falsehood. Were it not for the remnants of Allah's guardians and His justice on earth, and the revelation that comes to them, only a few honest ones would remain against the many false. Otherwise, how could Allah distinguish evil from good except by His servants? And how could Allah's argument be conveyed to all creation except through them?

Abu Malik acknowledged that there was no escape from Allah's justice and command. With the multiplicity of opposites, only Allah's just servants remain, and through them, Allah's argument against His creation is upheld.

Salih then established that the company of Allah's prophets, the just ones, includes one called "the just." Abu Malik affirmed this.

"It has been established for you, then, that the just one is the guardian of God — to the extent that you say it as a description, and the description holds?"

"Yes — this is true as a saying."

"And whoever does not understand the one being described — will the description benefit him?"

"The description does not benefit him except by understanding the described."

"Then whoever does not know the just one of God and His guardian — will his speech be just?"

Abu Malik smiled, a smile of one beaten by something he loves. "You have brought me out of my chapter on justice and monotheism," he said, "and what has brought me out is the truth itself, in the clearest of arguments and the kindest of speech. After this, what chapter remains to be proved?"

"From here, O Abu Malik," Salih said, "the camouflaged glass falls away from those who deal in the colors of jewels — they could not recognize its virtue. When the truth is set before me, the essentialists are exposed, and those who are deceived by them are exposed."

Abu Malik believed and acted accordingly. He then asked how they could be safe from the seducers of nations and know Allah's causes, or the cause among them, when they did not perceive them in their time, and in their time there were neither prophets nor messengers as in previous nations.

"Glory be to God, O Abu Malik," Salih exclaimed. "You keep speaking of the justice of God at every moment of your life — yet as soon as We have proved it to you in your own presence, where it is obligatory upon you, you turn back and say you would be deceived about God, and attribute injustice to Him."

"God forbid — whoever says such a thing has transgressed. He has lied against God. Where did you take me as committed to that?"

Salih challenged him: "You say the age of the prophets has passed and that in our time there is neither prophet nor messenger. Was the sending of those earlier two ranks simply for the sake of being worshipped — or was it in vain?"

"May God forbid — only what is just issues from Him."

"Then what has excluded us from that justice? Are we not worshippers as they were? They were created as we are created; we are bound in service as they were bound. Has the obligation been lifted from us after it was binding on them? Or has the giving run out in our time, so that we cling to Him only by virtue of what was given to them? Or has the Sunnah of God changed in our case after being firmly established in those who came before us — so that we should judge that God has changed? Or is justice a duty upon us, while its instrument is left lawful for us to neglect, when it was forbidden to those before us — when there is no substitute for the words of God and no overruling of His judgment? And who deserves the name 'just' except the guardians of God? Justice is not found among those God created, except in those whom God Himself chose — they became just by His command. That is why a judge is called 'just' for the sake of the one who appointed him, and a true word is called 'truthful' because of the truthfulness of the one who speaks it."

Abu Malik agreed. He stated that they were not permitted to say anything of Allah except the truth. If they followed their own opinion in speech and judgment, in the absence of the rightly-guided, they would abandon His judgment. This, he believed, was part of Allah's ruling and Sunnah with them, just as it was with those before them during the interregna between messengers, like the period between Abraham and Moses, and between Moses and Jesus. They had been on the same footing, relying on traditions and Sunnah during the interregnum.

**The Myth of Interregna**

"The religion of God is too precious to Him for that," Salih countered. He questioned the existence of an interregnum after Abraham's religion, pointing out that Allah sent Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Jonah, and Shu'ayb after him, inspiring them with righteous deeds and the call to Abraham's religion, establishing prayer and zakat, until the Torah was given to Moses. He further questioned any interregnum between Moses and the Gospel, noting that Allah sent Joshua, Elias, Talut, David, Solomon, Zechariah, and John after Moses, who upheld the Torah's ruling and called to it until the Gospel was revealed to Jesus. Salih emphasized the abundant evidence of prophets and messengers, whose full count only Allah knows, and who brought proofs. Allah did not abandon them but set parables and called them to truth. He then cited the Quran:
عَلَىٰ فَتْرَةٍ مِّنَ الرُّسُلِ
Q|5:19

Salih explained that this verse confirms the messengers, and the period after the call's rise came after fear, so they prayed in secret. Why, then, would they cut themselves off from the earth? Allah has made it so that they are either openly present or hidden in fear, veiled.

Abu Malik accepted this as an irrefutable statement, a proven word without doubt. He acknowledged the established proof of Allah's argument through justice and the succession of messengers. He then asked about the meaning of their absence in their own time.

Salih asked for the reason behind Moses' absence when he fled Pharaoh's city in fear until he reached Shu'ayb's land.

Abu Malik explained that Moses fled from Pharaoh's people, hiding from them, fearing for his life as they had agreed to kill him.

Salih then asked if Abu Malik had heard of any of Allah's just ones or sons of His prophets being killed while patiently bearing the burdens of religion.

"Yes — many of them have been killed," Abu Malik replied.

Salih then questioned the justice of this age: is there one whom Allah sends down after death, or one fleeing whom they would force back to the killing he escaped, when he has followed Allah's example in His prophets and messengers, patient under fear and concealment until Allah judges between him and them?

Abu Malik stated that there is no shame on one who follows the Sunnah of the righteous by fearing for himself and fleeing from his people. The shame, he said, is on the wicked nations and helpers of darkness for killing Allah's prophets and just ones, and for their unanimous agreement on that act.

Salih asked about the end of one who kills Allah's just one and His proof, and of one who helped him.

"Both are in the Fire — and so too is anyone who raised a banner or carried a flag in their cause," Abu Malik replied.

Salih then asked about the jurists' opinion on this.

"They show mercy to the slain, but see no disobedience in the killer," Abu Malik admitted.

Salih questioned if one who voluntarily obeys the killer is not a partner in the blood of the slain.

"Yes, by God — he is his partner, and even more wicked than he," Abu Malik affirmed.

Salih pressed further: if his companionship is acknowledged in helping the killer, how is he not worse than him?

Abu Malik confessed that were it not for the narrations of the jurists, no tyrannical king could ever be established.

"You have spoken the truth, O Abu Malik," Salih said. "I have laid this on you yourself: with their king, their house, their land, and their soldiers stationed as your neighbors, while we have been taken as their slaves. And you are their just ones — you judge them by your knowledge, you uphold their kingship, you have called them by your questioning, and you have thanked them for what you needed from them. You placed on their stockings the crown of your justice, and they grew proud; you covered their clay with the robes of the sacred law, and they went too far; you lit up their falsehood with the idleness of your truth. So the weak were enraged by your conduct — those who were scholars like you — and they said, 'These are the jurists of the umma; we have in them an example.' You went astray by your own reasoning, and you led others astray by the reasoning of the mighty. You supposed the one really in office was someone other than yourself, and you forgot the words of God Almighty —"

وَلَا تَرْكَنُوا إِلَى الَّذِينَ ظَلَمُوا فَتَمَسَّكُمُ النَّارُ
Q|11:113

"So you severed what God commanded to be joined, while seeing it, and you spoke about the outward forms of justice while being confused by it. You came across His saying —"

أَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ وَأُولِي الْأَمْرِ مِنكُمْ
Q|4:59

"— and you made these darknesses your authorities, even when they disbelieved. And you forgot the words of God Almighty —"

قَاتِلُوا الَّذِينَ يَلُونَكُم مِّنَ الْكُفَّارِ
Q|9:123

"— so you disobeyed those you were commanded to obey, and obeyed those you were commanded to fight, all the while thinking you were doing well. So you have nullified and gone too far. Your opinion has even reached the point of claiming that God will not send a messenger or a warner in your age. Is it that the goodness of your deeds has put you beyond need of the guardians of God for the argument? Or has the ugliness of your deeds cut you off from hope in God's acceptance of repentance? Or is it just to judge God without justice — and to be satisfied for your Messenger with what you would not be satisfied with for yourselves in his place? Would any one of you accept that his fifteen-year-old son be killed, or that his house and money be taken from him unjustly?"

Abu Malik affirmed that no one would be satisfied with such injustice.

Salih then stated that Allah and His Messenger have a right for them to be angry on their behalf if His vicegerent is killed and His guardian is driven from the land.

Abu Malik expressed that it was dear to them that Allah's guardian be killed or driven from his land, but they had deliberately absented themselves, and the mighty had appeared in their place.

Salih countered that had they supported Allah's friends, they would have appeared. Had they failed His enemies, they would not have prevailed. He explained that the verdict binds them to Allah's guardians in three ways: the killer of the ruler tirelessly seeks and is not healed, content with a stinging bite out of anger; they do not stir up their enemy or turn from them even in heedlessness; and out of hatred, believing in love for Allah's guardians, they lack insight into faith and trustworthiness in confidentiality. For these reasons, Allah's guardians have absented themselves from them by taqiyya (dissimulation). For this reason, also, great creatures who took their desires as their god and seized worldly goods committed acts on par with killing prophets. Ignorant of their fault, they did not hold themselves accountable or humble themselves in repentance. Instead, they attributed their injustice to Allah, claiming He would send no more messengers after thirteen, thus making the killing of Allah's guardians lawful unjustly. They were indeed unjust, wronging Him by that saying and by promising a lie.

"Yes," Abu Malik said. "This is the description of this nation of ours, and this is exactly what they say. Where is the salvation from this — where is the escape from the punishment of God?"

"By God — take refuge in Him and repent to Him; He accepts repentance from you. Ask Him for the way of His guardians, and He will save you by your works from the deeds of those who have done wrong. So it is with this creation: if they repent to God, He accepts their repentance. And what does God do with His punishment of them then?"

إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَظْلِمُ مِثْقَالَ ذَرَّةٍ
Q|4:40

"— even when they are unjust and lie; and He does not hasten them with punishment, even when they hasten to commit disobedience."

Abu Malik sought refuge from Allah's punishment by repenting and seeking means to His satisfaction, acknowledging Salih as a student and guide.

Salih promised that if he did so, he would find Allah pardoning and himself careful in guiding and watchful over him.

Salih's eyes welled with tears at the mention of repentance, and he broke off his speech. He encouraged them and ordered them to go to his father's house. He then consulted his father, the scholar who had first sent him out, about Abu Malik and his companions.

The scholar advised Salih to guide his companions with his guidance, test them by ignoring them without ignorance, and instruct his elders to be kind and righteous toward them. He affirmed that one who desires good is not hidden, and Allah will not abandon him, but will open the gates of guidance and show him the deeds of those who turn back to Him.

Thus, Abu Malik and his companions, each in their own way, underwent a transformation until orders came to them, and they recognized their guidance and the kindness shown. They thanked Allah, returned to their people as warners, and Allah guided whomever He willed of His servants to His religion.

**Etymology**

Here are some key Arabic terms from the chapter, with their linguistic and etymological roots:

**1. Salih (صالح)**
Root: ص ل ح (ṣ-l-ḥ)
Meaning: To be good, righteous, virtuous, to be in a state of soundness or integrity.
Derivatives:
صلاح (ṣalāḥ): Goodness, righteousness, integrity.
إصلاح (iṣlāḥ): Reform, rectification, improvement.
مصالحة (muṣālaḥah): Reconciliation, settlement.
The name صالح (Salih) directly reflects the meaning of being good and righteous, embodying the virtuous character of the individual in the story.

**2. Bakhtari (بختري)**
Root: ب خ ت ر (b-kh-t-r)
Meaning: To walk with pride, to strut, to be arrogant.
Derivatives:
بخت (bakht): Fortune, luck (often associated with pride in one's fortune).
The name بختري (Bakhtari) suggests a connection to pride or a stately demeanor, which aligns with the description of his family being "honored among their people, raised above others, granted a high standing."

**3. Abu Malik (أبو مالك)**
Root: م ل ك (m-l-k)
Meaning: To own, to possess, to rule, to control.
Derivatives:
ملك (malik): King, ruler.
مالك (mālik): Owner, possessor.
مملكة (mamlakah): Kingdom.
The name مالك (Malik) means "owner" or "possessor." Combined with أبو (Abu), meaning "father of," أبو مالك (Abu Malik) literally means "father of an owner/ruler." This name is fitting for a scholar known for his authority, pride, and abundance of rulings, suggesting a sense of mastery and control over knowledge.

**4. Sharia (شريعة)**
Root: ش ر ع (sh-r-ʿ)
Meaning: To legislate, to enact laws, to make clear, to open a path to water.
Derivatives:
شرع (sharaʿa): He legislated, he began.
شارع (shāriʿ): Street, path (a clear path).
تشريع (tashrīʿ): Legislation.
The term شريعة (Sharia) refers to the divine law, the clear path or way to Allah. Its root connection to "opening a path to water" signifies its role as a source of life and guidance, much like a well or spring in a desert.

**5. Sunnah (سنة)**
Root: س ن ن (s-n-n)
Meaning: To establish a way, to set a course, to sharpen.
Derivatives:
سن (sanna): He established, he set a precedent.
مسنون (masnūn): Established, customary.
The term سنة (Sunnah) refers to the established practice, custom, or tradition, particularly the way of life and teachings of the Prophet Muhammad. It represents a clear and established path to follow.

**6. Taqiyya (تقية)**
Root: و ق ي (w-q-y)
Meaning: To protect, to guard, to shield.
Derivatives:
وقاية (wiqāyah): Protection, prevention.
اتقى (ittaqā): He feared Allah, he guarded himself.
The term تقية (taqiyya) means dissimulation or precautionary dissimulation. It is derived from the root meaning "to protect" or "to guard oneself," indicating a practice of concealing one's beliefs or actions to protect oneself or one's community from harm, especially in times of persecution.

Etymology

The word "Allah" (الله) is derived from the root "ʾ-l-h" (أ ل ه), which means "to worship" or "to adore." It is the proper name for God in Islam, encompassing all divine attributes.

The term "Imam" (إمام) comes from the root "ʾ-m-m" (أ م م), meaning "to lead," "to precede," or "to be in front." An Imam is a leader, particularly in religious contexts, who guides the community.

"Messenger" (رسول) is derived from the root "r-s-l" (ر س ل), meaning "to send" or "to dispatch." A messenger is someone sent with a message, especially a divine message.

"Guardian" (ولي) comes from the root "w-l-y" (و ل ي), meaning "to be near," "to be a friend," "to protect," or "to be in charge." A guardian is a protector, a close friend, or someone entrusted with authority.

"Seeker" (سالك) is from the root "s-l-k" (س ل ك), meaning "to travel," "to enter," or "to follow a path." A seeker is someone who embarks on a spiritual journey or follows a particular path.

"Praise" (حمد) is from the root "h-m-d" (ح م د), meaning "to praise," "to commend," or "to laud." It signifies an expression of admiration and gratitude.

"Blessing" (صلاة) is from the root "s-l-w" (ص ل و), meaning "to pray," "to bless," or "to connect." It refers to prayers, blessings, and the connection with the divine.

"Defilement" (رجس) is from the root "r-j-s" (ر ج س), meaning "to be impure," "to be dirty," or "to be abominable." It signifies spiritual or physical impurity.

"Purifying" (تطهير) is from the root "t-h-r" (ط ه ر), meaning "to be pure," "to cleanse," or "to purify." It refers to the act of making something clean or pure.

"Power" (حول) is from the root "h-w-l" (ح و ل), meaning "to change," "to transform," or "to turn around." It signifies the ability to effect change or to move.

"Strength" (قوة) is from the root "q-w-y" (ق و ي), meaning "to be strong," "to be powerful," or "to be firm." It refers to physical or spiritual might and resilience.

"High" (علي) is from the root "ʿ-l-w" (ع ل و), meaning "to be high," "to be exalted," or "to ascend." It describes something elevated or supreme.

"Mighty" (عظيم) is from the root "ʿ-z-m" (ع ظ م), meaning "to be great," "to be vast," or "to be magnificent." It signifies greatness, majesty, and immense power.
