This is a manual of religious practice, laid out topic by topic the way a working handbook of law and worship is built. Its nine chapters move in the settled order such books follow: they open with the duty to seek knowledge, then turn to purity and the washing that readies the body, to the standing of formal prayer, to supplication in its many forms, to the rites owed the dead, to the alms due from wealth, to the fast of Ramadan, to the pilgrimage to the House, and finally to the wider business of the community — warfare, governance, and the doing of justice. Read straight through, it is a compact tour of the obligations a believer is expected to carry, from the most private act of cleansing to the ordering of a state.

The title carries a numeral, which places this volume as the first of a set rather than a whole standing alone; what follows here is its own opening, translated and printed directly below.

A word about who is speaking. This is a transmitted report: the text relays received teaching rather than voicing a single named author's opinions. When you meet a first person on the page, it belongs to the narrator carrying the material forward, not to an "I" who is confiding in you — read it as testimony being passed along a chain, not as memoir. Mistaking the one for the other will make the book sound more personal than it is.

Much of the working vocabulary stays in Arabic because the concepts have no clean English equivalent. The names of the obligations themselves — zakat, the pilgrimage, the fast, jihad — recur as fixed technical terms, each pointing to a defined act with its own conditions rather than to a loose English gloss. Treat these words as anchors: when one returns, it means the same precise thing it meant before, and the chapters are in large part an unfolding of what each one requires.
